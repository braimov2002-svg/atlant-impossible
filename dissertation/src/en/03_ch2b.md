## 2.6. Dose enhancement factor: definitions and calculation

The physical radio-enhancement effect of silver is quantified by the dose enhancement factor (DEF), the ratio of the absorbed dose in tissue containing the sensitizing element to the absorbed dose at the same point of the same tissue without it, under identical irradiation:

$$
\mathrm{DEF} = \frac{D_{t+\mathrm{Ag}}}{D_t}
$$ {#eq:def}

where $D_{t+\mathrm{Ag}}$ is the absorbed dose in the silver-loaded tissue and $D_t$ is the absorbed dose in the unloaded (reference) tissue. A value above unity means that silver increases the energy absorbed per unit mass, and a value below unity indicates a dose reduction, for example by shielding. This macroscopic DEF assumes a homogeneous distribution of silver and a dose averaged over volumes much larger than a cell; it characterises only the physical stage of radio-enhancement.

Below 50 keV the secondary electrons have ranges of at most a few tens of micrometres in soft tissue, so that charged-particle equilibrium (CPE) holds everywhere except in thin layers near interfaces [@attix1986; @andreo2017]. Since bremsstrahlung losses of these electrons are negligible in low-Z media, the absorbed dose under CPE equals the kerma [@attix1986; @icru85]:

$$
D = \int_0^{E_{\max}} \Psi(E)\,\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}(E)\,\mathrm{d}E
$$ {#eq:dose_cpe}

where $\Psi(E) = E\,\Phi(E)$ is the spectral energy fluence at the point of interest, $\Phi(E)$ is the spectral photon fluence, $\mu_{en}/\rho$ is the mass energy-absorption coefficient of the medium and $E_{\max}$ is the maximum photon energy (45 keV). The expression has the same form as the air kerma {{REF:eq:kerma}}. The coefficient $\mu_{en}/\rho$ excludes the energy carried away by fluorescence photons [@seltzer1993]. For silver, about 83 % of the K-shell vacancies relax by emission of K x-rays ($\omega_K = 0.831$, Ag Kα ≈ 22 keV), and in the kerma approximation this energy is assumed to leave the point of interaction; this assumption is tested by the Monte Carlo (MC) method of Section 2.7.

Silver-loaded tissue is treated as a homogeneous mixture. If $w$ is the mass fraction of silver, i.e. the mass of silver per unit mass of the loaded medium, the additivity (mixture) rule gives [@attix1986; @nist_xraymass]

$$
\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_{t+\mathrm{Ag}} = (1-w)\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_t + w\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_{\mathrm{Ag}}
$$ {#eq:mixture}

where the subscripts $t$ and Ag refer to tissue and silver. The same rule, written over the elemental mass fractions $w_i$ as $\mu/\rho = \sum_i w_i\,(\mu/\rho)_i$, gives the coefficients of soft tissue, water and air from the elemental NIST tables (Section 2.8). Mass fractions from 0.1 to 2 % (1–20 mg of silver per gram of loaded tissue) are considered.

Substituting {{REF:eq:dose_cpe}} and {{REF:eq:mixture}} into {{REF:eq:def}} gives the DEF at the surface of the medium:

$$
\mathrm{DEF} = \frac{\int \Psi(E)\left[(1-w)\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_t + w\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_{\mathrm{Ag}}\right]\mathrm{d}E}{\int \Psi(E)\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_t\mathrm{d}E}
$$ {#eq:def_spec}

where all coefficients are evaluated at the photon energy $E$. In this spectrum-weighted ratio the absolute normalisation of the fluence (tube output, current-time product, distance) cancels, so the DEF depends only on the spectral shape. Therefore the developed model (DM) can be used for DEF calculations on an equal footing with SpekPy, although its absolute output is less accurate (Section 3.1). In the code (function `def_factor` of `xspec.py`) the integrals are evaluated as sums over the 0.05 keV bins of the spectrum.

Because the numerator of {{REF:eq:def_spec}} is linear in $w$, it can be rearranged exactly into

$$
\mathrm{DEF} = 1 + w\left(\langle R\rangle - 1\right)
$$ {#eq:def_lin}

with

$$
\langle R\rangle = \frac{\int \Psi(E)\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_{\mathrm{Ag}}\mathrm{d}E}{\int \Psi(E)\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_t\mathrm{d}E} = \frac{\int R(E)\,d_t(E)\,\mathrm{d}E}{\int d_t(E)\,\mathrm{d}E}
$$ {#eq:ratio_avg}

where $R(E) = (\mu_{en}/\rho)_{\mathrm{Ag}}/(\mu_{en}/\rho)_t$ is the ratio of the mass energy-absorption coefficients and $d_t(E) = \Psi(E)\,(\mu_{en}/\rho)_t$ is the dose spectrum of unloaded tissue. Thus $\langle R\rangle$ is the mean of $R(E)$ weighted by the tissue dose spectrum, $\mathrm{DEF} - 1$ is strictly proportional to $w$, and the slope $\langle R\rangle - 1$ is a property of the spectrum alone. For ICRU-44 tissue, $R(E)$ grows from 21.0 at 8 keV to about 32 just below the Ag K-edge (25.514 keV), jumps to about 76 above it and reaches about 139 near 40–45 keV (Section 1.2). Any shift of the dose spectrum above the K-edge therefore increases the slope, which is the basis of the filter optimisation in Chapter 3.

Inside the medium the spectrum hardens with depth. For a point at depth $z$ where silver is present only locally (a thin layer or a small region that does not perturb the beam), the DEF for the primary beam is

$$
\mathrm{DEF}(z) = \frac{\int \Psi(E)\,e^{-\mu_t(E)\,z}\left[(1-w)\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_t + w\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_{\mathrm{Ag}}\right]\mathrm{d}E}{\int \Psi(E)\,e^{-\mu_t(E)\,z}\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_t\mathrm{d}E}
$$ {#eq:def_depth}

where $\Psi(E)$ is the energy fluence at the surface and $\mu_t(E) = \rho_t\,(\mu/\rho)_t(E)$ is the linear attenuation coefficient of tissue of density $\rho_t = 1.06$ g/cm³. Scattered photons are neglected, and the energy-independent inverse-square factor cancels. The denominator, normalised to its value at $z = 0$, gives the relative primary dose in unloaded tissue. As the attenuation coefficient of tissue decreases with energy, the DEF increases with depth.

When silver fills the whole region between the surface and the point of interest, it also attenuates the beam, especially just above the K-edge, where its mass attenuation coefficient increases 5.8-fold. For a slab loaded uniformly from the surface to a depth larger than $z$,

$$
\mathrm{DEF}_{\mathrm{slab}}(z) = \frac{\int \Psi(E)\,e^{-\mu_{t+\mathrm{Ag}}(E)\,z}\left[(1-w)\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_t + w\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_{\mathrm{Ag}}\right]\mathrm{d}E}{\int \Psi(E)\,e^{-\mu_t(E)\,z}\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_t\mathrm{d}E}
$$ {#eq:def_slab}

where $\mu_{t+\mathrm{Ag}}(E) = \rho_t\left[(1-w)(\mu/\rho)_t + w\,(\mu/\rho)_{\mathrm{Ag}}\right]$ is the linear attenuation coefficient of the loaded medium (its density is approximated by $\rho_t$), and the denominator refers to the same depth in silver-free tissue. The additional attenuation removes preferentially the most effective photons above the K-edge, so $\mathrm{DEF}_{\mathrm{slab}}$ decreases with depth (self-shielding) and is no longer linear in $w$. At $w = 1$ % the attenuation added by silver just above its K-edge is comparable to that of tissue itself, and behind a loaded region the dose falls below its silver-free value.

The reference medium is ICRU-44 soft tissue (H 10.2, C 14.3, N 3.4, O 70.8, Na 0.2, P 0.3, S 0.3, Cl 0.2 and K 0.3 % by mass; 1.06 g/cm³) [@icru44]; water and the ICRU four-component tissue are used as alternatives. This definitional choice affects the DEF and must be reported with it; its influence is quantified in Section 3.10. Finally, the macroscopic DEF does not resolve the subcellular scale. Around nanoparticles the dose enhancement is highly localised, because short-range photo- and Auger electrons deposit their energy close to the particle [@jones2010]. Such nanoscale deposition can cause biological effects beyond those expected from the macroscopic dose enhancement (local effect model) [@mcmahon2011], and the outcome depends on photon energy, particle size, concentration and location [@lechtman2011]. The DEF calculated here is thus the physical baseline for identifying chemical, biological and nanoscale contributions.

## 2.7. Monte Carlo method for verification

The analytical model assumes that the Ag K-fluorescence energy escapes from the point of interaction and that only exponentially attenuated primary photons contribute. To test these assumptions, an independent MC code (`mc_dose.py`) was written. It is much simpler than the general-purpose codes PENELOPE, EGSnrc and Geant4 [@salvat2019; @kawrakow2000; @agostinelli2003], which remain the reference tools for coupled electron–photon transport, but it includes all photon interactions relevant below 50 keV and the transport of the silver fluorescence.

The geometry is a laterally infinite slab of ICRU-44 tissue, 30 mm thick, irradiated by a broad parallel beam at normal incidence. A layer at depths of 2–12 mm contains silver with $w = 1$ %, modelled by adding silver of partial density $\rho_{\mathrm{Ag}} = \rho_t\,w/(1-w)$ to the tissue. A photon is described by its energy $E$, depth $z$ and direction cosine $u$; photons leaving the slab are terminated, and energy is scored in 0.25 mm bins. The incident spectra are the SpekPy spectra of the 45 kV tube (θ = 12°, 0.5 mm Be, 10 cm air) without filter and with 1 mm Al, sampled by inversion of the cumulative distribution with a uniform position inside each 0.05 keV bin.

Free paths are sampled by Woodcock (delta) tracking [@woodcock1965; @kalos2008] with the majorant $\mu_{\max}(E) = \mu_t(E) + \mu_{\mathrm{Ag}}(E)$, the total linear attenuation coefficient of the loaded layer, where $\mu_{\mathrm{Ag}}$ is the product of $\rho_{\mathrm{Ag}}$ and the mass attenuation coefficient of silver. The path length is

$$
s = -\frac{\ln \xi_1}{\mu_{\max}(E)}
$$ {#eq:mc_path}

where $\xi_1$ is a random number uniform between 0 and 1, and the photon moves to $z + u\,s$. There the collision is real with the probability

$$
P_{\mathrm{real}} = \frac{\mu(z,E)}{\mu_{\max}(E)}
$$ {#eq:mc_real}

where $\mu(z,E)$ is the actual coefficient ($\mu_t$ outside the layer, $\mu_t + \mu_{\mathrm{Ag}}$ inside); otherwise it is virtual and the photon continues unchanged. No distances to boundaries are needed.

A real interaction is chosen in proportion to the partial coefficients of photoabsorption in tissue, photoabsorption in silver, incoherent and coherent scattering, taken from xraylib [@schoonjans2011] and tabulated at 1–46 keV with a 5 eV step. Photoabsorption in tissue deposits the whole photon energy locally. After photoabsorption in silver above the K-edge, the K shell is selected with the probability $\tau_K/\tau$ (ratio of the K-shell to the total photoelectric cross-section), and the vacancy emits, with the probability $\omega_K$, a K x-ray chosen among the six strongest K lines (KL2, KL3, KM2, KM3, KN2, KN3) according to their radiative rates. This photon is emitted isotropically and transported, and the remaining energy is deposited locally; otherwise the whole energy is deposited.

The polar scattering angle $\vartheta$ is sampled from cumulative tables of the xraylib differential cross-sections, i.e. the Klein–Nishina cross-section multiplied by the incoherent scattering function and the Thomson cross-section multiplied by the squared atomic form factor [@hubbell1975; @schoonjans2011]. The tables (91 energies from 1 to 46 keV, 1441 angles) are computed for tissue and used for all events, since silver contributes only a few per cent of the scattering cross-section of the layer. After incoherent scattering

$$
E' = \frac{E}{1 + \frac{E}{m_e c^2}\left(1 - \cos\vartheta\right)}
$$ {#eq:compton}

where $E$ and $E'$ are the photon energies before and after scattering and $m_e c^2 = 510.999$ keV; the energy $E - E'$ is deposited locally. The direction cosine becomes $u' = u\cos\vartheta + \sqrt{1-u^2}\,\sin\vartheta\cos\varphi$ with $\varphi$ uniform in $[0, 2\pi)$. Photons below 1 keV are absorbed locally.

Secondary electrons are absorbed where they are created, since their ranges of a few tens of micrometres are much smaller than the bins. The dose in bin $j$ per unit incident fluence is

$$
D_j = \frac{1}{N}\,\frac{\sum_{i \in j} \varepsilon_i}{\rho_j\,\Delta z}
$$ {#eq:mc_dose}

where $\varepsilon_i$ are the energy deposits in the bin, $N$ is the number of incident photons, $\rho_j$ is the density ($\rho_t$ or $\rho_t + \rho_{\mathrm{Ag}}$) and $\Delta z = 0.25$ mm. The MC DEF is the ratio of the doses for $w = 1$ % and $w = 0$; each run comprises $4\times10^{6}$ histories in five independent batches, whose spread gives the standard error. The analytical DEF for this geometry follows from {{REF:eq:def_slab}} generalised to a buried layer.

Two checks preceded the runs. For a broad 30 keV beam the ratio of the surface dose to the primary kerma is 1.31, reflecting backscatter, which is significant for broad kilovoltage beams [@grosswendt1990]. The energy-transfer coefficient reconstructed from the xraylib photoabsorption and Compton data agrees with the NIST $\mu_{en}/\rho$ within 0.3 % for O, C and H at 20–40 keV, confirming the consistency of the MC data with the NIST tables. The results are compared in Section 3.9.

## 2.8. Data sources, software implementation and validation strategy

The coefficients $\mu/\rho$ and $\mu_{en}/\rho$ of tissue, water, air and the high-Z elements are taken from the NIST tables of Hubbell and Seltzer [@nist_xraymass], based on the $\mu_{en}/\rho$ calculations of Seltzer [@seltzer1993]. The module `nist.py` reads the locally stored tables and interpolates $\ln(\mu/\rho)$ linearly in $\ln E$ separately within each segment between absorption edges, as recommended by NIST; the edges are recognised as repeated energies, and an energy exactly at an edge receives the below-edge value. Compounds are treated by the mixture rule with the ICRU-44 tissue composition, the stoichiometric composition of water and the NIST composition of dry air. The elemental $\mu/\rho$ of the anode, window and filter materials, edge and line energies, fluorescence yields, radiative rates and the cross-sections of the MC code are taken from xraylib 4.3.0 [@schoonjans2011], whose $\mu/\rho$ agree with the NIST data [@nist_xraymass; @nist_xcom] to better than 0.1 %.

The verification revealed an error in the xraylib 4.3.0 function `CS_Energy`, which returns $\mu_{en}/\rho$. For light elements at 15–45 keV its values are too low: for oxygen at 30 keV it gives 0.108 cm²/g instead of the NIST value 0.173 cm²/g (−37 %), and for carbon at the same energy 0.0515 instead of 0.0661 cm²/g, while for silver it agrees with NIST (16.58 versus 16.60 cm²/g). Since tissue consists mainly of O, C and H, this function would underestimate the denominator of {{REF:eq:def_spec}} and strongly overestimate the DEF: 1.72 instead of 1.48 for 1 % Ag with 1 mm Al. The underlying xraylib cross-sections are correct, as the reconstructed energy-transfer coefficient shows (Section 2.7). All $\mu_{en}/\rho$ values are therefore taken directly from NIST, and `CS_Energy` should be checked against NIST before use for low-Z media.

SpekPy v2.5.4 [@poludniowski2021; @spekpy_soft] with the "casim" physics model, which supports copper anodes at 10–50 kV and has been validated against measured spectra and half-value layers (HVL) [@bujila2020], is the reference spectrum model. Its spectra serve to validate the DM and as the principal input of the DEF calculations, which are repeated with the DM spectra. The software is written in Python 3.13 with NumPy [@harris2020], SciPy [@virtanen2020] and Matplotlib [@hunter2007]; SciPy provides the bounded minimisation for the model parameter and Brent's root-finding for the effective energy and the equal-transmission filter thicknesses. The scripts `run_all.py`, `run_sweeps.py` and `mc_dose.py` produce the main results, the filter sweeps and the MC verification.

The single adjustable parameter of the DM, the electron depth factor $\eta$ {{REF:eq:depth}}, minimises the root-mean-square deviation between the DM and SpekPy bremsstrahlung spectra (characteristic lines excluded) at 45 kV, θ = 12° and 0.5 mm Be. Both spectra are normalised to unit area above 3 keV, the deviation is taken relative to the maximum of the reference, and $\eta$ is searched in 0.25–0.7 and rounded to two decimals. All other configurations are predictions without refitting. Validation includes: (i) model components, e.g. the Bethe stopping power of water at 50 keV, 6.603 MeV cm²/g, identical to NIST ESTAR [@nist_estar]; (ii) spectra and beam-quality parameters versus SpekPy for 25–50 kV and anode angles of 6–30°, with and without 1 mm Al, plus a tungsten-anode check at 50, 80 and 100 kV; (iii) DEF from DM and SpekPy spectra; (iv) MC verification; and (v) a sensitivity analysis of the DEF.

## 2.9. Radiation safety when working with a kilovoltage x-ray tube

A 45 kV copper-anode tube is a source of very intense low-energy x-rays. According to Chapter 3, the unfiltered air kerma at 10 cm from the focal spot is 383 mGy per mAs, i.e. 15.3 Gy/s at 40 mA, and 2 Gy at the tissue surface is delivered in 0.12 s. An accidental exposure of the hands to the primary beam can therefore cause severe local radiation injuries within seconds. The primary beam, the radiation scattered by the sample and its holder, and the leakage radiation from the housing must all be controlled.

Radiation protection rests on the principles of justification, optimisation, which keeps exposures as low as reasonably achievable (ALARA), and dose limitation, formulated by the International Commission on Radiological Protection [@icrp103] and implemented in the IAEA Basic Safety Standards [@iaea_gsr3]. In the Republic of Uzbekistan the legal basis of radiation safety is the Law "On Radiation Safety" [@uz_law_radsafety]. Work with an x-ray tube is a planned exposure situation, in which occupational exposure must remain below the limits on the effective dose and on the equivalent doses to the skin, extremities and lens of the eye [@icrp103; @iaea_gsr3].

The main measures are technical. The shielded tube housing lets radiation out only through the window and shutter, and the sample is irradiated in a closed enclosure whose door interlocks switch off the high voltage or close the shutter. Warning lights show that the high voltage is on and the shutter open, and fixed holders and collimators keep the hands out of the beam. The high-voltage generator is an additional electrical hazard, and the fragile beryllium window must not be touched, since beryllium compounds are toxic. Staff training, local rules, personal dosimetry and dose-rate surveys around the enclosure, particularly after maintenance, complete these measures.

Added filtration also serves safety. The filters that markedly raise the DEF (from 1 mm Al to 0.2 mm Cu) lower the dose rate 120–1300-fold; with 1 mm Al the tissue kerma rate at 40 mA falls from 16.5 to 0.137 Gy/s, and the time for 2 Gy rises to 14.6 s, which timers and shutters control with small relative error. The filter removes the soft component, which is absorbed superficially without reaching depth: the relative primary dose at 10 mm is 0.009 of the surface value without a filter and 0.44 with 1 mm Al. Filtration thus reduces the unnecessary skin or surface dose and yields a beam specified by its HVL, as required by kilovoltage dosimetry protocols [@ma2001]. The filter that maximises the DEF is therefore also an optimisation measure in the sense of ALARA.

## 2.10. Conclusions to Chapter 2

1. A semi-analytical thick-target model (DM) of the spectrum of a reflection x-ray tube with a copper anode has been formulated. It includes electron slowing-down with the Bethe stopping power, bremsstrahlung with the non-relativistic Bethe–Heitler cross-section and the Elwert factor, direct and bremsstrahlung-induced K radiation, self-absorption in the anode and attenuation in the window, air and filters, with a single adjustable parameter, the electron depth factor $\eta$.
2. The beam-quality parameters (air kerma, first and second HVL, homogeneity coefficient, mean and effective energies) are calculated identically for the DM and SpekPy spectra.
3. Under CPE below 50 keV, the DEF of silver-loaded tissue is a spectrum-weighted ratio of mass energy-absorption coefficients; $\mathrm{DEF} - 1$ is exactly proportional to the silver mass fraction, with a slope set by the part of the dose spectrum above the Ag K-edge. Its depth and loaded-slab forms describe beam hardening and self-shielding.
4. An independent MC code with Woodcock tracking, Compton and Rayleigh scattering and transport of Ag K-fluorescence verifies the analytical assumptions.
5. All $\mu_{en}/\rho$ values are taken from NIST, because the xraylib 4.3.0 function `CS_Energy` underestimates them for light elements by up to 37 % and would overestimate the DEF (1.72 instead of 1.48 for 1 % Ag with 1 mm Al).
6. The unfiltered tube output (15.3 Gy/s at 10 cm and 40 mA) requires shielding, interlocks and dosimetric control; filtration reduces both the dose rate and the superficial dose.
