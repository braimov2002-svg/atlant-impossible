### 3.2.7. Validation of the developed spectrum model

With no measured spectrum of this tube available, the DM was validated against SpekPy v2, a deterministic physics-based code that supports copper anodes at 10–50 kV [@poludniowski2021] and has been validated against measured spectra and half-value layers (HVL) [@bujila2020; @omar2020b]. The only adjustable parameter of the DM, the electron depth factor $\eta$ of {{REF:eq:depth}}, was fitted to the bremsstrahlung continuum alone (lines switched off in both codes; 45 kV, θ = 12°, 0.5 mm Be, no air) by minimising the root-mean-square deviation of the area-normalised spectra,

$$
\delta(\eta) = \frac{1}{\max_i b_i}\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(a_i(\eta) - b_i\right)^2\right]^{1/2}
$$ {#eq:rms}

where $a_i(\eta)$ and $b_i$ are the bremsstrahlung fluences of the DM and SpekPy in energy bin $i$, each divided by its sum over the $N$ bins with $E_i > 3$ keV, and $1/\max_i b_i$ expresses $\delta$ as a fraction of the spectrum maximum. The fit gave $\eta = 0.40$ with $\delta = 2.46$ %, against 8.47 % for straight-line penetration ($\eta = 1$). Because of multiple elastic scattering, photons are thus produced at less than half the depth of a straight path in the continuous slowing-down approximation (CSDA), 2.536 µm instead of 6.339 µm (CSDA range 6.481 µm × cos 12°) for 45 keV electrons in copper, and the soft bremsstrahlung is less self-absorbed in the anode; $\eta = 1$ therefore yields too hard a spectrum. With $\eta$ fixed at 0.40, all other configurations are predictions rather than fits.

{{REF:t3_1}} compares the two codes for 25–50 kV and 6–30° without the air path. With 1 mm Al, which removes the Cu K lines, the first HVL ({{REF:eq:hvl}}) of the DM is 3.5–4.6 % lower than that of SpekPy. Without added filtration the deviation ranges from −2.6 % to −19.4 %, because the DM underestimates the Cu K lines by about 26–43 % and a weaker 8 keV line gives more weight to the softer 3–8 keV continuum in the air kerma. The deficit is largest at low voltage, where the Gryziński cross-section [@gryzinski1965] rises too slowly above the threshold compared with the distorted-wave Born calculations [@bote2008; @llovet2014] used by SpekPy [@omar2018].

{{TABLE:t3_1}}

The spectral shapes agree within about 10 % between 10 and 30 keV ({{REF:fig05_validation_cu}}). The DM continuum is slightly richer at 10–20 keV and poorer above about 25 keV, the ratio falling to 0.80–0.85 at 40 keV; this mild softening explains the HVL deficit of the filtered beams. Its probable causes are the non-relativistic Born cross-section with the Elwert factor [@koch1959], which only approximates the tabulated cross-sections [@seltzer1986], and the assumed isotropic emission, whereas SpekPy models the angular distribution of bremsstrahlung [@omar2020a].

The absolute air kerma of the DM exceeds that of SpekPy by a factor of 1.08–1.22 without filtration and 1.21–1.42 with 1 mm Al, mainly because the DM neglects electron backscattering (the backscatter coefficient of copper is about 0.3). The DEF depends only on the spectral shape and is unaffected; absolute dose rates are taken from SpekPy. As a check beyond copper, the same $\eta$ was applied to a tungsten anode at 50, 80 and 100 kV with 2.5 mm Al, which removes the W L lines not included in the DM ({{REF:t3_1w}}). The HVL deviations lie between −5.7 % and +0.5 %, and at 50 kV both values are comparable to the first HVL (1.78 mm Al) of the quality RQR3 of the International Electrotechnical Commission standard IEC 61267 [@iec61267]. As for copper, the DM spectra have a slightly weaker high-energy tail ({{REF:fig06_validation_w}}). A single $\eta$ thus serves anodes with Z = 29 and Z = 74. The main weakness of the DM, the underestimated Cu K-line intensity, matters for unfiltered and Kα-dominated beams, so the main DEF results use SpekPy spectra, with the DM as an independent check.

### 3.2.8. Spectrum of the 45 kV copper-anode tube

In the baseline spectrum ({{REF:fig07_baseline}}) the Be window and air remove practically all photons below about 3 keV. The continuum rises to a plateau at about 7–9 keV and drops abruptly at the Cu K-edge (8.98 keV), above which the anode absorbs much more strongly and acts as its own K-edge filter. It then recovers to a shallow maximum near 12–13 keV and decreases to zero at 45 keV, so the Ag K-edge falls into its weak high-energy part. In 0.05 keV bins the Cu Kα line (8.05 keV) exceeds the neighbouring continuum by more than two orders of magnitude and the Kβ line (8.91 keV) by a factor of 45–80.

{{FIG:fig07_baseline|Spectrum of the 45 kV Cu-anode tube (θ = 12°, 0.5 mm Be, 10 cm air) from SpekPy v2 and the developed model. The dashed line marks the Ag K-edge (25.514 keV).}}

According to SpekPy, characteristic photons make up 68.5 % of the photon fluence ({{REF:t3_2}}), so the unfiltered beam is essentially a source of Cu K radiation. The Kβ/Kα fluence ratio of SpekPy, 0.1431, slightly exceeds the ratio of the radiative rates of xraylib (0.134) [@schoonjans2011], because the more energetic Kβ photons are attenuated less on their way out. A 20 µm Ni filter, whose K-edge (8.333 keV, {{REF:t2_2}}) lies between the Kα and Kβ energies, reduces this ratio to 0.0023, acting as the classical β-filter of x-ray diffraction [@cullity2001].

The beam quality is very low: the mean energy ({{REF:eq:emean}}) is 10.38 keV, the first HVL below 50 µm Al and the effective energy below that of Kα, so the dose to soft tissue comes mainly from the Cu K lines and the soft continuum. Only 4.64 % of the photons, carrying 14.1 % of the energy fluence, lie above the Ag K-edge, and their share in the kerma is smaller still because $\mu_{en}/\rho$ decreases steeply with energy. The output is very high: 383 mGy/mAs at 10 cm, i.e. 15.3 Gy/s at 40 mA.

### 3.2.9. Influence of tube design parameters

The anode angle, tube voltage and Be window thickness were varied one at a time around the baseline ({{REF:t3_3}}). Increasing the anode angle from 6° to 30° raises the air kerma by a factor of 1.56 through weaker self-filtration in the anode: the exit path $d/\sin\theta$ of a photon produced at depth $d$ is about 4.8 times longer at 6° than at 30°. The effect is strongest where copper is most opaque, at the soft end and just above the Cu K-edge, whereas above about 25 keV the curves nearly merge ({{REF:fig08_angle}}). The same mechanism causes the heel effect [@bushberg2012], not treated here since the calculations refer to the central ray. A small anode angle thus gives a smaller effective focal spot but a lower output and a slightly harder beam.

{{FIG:fig08_angle|Influence of the anode angle (6–30°) on the spectrum of the 45 kV Cu-anode tube (0.5 mm Be, 10 cm air; SpekPy v2). The dashed line marks the Ag K-edge.}}

The tube voltage has the strongest influence on the output, which grows largely through the Cu K lines, since the electrons travel a longer path at energies where the K-shell ionisation cross-section is near its maximum (at about 35 keV). The continuum grows strongly at high energies ({{REF:fig09_kvp}}) but hardly changes just above the Cu K-edge, because such photons escape only from a thin surface layer of the anode. The DEF increases only from 1.1932 at 25 kV, where no photon reaches the Ag K-edge, to 1.2027 at 50 kV, so the enhancement of the unfiltered beam is caused almost entirely by photoabsorption in the L and M shells of Ag, where $R(E)$ does not exceed 32 (21–32 at 8–25 keV).

{{FIG:fig09_kvp|Spectra of the Cu-anode tube at tube voltages of 25–50 kV (baseline geometry; SpekPy v2). The dashed line marks the Ag K-edge.}}

The Be window acts mainly on the soft end of the spectrum ({{REF:fig10_be}}). Because beryllium (Z = 4) absorbs weakly, increasing the window thickness from 0.15 to 2.0 mm removes about three quarters of the photons below 6 keV but also about 30 % of the Cu K-line photons, which account for most of the decrease in air kerma from 423 to 271 mGy/mAs. The thickness, fixed by the tube design, must be known accurately, since it changes the output by more than one third.

{{FIG:fig10_be|Influence of the Be window thickness (0.15–2.0 mm) on the low-energy part of the spectrum of the 45 kV Cu-anode tube (θ = 12°, 10 cm air; SpekPy v2).}}

The DM spectra give DEF values within 0.004 of SpekPy for all design variants. While the design parameters change the output by factors of up to 1.6 (anode angle, window) and 2.6 (voltage), the DEF for 1 % Ag remains between 1.19 and 1.21, because in every unfiltered beam the tissue dose is dominated by the Cu K lines and photons of 3–15 keV. The tube design is therefore a lever for the dose rate rather than the DEF; a substantial increase in the DEF requires added filters.

### 3.2.10. Influence of added filters on the spectrum

The added filters, described by the Beer–Lambert transmission of {{REF:eq:filter}}, form three groups ({{REF:t3_4}}): aluminium, with its K-edge (1.56 keV) below the spectrum; nickel and copper, with K-edges near the Cu K lines; and Mo, Ag and Sn, with K-edges at 20.0, 25.514 and 29.2 keV ({{REF:t2_2}}). Aluminium acts as a classical hardening filter ({{REF:fig11_al}}): its photoelectric cross-section falls roughly as $E^{-3}$, so the soft continuum and the Cu K lines are removed far more efficiently than the photons above the Ag K-edge. With 1–2 mm Al the Kα line practically disappears. The HVL jumps from 0.104 to 0.454 mm Al between the 0.25 and 0.5 mm filters, because behind the thinner filter the residual Cu K lines still carry a large part of the air kerma. The price is the output: 1 mm Al reduces the dose rate about 120-fold and 2 mm Al about 270-fold.

{{FIG:fig11_al|Spectra of the 45 kV Cu-anode tube with added Al filters of 0.25–2.0 mm (baseline geometry; SpekPy v2). The dashed line marks the Ag K-edge.}}

Copper filters behave differently ({{REF:fig12_cu_ni}}): with the K-edge (8.979 keV) just above the Kα and Kβ energies, copper is relatively transparent to its own K radiation but absorbs the 9–20 keV continuum very strongly. With 0.1 mm Cu the mean energy is close to that with 0.5 mm Al, yet the HVL is 6.5 times smaller: the mean energy is weighted by fluence and reflects the hard continuum, whereas the HVL is weighted by air kerma and controlled by the transmitted Kα line, for which $\mu_{en}/\rho$ of air is much larger. Only 0.2 mm Cu suppresses the lines sufficiently, at a relative air kerma of $7.64 \times 10^{-4}$. The 0.02 mm Ni filter, which removes Kβ and the continuum just above 8.33 keV, leaves a quasi-monochromatic 8 keV beam that suits x-ray diffraction but not dose enhancement by Ag.

{{FIG:fig12_cu_ni|Spectra of the 45 kV Cu-anode tube with a 0.02 mm Ni filter and with Cu filters of 0.05–0.2 mm (baseline geometry; SpekPy v2). The dashed line marks the Ag K-edge.}}

Each K-edge filter transmits a band below its edge and cuts the fluence above it several-fold or more ({{REF:fig13_kedge}}). The 0.05 mm Ag filter thus removes precisely the photons above 25.5 keV that are most useful for enhancement by Ag: although its HVL exceeds that of 0.5 mm Al, only 20.1 % of its photons lie above the Ag K-edge, against 38.2 %. Tin preserves the 25.5–29.2 keV window but removes the photons above 29.2 keV, where $R(E)$ is highest, so 0.1 mm Sn gives a slightly larger HVL than 1 mm Al but fewer photons above the Ag K-edge (40.4 % against 49.8 %). Neither the mean energy nor the HVL alone therefore characterises the spectral region that matters for Ag. The DEF follows the fraction of photons above the Ag K-edge only while the kerma is not dominated by the Cu K lines: with 55.1 % of its photons above the edge, 0.1 mm Cu gives a DEF of only 1.288, because the transmitted Kα line delivers most of the tissue dose.

## 3.3. Dose enhancement factor for biological tissue with Ag

### 3.3.1. Definitions and calculation of the dose enhancement factor

The physical radio-enhancement is quantified by the DEF, the ratio of absorbed doses at a point with and without silver under identical irradiation:

$$
\mathrm{DEF} = \frac{D_{t+\mathrm{Ag}}}{D_t}
$$ {#eq:def}

where $D_{t+\mathrm{Ag}}$ and $D_t$ are the absorbed doses in silver-loaded and unloaded (reference) tissue. This macroscopic DEF assumes a homogeneous silver distribution and doses averaged over volumes much larger than cells and electron ranges.

Below 50 keV secondary electrons have sub-millimetre ranges in tissue, so charged-particle equilibrium (CPE) holds except near interfaces [@attix1986; @andreo2017] and the dose equals the collision kerma {{REF:eq:dose_cpe}}, practically the kerma in low-Z media [@icru85]. By analogy with {{REF:eq:kerma}},

$$
D = \int_{0}^{T_0} \Psi(E)\,\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}(E)\,\mathrm{d}E
$$ {#eq:dose_spec}

where $\Psi(E) = E\,\Phi(E)$ is the spectral energy fluence, $\Phi(E)$ the spectral photon fluence {{REF:eq:fluence}}, $\mu_{en}/\rho$ the mass energy-absorption coefficient of the medium and $T_0$ = 45 keV the maximum photon energy. Since $\mu_{en}/\rho$ excludes the energy of scattered and fluorescence photons [@seltzer1993], the Ag K x-rays ($\omega_K = 0.831$) are assumed to escape. For a silver mass fraction $w$ the mixture rule gives [@nist_xraymass]

$$
\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_{t+\mathrm{Ag}} = (1-w)\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_t + w\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_{\mathrm{Ag}}
$$ {#eq:mixture}

where $t$ and Ag denote tissue and silver; mass fractions of 0.1–2 % are considered. Substituting {{REF:eq:dose_spec}} and {{REF:eq:mixture}} into {{REF:eq:def}} gives the surface DEF:

$$
\mathrm{DEF} = \frac{\int \Psi(E)\left[(1-w)\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_t + w\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_{\mathrm{Ag}}\right]\mathrm{d}E}{\int \Psi(E)\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_t\mathrm{d}E}
$$ {#eq:def_spec}

The fluence normalisation cancels, so the DEF depends only on the spectral shape, and DM spectra are usable despite their less accurate absolute output (Section 3.2.7). As the numerator is linear in $w$, {{REF:eq:def_spec}} becomes exactly

$$
\mathrm{DEF} = 1 + w\left(\langle R\rangle - 1\right)
$$ {#eq:def_lin}

with

$$
\langle R\rangle = \frac{\int \Psi(E)\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_{\mathrm{Ag}}\mathrm{d}E}{\int \Psi(E)\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_t\mathrm{d}E} = \frac{\int R(E)\,d_t(E)\,\mathrm{d}E}{\int d_t(E)\,\mathrm{d}E}
$$ {#eq:ratio_avg}

where $R(E) = (\mu_{en}/\rho)_{\mathrm{Ag}}/(\mu_{en}/\rho)_t$ and $d_t(E) = \Psi(E)\,(\mu_{en}/\rho)_t$ is the dose spectrum of unloaded tissue, so $\langle R\rangle$, the dose-weighted mean of $R(E)$, depends on the spectrum alone. For ICRU-44 tissue, $R(E)$ jumps at the Ag K-edge (25.514 keV) from about 32 to about 76 and reaches about 139 at 40–45 keV. Shifting the dose spectrum above the K-edge therefore raises $\langle R\rangle$ and the slope, the basis of the filter optimisation in Section 3.3.5.

Where silver is present only locally at depth $z$, without perturbing the beam, the primary-beam DEF is

$$
\mathrm{DEF}(z) = \frac{\int \Psi(E)\,e^{-\mu_t(E)\,z}\left[(1-w)\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_t + w\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_{\mathrm{Ag}}\right]\mathrm{d}E}{\int \Psi(E)\,e^{-\mu_t(E)\,z}\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_t\mathrm{d}E}
$$ {#eq:def_depth}

where $\Psi(E)$ is the surface energy fluence and $\mu_t(E) = \rho_t\,(\mu/\rho)_t(E)$ the linear attenuation coefficient of tissue of density $\rho_t$ = 1.06 g/cm³; scatter and inverse-square fall-off are neglected, and the denominator normalised to $z = 0$ is the relative primary dose in unloaded tissue. Tissue removes first the low-energy photons, for which $R(E)$ is small, so the DEF increases with depth. Silver filling the region above the point also attenuates the beam, most strongly just above the K-edge, where its $\mu/\rho$ increases 5.8-fold. For a slab loaded from the surface to beyond $z$,

$$
\mathrm{DEF}_{\mathrm{slab}}(z) = \frac{\int \Psi(E)\,e^{-\mu_{t+\mathrm{Ag}}(E)\,z}\left[(1-w)\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_t + w\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_{\mathrm{Ag}}\right]\mathrm{d}E}{\int \Psi(E)\,e^{-\mu_t(E)\,z}\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_t\mathrm{d}E}
$$ {#eq:def_slab}

where $\mu_{t+\mathrm{Ag}}(E) = \rho_t\left[(1-w)(\mu/\rho)_t + w\,(\mu/\rho)_{\mathrm{Ag}}\right]$ is the linear attenuation coefficient of the loaded medium (density approximated by $\rho_t$). The added attenuation preferentially removes the most effective photons, so $\mathrm{DEF}_{\mathrm{slab}}$ decreases with depth (self-shielding) and is no longer linear in $w$. At $w$ = 1 % silver attenuates just above its K-edge about as much as the tissue itself, and behind a loaded region the dose falls below its silver-free value.

The reference medium is ICRU-44 soft tissue (H 10.2, C 14.3, N 3.4, O 70.8, Na 0.2, P 0.3, S 0.3, Cl 0.2 and K 0.3 % by mass; 1.06 g/cm³) [@icru44], with water and the ICRU four-component tissue as alternatives; the choice affects the DEF (Section 3.3.9) and must be reported. On the subcellular scale, short-range photo- and Auger electrons deposit much of their energy close to nanoparticles [@jones2010], which can produce biological effects beyond the macroscopic enhancement (local effect model) [@mcmahon2011] depending on photon energy and particle size, concentration and location [@lechtman2011]. The calculated DEF is thus the physical baseline for identifying chemical, biological and nanoscale contributions.

### 3.3.2. Monte Carlo method for verification

The escape of Ag K-fluorescence and the neglect of scatter in the analytical model are tested with an independent MC code (`mc_dose.py`), simpler than PENELOPE, EGSnrc or Geant4 [@salvat2019; @kawrakow2000; @agostinelli2003] but including all photon interactions relevant below 50 keV. A laterally infinite 30 mm slab of ICRU-44 tissue is irradiated by a broad parallel beam at normal incidence; a layer at 2–12 mm contains silver with $w$ = 1 %, added as a partial density $\rho_{\mathrm{Ag}} = \rho_t\,w/(1-w)$. Photons are tracked by energy $E$, depth $z$ and direction cosine $u$. The SpekPy spectra of the 45 kV tube (θ = 12°, 0.5 mm Be, 10 cm air), unfiltered and with 1 mm Al, are sampled by inverting the cumulative distribution. Free paths are sampled by Woodcock (delta) tracking [@woodcock1965; @kalos2008] with the majorant $\mu_{\max}(E) = \mu_t(E) + \mu_{\mathrm{Ag}}(E)$, where $\mu_{\mathrm{Ag}} = \rho_{\mathrm{Ag}}(\mu/\rho)_{\mathrm{Ag}}$:

$$
s = -\frac{\ln \xi_1}{\mu_{\max}(E)}
$$ {#eq:mc_path}

where $s$ is the path length and $\xi_1$ a random number uniform in (0, 1); at the new depth $z + u\,s$ the collision is real with probability

$$
P_{\mathrm{real}} = \frac{\mu(z,E)}{\mu_{\max}(E)}
$$ {#eq:mc_real}

where $\mu(z,E)$ is the local coefficient ($\mu_t$ outside the layer, $\mu_t + \mu_{\mathrm{Ag}}$ inside it); otherwise it is virtual, so boundary distances are never computed.

The interaction type (photoabsorption in tissue or silver, incoherent or coherent scattering) is sampled from partial coefficients tabulated from xraylib [@schoonjans2011] at 1–46 keV in 5 eV steps. After photoabsorption in silver, a K vacancy (probability $\tau_K/\tau$, the K-shell share of the photoelectric cross-section) emits with probability $\omega_K$ one of the six principal K lines, chosen by radiative rates and transported isotropically; all other absorbed energy is deposited locally. The scattering angle $\vartheta$ is sampled from tissue tables of the Klein–Nishina cross-section times the incoherent scattering function and of the Thomson cross-section times the squared atomic form factor [@hubbell1975]; silver contributes only a few per cent of scattering. After incoherent scattering

$$
E' = \frac{E}{1 + \frac{E}{m_e c^2}\left(1 - \cos\vartheta\right)}
$$ {#eq:compton}

where $E$ and $E'$ are the photon energies before and after scattering and $m_e c^2$ = 510.999 keV is the electron rest energy; the direction cosine becomes $u' = u\cos\vartheta + \sqrt{1-u^2}\,\sin\vartheta\cos\varphi$ with $\varphi$ uniform in $[0, 2\pi)$. Photons below 1 keV and secondary electrons (ranges of a few tens of micrometres) deposit locally, giving the dose in bin $j$ per unit incident fluence

$$
D_j = \frac{1}{N}\,\frac{\sum_{i \in j} \varepsilon_i}{\rho_j\,\Delta z}
$$ {#eq:mc_dose}

where $\varepsilon_i$ are the energy deposits in the bin, $N$ the number of incident photons, $\rho_j$ the density ($\rho_t$ or $\rho_t + \rho_{\mathrm{Ag}}$) and $\Delta z$ = 0.25 mm the bin width. The MC DEF is the dose ratio for $w$ = 1 % and $w = 0$, from $4\times10^{6}$ histories in five batches whose spread gives the standard error; the analytical reference is {{REF:eq:def_slab}} applied to the buried layer. Two checks preceded the runs: for a broad 30 keV beam the ratio of surface dose to primary kerma is 1.31 owing to backscatter, which depends on field size and source distance [@grosswendt1990], and the energy-transfer coefficient from xraylib data agrees with the NIST $\mu_{en}/\rho$ within 0.3 % for O, C and H at 20–40 keV, where the two coefficients practically coincide.

### 3.3.3. Dose enhancement factor of Ag-loaded soft tissue

The DEF is a ratio of two spectrum-weighted integrals ({{REF:eq:def_spec}}), determined by the energy distribution of the dose integrand $E\,\Phi(E)\,\mu_{en}/\rho$ ({{REF:fig14_integrand}}). Without filtration the integrand is dominated by the Cu K lines and the 3–15 keV continuum and falls below $10^{-4}$ of its maximum above about 20 keV, because $\mu_{en}/\rho$ of tissue is so small there; the photons above the Ag K-edge therefore contribute almost nothing to the dose. In the dominant region $R(E)$ is only 21.1 at 8 keV and 27.0 at 15 keV, so 1 % Ag raises the integrand almost uniformly by about 20 %. With 1 mm Al the integrand is concentrated between 15 and 45 keV, and above the Ag K-edge, where the integrand of the loaded tissue jumps, $R(E)$ increases from 76.8 at 25.6 keV to 138.5 at 45 keV. Since about half of the photons of this beam lie above the edge, this region provides the larger part of the enhancement.

{{FIG:fig14_integrand|Dose integrand $E\,\Phi(E)\,\mu_{en}/\rho$ for ICRU-44 soft tissue (shaded) and tissue with 1 % Ag (line), without filtration (left) and with 1.0 mm Al (right); 45 kV Cu-anode tube, SpekPy v2. The dashed line marks the Ag K-edge.}}

The DEF increases linearly with the Ag mass fraction $w$ for every filter ({{REF:fig15_def_conc}}, {{REF:t3_5}}), as follows from the mixture rule ({{REF:eq:def_lin}}): $\mathrm{DEF} = 1 + w(\langle R\rangle - 1)$, where $\langle R\rangle$ is the mean of $R(E)$ weighted by the dose spectrum of unloaded tissue ({{REF:eq:ratio_avg}}). The slope per 1 % Ag grows from 0.202 without a filter to 0.804 with 0.2 mm Cu, i.e. $\langle R\rangle$ from about 21 to 81. For 1 % Ag the DEF is 1.20 without a filter, 1.48 with 1 mm Al, 1.62 with 2 mm Al and 1.80 with 0.2 mm Cu, whereas at 0.1 % Ag (1 mg of Ag per gram of tissue) the enhancement is only 2–8 %. The ranking of the filters ({{REF:t3_4}}) confirms that the DEF follows the share of the dose integrand above the Ag K-edge rather than the mean energy: the nearly pure Kα beam behind the Ni filter gives practically the same DEF as the unfiltered beam (1.208 against 1.202), and at a similar HVL 0.1 mm Sn yields less than 1 mm Al (1.444 against 1.482). Strict linearity holds at the surface; inside an extended Ag-loaded region the attenuation by Ag itself makes the dependence non-linear (Section 3.3.6).

{{FIG:fig15_def_conc|Dose enhancement factor of ICRU-44 soft tissue versus the Ag mass fraction for different added filters (SpekPy v2 spectra, kerma approximation at the surface).}}

Compared with SpekPy, the DM gives a DEF lower by 0.6–1.3 % with 0.5–2 mm Al and by less than 1 % with the Sn, Ag and Mo filters, consistent with its weaker high-energy tail. Where residual Cu K lines carry much of the kerma, it is higher, by 0.5 % for 0.25 mm Al and about 2 % for 0.1 and 0.2 mm Cu, because the underestimated lines shift the dose towards the hard continuum. These differences are small compared with those between filters, so the DEF is robust to the spectrum model. With water instead of ICRU-44 tissue as the reference medium, the DEF for 1 % Ag is up to 1.3 % higher for filtered beams (1.4965 with 1 mm Al and 1.8274 with 0.2 mm Cu). Although ICRU-44 tissue contains less oxygen than water, its 1.3 % by mass of Na, P, S, Cl and K raises its $\mu_{en}/\rho$ above that of water at 15–40 keV and so lowers the DEF.

### 3.3.4. Dose enhancement factor in different biological media

The DEF depends on the medium through its $\mu_{en}/\rho$ (Section 2.3). {{REF:t3_13}} gives the DEF for 1 % Ag in soft tissue, brain, cortical bone and water for the unfiltered beam and three filters. Brain and water give practically the same DEF as soft tissue (within 0.006 and 0.023, respectively), so the results for soft tissue also apply to brain tumours such as gliomas, the most frequently studied model of AgNP radiosensitization (Section 1.5), and to aqueous phantoms and cell cultures. In cortical bone, by contrast, the DEF is only 1.03 without a filter and 1.07–1.12 with filtration, because calcium and phosphorus already absorb strongly and the silver-to-bone ratio of $\mu_{en}/\rho$ is 4–22. Silver therefore enhances the dose in soft-tissue tumours selectively with respect to bone, while dosimetry in phantoms must use media whose composition matches that of the target tissue.

### 3.3.5. Optimisation of the filtration

Every filter raises the DEF at the expense of the dose rate, so the optimal filter maximises the DEF at a given transmission. In {{REF:fig16_tradeoff}} (xraylib attenuation coefficients applied to the unfiltered SpekPy spectrum) the Al and Ti curves practically coincide and lie highest from 1 down to about $10^{-3}$, whereas Ni and Cu stay low while the Cu Kα line is transmitted. The K-edge filters fall behind: Sn practically coincides with Ti down to about 10 % transmission and falls below Al and Ti at lower transmissions, Mo levels off near 1.4, and Ag passes through a maximum of about 1.35.

{{FIG:fig16_tradeoff|Dose enhancement factor for 1 % Ag versus the relative air kerma for Al, Ti, Ni, Cu, Mo, Ag and Sn filters of increasing thickness (45 kV Cu-anode tube, baseline geometry; SpekPy v2).}}

For a systematic comparison, the thickness $t$ of each filter was set by the condition of equal air-kerma transmission

$$
\mathcal{T}_K(t) = \frac{\int_{0}^{T_0} \Phi_0(E)\,E\,\left(\mu_{en}/\rho\right)_{\mathrm{air}}\exp\left[-\mu_f(E)\,t\right]\mathrm{d}E}{\int_{0}^{T_0} \Phi_0(E)\,E\,\left(\mu_{en}/\rho\right)_{\mathrm{air}}\,\mathrm{d}E}
$$ {#eq:transm}

where $\mathcal{T}_K(t)$ is the air-kerma transmission, $\Phi_0(E)$ the fluence spectrum of the unfiltered beam, $(\mu_{en}/\rho)_{\mathrm{air}}$ the mass energy-absorption coefficient of air, $\mu_f(E)$ the linear attenuation coefficient of the filter element and $T_0$ = 45 keV the maximum photon energy. Equation {{REF:eq:transm}} was solved for $\mathcal{T}_K$ = 10 %, 1 % and 0.1 % for the elements from Al (Z = 13) to Bi (Z = 83), excluding gases, liquids, P, S, Se, the alkali metals, radioactive elements, Tl and the lanthanides other than Gd ({{REF:fig17_filter_scan}}; selected elements in {{REF:t3_6}}).

The dependence on Z reflects the positions of the absorption edges relative to the Cu lines and the Ag K-edge; at 10 % transmission all elements still give 1.220–1.241. Al to Co (Z = 13–27), with K-edges below the Cu Kα energy, absorb the Cu lines strongly, whereas for Ni to As (Z = 28–33) the K-edge lies above Kα, the transmitted line dominates the kerma, and these are the worst filters at 10 % and 1 %. Sr to Ag (Z = 38–47) absorb the photons above the Ag K-edge and form a deep minimum at 0.1 %, lowest for Ag, Pd and Rh (1.335–1.346); for Cd to Ba (Z = 48–56), with K-edges above the Ag K-edge, the DEF grows with Z. Heavier elements act through L-shell absorption: Gd, with its L3 edge (7.243 keV) below Kα, behaves like the light absorbers, whereas for Hf to Bi the L3 edges lie above Kα (11.919 keV for Au, {{REF:t2_2}}) and W, Au and Pb give only 1.350–1.351 at 1 %.

{{FIG:fig17_filter_scan|Dose enhancement factor for 1 % Ag with filters of elements Z = 13–83 whose thickness gives an equal air-kerma transmission of 10 %, 1 % and 0.1 % (45 kV Cu-anode tube, baseline geometry; SpekPy v2).}}

Among the light and medium-Z absorbers, Al, Si, Ca, Sc and Ti give practically the same DEF at 10 % and 1 % transmission, whereas at 0.1 % Ca, Sc, Ti and V give 1.907 and Al 1.884, i.e. 1.2 % less. As calcium and scandium are unsuitable as filter foils, Al and Ti are the practical materials. Titanium needs a much thinner foil (111.2 instead of 824.4 µm at 1 %) and is slightly more effective at strong attenuation, because in the several-millimetre-thick Al filter the weakly energy-dependent Compton scattering contributes more to the attenuation of the hard photons.

For an irradiation experiment, {{REF:t3_7}} gives the tissue kerma at the sample surface and the time needed to deliver a dose $D$ at tube current $I$,

$$
t_D = \frac{D}{c\,I\int_{0}^{T_0} \Phi(E)\,E\,\left(\mu_{en}/\rho\right)_t\,\mathrm{d}E}
$$ {#eq:texp}

where $D$ = 2 Gy, $I$ = 40 mA, $\Phi(E)$ is the fluence spectrum per unit tube charge at 10 cm (cm$^{-2}$ keV$^{-1}$ mAs$^{-1}$), $(\mu_{en}/\rho)_t$ refers to ICRU-44 tissue and $c = 1.602 \times 10^{-13}$ Gy g keV$^{-1}$ converts keV/g into Gy; under CPE this kerma equals the absorbed dose at the surface, without backscatter (Section 3.2.5). Without a filter 2 Gy are delivered in 0.12 s, which is difficult to time reproducibly, whereas 1 mm and 2 mm Al need 14.6 and 32.5 s. The 0.2 mm Cu filter owes the highest DEF of the studied set (1.80) only to its very strong attenuation (at an equal transmission of 0.1 %, Cu gives 1.74, Al and Ti 1.88–1.91); it needs 158.4 s, and its DEF changes steeply with thickness ({{REF:fig16_tradeoff}}), which makes it sensitive to tolerances.

Filtration is thus the decisive design lever for dose enhancement by Ag with a copper-anode tube. Aluminium of 1–2 mm, which allows convenient exposure times, or titanium of 0.1–0.3 mm is the optimal choice. K-edge filters with edges at 20–30 keV (Mo, Ag, Sn) and filters that transmit the Cu Kα line (Ni, thin Cu) should be avoided.
