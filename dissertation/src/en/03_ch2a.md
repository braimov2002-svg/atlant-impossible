# CHAPTER 2. INTERACTION OF X-RAYS WITH BIOLOGICAL MATTER AND SILVER

This chapter considers the interactions through which kilovoltage photons transfer energy to biological matter and to silver: the physical processes and their dependence on energy and atomic number (Section 2.1), the cross-sections of silver (Section 2.2) and the attenuation and energy-absorption coefficients of soft tissue, brain, bone and water, which determine the absorbed dose and the dose enhancement factor (DEF) (Section 2.3). Coefficients of the International Commission on Radiation Units and Measurements (ICRU) reference media are used throughout.

## 2.1. Physical processes of photon interaction with matter

At energies up to 50 keV, photons interact through photoelectric absorption, incoherent (Compton) and coherent (Rayleigh) scattering; pair production (threshold $2m_ec^2 = 1.022$ MeV) is impossible [@podgorsak2016; @attix1986]. The mass attenuation coefficient is the sum of the partial coefficients [@attix1986; @andreo2017]:

$$
\frac{\mu}{\rho} = \frac{\tau}{\rho} + \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{coh}}}{\rho} + \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{inc}}}{\rho}
$$ {#eq:mu_sum}

where $\mu$ is the linear attenuation coefficient, $\rho$ the density, and $\tau/\rho$, $\sigma_{\mathrm{coh}}/\rho$ and $\sigma_{\mathrm{inc}}/\rho$ the mass coefficients (cm²/g) of photoelectric absorption, coherent and incoherent scattering. Mixture coefficients are mass-fraction-weighted sums of elemental ones [@attix1986], taken from the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) tables [@nist_xraymass; @nist_xcom].

### 2.1.1. The photoelectric effect

In the photoelectric effect the photon is absorbed by an atom as a whole and a bound electron of shell $i$ is ejected with the kinetic energy

$$
T_e = E - E_i
$$ {#eq:photo_energy}

where $E$ is the photon energy and $E_i$ the binding energy of the shell, the recoil energy of the atom being negligible [@podgorsak2016; @attix1986]. Absorption by a free electron is forbidden by energy and momentum conservation, so the effect is strongest for the most tightly bound accessible electrons: when $E$ exceeds the K-shell binding energy, about 80 % of photoabsorptions occur in the K shell [@podgorsak2016]. At kilovoltage energies the photoelectrons are emitted mainly at large angles to the photon direction [@chernyaev2004].

In photoabsorption a bound electron, preferentially of the innermost accessible shell, is ejected with the photon energy $E$ minus its binding energy; between absorption edges [@podgorsak2016]

$$
\frac{\tau}{\rho} \propto \frac{Z^{3}}{E^{3}}
$$ {#eq:tau_scaling}

where $Z$ is the atomic number of the absorber; the atomic cross-section scales as $Z^{4}/E^{3}$ [@podgorsak2016; @attix1986]. Per unit mass, a heavy element thus absorbs far more strongly than H, C, N and O, which make up almost all the mass of soft tissue [@icru44]. Below its K-edge, however, the K-shell electrons cannot take part, and the advantage is much smaller than the atomic numbers suggest.

Absorption edges are abrupt increases of $\tau/\rho$ at the shell binding energies [@podgorsak2016]; for silver the K-edge lies at 25.514 keV and the L-subshell edges between 3.35 and 3.81 keV. The vacancy is filled from an outer shell, with emission of a fluorescence x-ray or an Auger electron [@bambynek1972; @krause1979]. The K-shell fluorescence yield $\omega_K$ rises steeply with $Z$ and equals 0.831 for silver ({{REF:t2_2}}) [@krause1979; @schoonjans2011]. In H, C, N and O (K-shell binding energies below 1 keV) practically all the absorbed energy stays local, whereas silver re-emits much of it as Kα photons of about 22 keV with a mean free path of about 1.5 cm in soft tissue; its low-energy Auger electrons deposit their energy close to the atom (Section 1.4).

### 2.1.2. Compton and Rayleigh scattering

In incoherent (Compton) scattering the photon transfers part of its energy to an electron that can be regarded as free and at rest; energy and momentum conservation give the Compton shift [@compton1923]

$$
\lambda' - \lambda = \frac{h}{m_ec}\left(1 - \cos\vartheta\right)
$$ {#eq:compton_shift}

where $\lambda$ and $\lambda'$ are the photon wavelengths before and after scattering, $h$ the Planck constant, $m_e$ the electron mass, $c$ the speed of light and $\vartheta$ the scattering angle; $h/m_ec$ = 2.426 pm is the Compton wavelength. The angular distribution follows the Klein–Nishina differential cross-section per electron [@klein1929; @attix1986]

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\mathrm{KN}}}{\mathrm{d}\Omega} = \frac{r_e^{2}}{2}\left(\frac{E'}{E}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{E}{E'} + \frac{E'}{E} - \sin^{2}\vartheta\right)
$$ {#eq:kn}

where $r_e$ is the classical electron radius and $E$ and $E'$ the photon energies before and after scattering. Since the cross-section is per electron, the Compton mass coefficient is proportional to the number of electrons per gram, $Z/A$, which is about 0.5 for all elements except hydrogen; it therefore depends only weakly on $Z$ [@podgorsak2016].

In Compton scattering at these energies the photon retains most of its energy [@podgorsak2016; @attix1986], and electron binding, described by the incoherent scattering function $S(x,Z)$, reduces the Klein–Nishina cross-section at low energies and small angles; Rayleigh scattering, described by the atomic form factor $F(x,Z)$ ($x$ is the momentum-transfer variable), only deflects the photon [@hubbell1975]. In soft tissue incoherent scattering overtakes photoabsorption in the upper part of the range, whereas in silver photoabsorption dominates throughout [@podgorsak2016; @nist_xcom].

### 2.1.3. Pair production

In the Coulomb field of a nucleus a photon can be converted into an electron–positron pair if its energy exceeds the threshold $2m_ec^{2}$ = 1.022 MeV; in the field of an atomic electron (triplet production) the threshold is $4m_ec^{2}$ = 2.044 MeV [@podgorsak2016; @hubbell2006]. The atomic cross-section of pair production grows approximately as $Z^{2}$ and with photon energy, and it dominates in high-Z materials above about 5 MeV [@podgorsak2016]. For the 45 kV tube studied here pair production is energetically impossible; it matters only for the comparison with megavoltage beams, in which Compton scattering and pair production, both weakly dependent on $Z$ per unit mass, make the physical dose enhancement by high-Z elements small [@cho2005].

## 2.2. Cross-sections of photon interaction with silver

{{REF:fig24_partial}} compares the partial mass cross-sections of soft tissue and silver, calculated with the xraylib library [@schoonjans2011] by the mixture rule. In ICRU-44 soft tissue the photoelectric effect provides 93 % of the interactions at 10 keV but only 40 % at 30 keV and 17 % at 45 keV; incoherent scattering overtakes it at 28.4 keV, and coherent scattering contributes at most about 12 %. In silver, the photoelectric effect provides at least 93 % of the interactions throughout 10–45 keV, and its cross-section jumps at the L-edges (3.35–3.81 keV) and at the K-edge (25.514 keV), whereas the scattering cross-sections are comparable to those of tissue. Silver thus differs from tissue almost only by photoabsorption, the process that carries practically all of the energy transferred to electrons [@hubbell2006; @nist_xcom].

{{FIG:fig24_partial|Partial mass cross-sections of photoelectric absorption, incoherent (Compton) and coherent (Rayleigh) scattering and the total cross-section for (a) ICRU-44 soft tissue and (b) silver at 1–200 keV (xraylib data). The vertical dashed line marks the Ag K-edge.}}

The mass energy-absorption coefficient, tabulated by NIST from Seltzer's calculations [@seltzer1993; @nist_xraymass], counts only the energy transferred to charged particles and not re-emitted as bremsstrahlung [@attix1986]:

$$
\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho} = \left[\frac{\tau}{\rho}\left(1-\frac{X}{E}\right) + \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{inc}}}{\rho}\,\frac{\bar{T}_{\mathrm{inc}}}{E}\right]\left(1-\bar{g}\right)
$$ {#eq:muen_def}

where $X$ is the mean fluorescence energy emitted per photoabsorption, $\bar{T}_{\mathrm{inc}}$ the mean kinetic energy of the Compton recoil electron and $\bar{g}$ the mean fraction of the secondary-electron kinetic energy converted into bremsstrahlung, negligible here; other symbols are as in {{REF:eq:mu_sum}} [@attix1986].

{{REF:fig02_coeffs}} compares the NIST coefficients [@nist_xraymass] of silver and ICRU-44 soft tissue [@icru44]. Above the L-edges both coefficients of silver exceed those of tissue by more than an order of magnitude, the ratio of the $\mu_{en}/\rho$ values rising from 21.1 at 8 keV to 31.9 at 25 keV. At the K-edge $\mu/\rho$ of silver jumps 5.8-fold (from 9.53 to 55.4 cm²/g) but $\mu_{en}/\rho$ only 2.4-fold (from 8.53 to 20.3 cm²/g). The reason is K-shell fluorescence: after most K-shell photoabsorptions the K x-ray energy escapes the interaction site and is excluded from $\mu_{en}/\rho$.

{{FIG:fig02_coeffs|Mass attenuation (solid) and mass energy-absorption (dashed) coefficients of silver and ICRU-44 soft tissue at 1–50 keV (NIST data). The vertical dashed line marks the Ag K-edge at 25.51 keV, the arrow the Ag L-subshell edges.}}

For tissue the two coefficients diverge above about 10 keV, as scattering, which deposits little energy, gains weight. Above the K-edge, $\mu_{en}/\rho$ of silver falls more slowly than its $\mu/\rho$, since the fixed fluorescence energy becomes a smaller fraction of the photon energy, and more slowly than that of tissue. After the jump at the edge from about 32 to about 76, the silver-to-tissue ratio therefore keeps growing, to 102.8 at 30 keV, 120.1 at 35 keV and a maximum of about 139 at 40–45 keV. The fraction of the energy fluence above the Ag K-edge is thus the key beam characteristic for silver.

## 2.3. Mass attenuation coefficients of biological matter

The composition of a biological medium determines its interaction coefficients. {{REF:t2_3}} gives the compositions and densities of the ICRU-44 reference media used here [@icru44; @nist_xraymass]: soft tissue, brain (grey/white matter), cortical bone and water. Soft tissue, brain and water consist essentially of H, C, N and O, with less than 2 % of heavier elements, whereas cortical bone contains 10.3 % phosphorus and 22.5 % calcium by mass. Their coefficients were calculated by the mixture rule from the NIST elemental tables (the deviation from the NIST compound tables is below 0.12 % at 10–40 keV) and are given in {{REF:t2_4}} and {{REF:fig25_media}}.

{{TABLE:t2_3}}

According to the International Commission on Radiation Units and Measurements (ICRU), kerma $K$ is the total initial kinetic energy of the charged particles liberated by photons per unit mass, the collision kerma $K_{\mathrm{col}}$ its part not converted into bremsstrahlung, and the absorbed dose $D$ the mean energy imparted per unit mass [@icru85; @attix1986]. Under charged-particle equilibrium (CPE), when the charged-particle energy entering a small volume equals that leaving it, the absorbed dose equals the collision kerma [@attix1986; @andreo2017]:

$$
D = K_{\mathrm{col}} = \Psi\,\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}
$$ {#eq:dose_cpe}

where $\Psi = \Phi E$ is the energy fluence of monoenergetic photons of energy $E$ and fluence $\Phi$; for a polyenergetic beam the expression is integrated over the spectrum (Section 3.2.5). Secondary electrons from photons below 50 keV have sub-millimetre ranges in tissue, so CPE holds except close to boundaries, and by the Fano theorem it is not disturbed by density variations in a medium of uniform composition [@fano1954]. Two media in the same spectrum therefore receive doses in the ratio of their spectrum-averaged $\mu_{en}/\rho$, which underlies the macroscopic, kerma-based DEF (Section 3.3.1); the relation fails only within the electron ranges from a high-Z inclusion.

Soft tissue, brain and water have practically the same coefficients: their $\mu_{en}/\rho$ differ by less than 4 % at 8–40 keV, and incoherent scattering overtakes photoabsorption at 28.4 keV in tissue and brain and at 27.9 keV in water. Bone, owing to calcium and phosphorus, absorbs about five times more strongly per unit mass at the Cu K lines (49.0 against 9.74 cm²/g at 8.05 keV) and 6.6 times more at 30 keV, and photoabsorption dominates in it up to 55.2 keV. As a result, the ratio $R$ of $\mu_{en}/\rho$ of silver to that of the medium is 21–32 below the Ag K-edge and 102–142 at 30–40 keV for soft tissue, brain and water, but only 4–5 below the edge and 15–22 above it for bone ({{REF:fig25_media}}). Since the DEF is a spectrum-weighted mean of $R$ (Section 3.3.1), silver can be expected to enhance the dose in brain and water as in soft tissue, but much less in bone.

## 2.4. Conclusions to Chapter 2

1. At photon energies up to 45 keV the interaction of x-rays with biological matter and silver consists of photoelectric absorption and incoherent and coherent scattering; pair production (threshold 1.022 MeV) is impossible.
2. In silver the photoelectric effect provides at least 93 % of the interactions at 10–45 keV and jumps at the L-edges (3.35–3.81 keV) and the K-edge (25.514 keV); in soft tissue it is overtaken by Compton scattering at 28.4 keV. Because of K fluorescence, the mass energy-absorption coefficient of silver rises at the K-edge only 2.4-fold, against 5.8-fold for the attenuation coefficient.
3. Soft tissue, brain and water have practically identical mass energy-absorption coefficients, so the silver-to-medium ratio is 21–32 below and 102–142 above the Ag K-edge in all three; in cortical bone, rich in calcium and phosphorus, the ratio is only 4–22.
4. Under charged-particle equilibrium the absorbed dose equals the collision kerma, so the macroscopic dose enhancement by silver is determined by the spectrum-weighted ratio of the mass energy-absorption coefficients, i.e. by the fraction of the photon energy fluence above the Ag K-edge.
5. NIST $\mu_{en}/\rho$ are used, because xraylib 4.3.0 `CS_Energy` underestimates them for light elements by up to 56 % (oxygen by 37 %), which would overestimate the DEF (1.62 instead of 1.48).
