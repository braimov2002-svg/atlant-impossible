# CHAPTER 1. RADIOSENSITIZATION BY HIGH-Z ELEMENTS AND X-RAY SPECTRUM MODELING: LITERATURE REVIEW

## 1.1. Radiotherapy and the problem of normal-tissue tolerance

Cancer is a major cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide [@bray2024], and radiotherapy (RT) is one of its principal treatment modalities [@baskar2012], with an optimal utilisation rate of approximately 52 % estimated from evidence-based guidelines [@delaney2005]. RT damages cellular DNA directly through secondary electrons or indirectly through the free radicals of water radiolysis [@hall2018]. Because energy deposition is not selective, the healthy tissues traversed by the beam are damaged as well, and their response limits the tumour dose [@hall2018; @baskar2012]. When the sigmoid dose–response curves for tumour control and normal-tissue complications lie close together, i.e. when the therapeutic ratio is small, normal-tissue toxicity becomes the main factor limiting RT [@hall2018; @baskar2012]. Hypoxic tumour cells, less sensitive to x-rays because of the oxygen effect, aggravate the problem [@hall2018].

Fractionation [@hall2018] and conformal, image-guided techniques such as intensity-modulated RT [@khan2014] spare normal tissue but do not change the ratio of the energy absorbed per unit mass in tumour and normal cells within the same beam region. Radiosensitizers, which increase the effect of radiation on tumour cells more than on normal cells, include oxygen mimetics such as the nitroimidazoles [@wardman2007] and compounds of platinum, gold, ruthenium and other transition metals that combine chemical and biological activity with heavy atoms [@gill2019]. High-Z elements act by an essentially physical principle: concentrated in the tumour, they absorb more photon energy per unit mass than soft tissue, raising the tumour dose while the dose to element-free surrounding tissue remains practically unchanged [@hainfeld2004; @kuncic2018]. The gain is expressed by the dose enhancement factor (DEF), the ratio of the absorbed doses in tissue with and without the agent (Section 2.6), which, unlike chemical sensitization, can be calculated from interaction data once the radiation field is known.

Monte Carlo (MC) calculations for gold nanoparticles showed a much larger macroscopic dose enhancement with kilovoltage than with megavoltage beams [@cho2005], because the strongly Z-dependent photoelectric effect dominates only at low energies (Section 1.2). Kilovoltage beams are used in superficial and orthovoltage therapy [@khan2014], and their dosimetry protocols specify the beam quality by the half-value layer (HVL) [@ma2001; @iaea_trs398]. The DEF of an agent therefore cannot be stated without the x-ray spectrum, which depends on the tube design and filtration; the present work addresses this problem for silver and a 45 kV copper-anode tube.

## 1.2. Interaction of kilovoltage photons with biological matter

At energies up to 50 keV, photons interact through photoelectric absorption, incoherent (Compton) and coherent (Rayleigh) scattering; pair production (threshold $2m_ec^2 = 1.022$ MeV) is impossible [@podgorsak2016; @attix1986]. The mass attenuation coefficient is the sum of the partial coefficients [@attix1986; @andreo2017]:

$$
\frac{\mu}{\rho} = \frac{\tau}{\rho} + \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{coh}}}{\rho} + \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{inc}}}{\rho}
$$ {#eq:mu_sum}

where $\mu$ is the linear attenuation coefficient, $\rho$ the density, and $\tau/\rho$, $\sigma_{\mathrm{coh}}/\rho$ and $\sigma_{\mathrm{inc}}/\rho$ the mass coefficients (cm²/g) of photoelectric absorption, coherent and incoherent scattering. For mixtures such as soft tissue the elemental coefficients are weighted by mass fractions [@attix1986], using data tabulated by the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) [@nist_xraymass; @nist_xcom]. In photoabsorption a bound electron, preferentially of the innermost accessible shell, is ejected with the photon energy $E$ minus its binding energy; between absorption edges [@podgorsak2016]

$$
\frac{\tau}{\rho} \propto \frac{Z^{3}}{E^{3}}
$$ {#eq:tau_scaling}

where $Z$ is the atomic number of the absorber; the atomic cross-section scales as $Z^{4}/E^{3}$ [@podgorsak2016; @attix1986]. Per unit mass, a heavy element thus absorbs far more strongly than H, C, N and O, which make up almost all the mass of soft tissue [@icru44]. Below its K-edge, however, the K-shell electrons cannot take part, and the advantage is much smaller than the atomic numbers suggest.

Absorption edges are abrupt increases of $\tau/\rho$ at the shell binding energies [@podgorsak2016]; for silver the K-edge lies at 25.514 keV and the L-subshell edges between 3.35 and 3.81 keV. The vacancy is filled from an outer shell, with emission of a fluorescence x-ray or an Auger electron [@bambynek1972; @krause1979]. The K-shell fluorescence yield $\omega_K$ rises steeply with $Z$ and equals 0.831 for silver ({{REF:t2_2}}) [@krause1979; @schoonjans2011]. In H, C, N and O (K-shell binding energies below 1 keV) practically all the absorbed energy stays local, whereas silver re-emits much of it as Kα photons of about 22 keV with a mean free path of about 1.5 cm in soft tissue; its low-energy Auger electrons deposit their energy close to the atom (Section 1.3).

In Compton scattering at these energies the photon retains most of its energy [@podgorsak2016; @attix1986]. Binding reduces the Klein–Nishina cross-section at low energies and small angles, as described by the incoherent scattering function $S(x,Z)$ ($x$ is the momentum-transfer variable), while Rayleigh scattering only deflects the photon and is described by the atomic form factor $F(x,Z)$ [@hubbell1975]. In soft tissue incoherent scattering overtakes photoabsorption in the upper part of the range, whereas in silver photoabsorption dominates throughout [@podgorsak2016; @nist_xcom]. The mass energy-absorption coefficient counts only the energy transferred to charged particles and not re-emitted as bremsstrahlung [@attix1986; @seltzer1993]:

$$
\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho} = \left[\frac{\tau}{\rho}\left(1-\frac{X}{E}\right) + \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{inc}}}{\rho}\,\frac{\bar{T}_{\mathrm{inc}}}{E}\right]\left(1-\bar{g}\right)
$$ {#eq:muen_def}

where $X$ is the mean fluorescence energy emitted per photoabsorption, $\bar{T}_{\mathrm{inc}}$ the mean kinetic energy of the Compton recoil electron and $\bar{g}$ the mean fraction of the secondary-electron kinetic energy converted into bremsstrahlung, negligible for kilovoltage photons in low-Z media; other symbols are as in {{REF:eq:mu_sum}} [@attix1986]. The NIST $\mu_{en}/\rho$ values used in this work are based on the calculations of Seltzer [@seltzer1993; @nist_xraymass].

According to the International Commission on Radiation Units and Measurements (ICRU), kerma $K$ is the sum of the initial kinetic energies of all charged particles liberated by photons per unit mass, the collision kerma $K_{\mathrm{col}}$ its part not converted into bremsstrahlung, and the absorbed dose $D$ the mean energy imparted per unit mass (1 Gy = 1 J/kg) [@icru85; @attix1986]. Under charged-particle equilibrium (CPE), when the charged-particle energy entering a small volume equals that leaving it, the absorbed dose equals the collision kerma [@attix1986; @andreo2017]:

$$
D = K_{\mathrm{col}} = \Psi\,\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}
$$ {#eq:dose_cpe}

where $\Psi = \Phi E$ is the energy fluence of monoenergetic photons of energy $E$ and fluence $\Phi$; for a polyenergetic beam the expression is integrated over the spectrum (Section 2.5). Secondary electrons from photons below 50 keV have sub-millimetre ranges in tissue, so CPE holds except close to boundaries, and by the Fano theorem it is not disturbed by density variations in a medium of uniform composition [@fano1954]. Two media in the same spectrum therefore receive doses in the ratio of their spectrum-averaged $\mu_{en}/\rho$, which underlies the macroscopic, kerma-based DEF (Section 2.6); the relation fails only within the electron ranges from a high-Z inclusion.

{{REF:fig02_coeffs}} compares the coefficients of silver and ICRU-44 soft tissue [@icru44] from the NIST tables [@nist_xraymass]. Above the L-edges both coefficients of silver exceed those of tissue by more than an order of magnitude, the ratio of the $\mu_{en}/\rho$ values rising from 21.0 at 8 keV to 31.8 at 25 keV. At the K-edge $\mu/\rho$ of silver increases by a factor of 5.8, from 9.53 to 55.4 cm²/g, but $\mu_{en}/\rho$ only by a factor of 2.4, from 8.54 to 20.3 cm²/g. The reason is K-shell fluorescence: about 83 % of the K vacancies relax radiatively, mainly by emitting Kα photons, whose energy escapes the interaction site and is excluded from $\mu_{en}/\rho$.

{{FIG:fig02_coeffs|Mass attenuation (solid) and mass energy-absorption (dashed) coefficients of silver and ICRU-44 soft tissue at 1–50 keV (NIST data). The vertical dashed line marks the Ag K-edge at 25.51 keV; the arrow marks the Ag L-subshell edges.}}

For soft tissue the two coefficients nearly coincide below about 10 keV and diverge above, as scattering, which deposits little energy, gains weight. Above the K-edge, $\mu_{en}/\rho$ of silver falls more slowly than its $\mu/\rho$, since the fixed fluorescence energy becomes a smaller fraction of the photon energy, and more slowly than that of tissue. After the jump at the edge from about 32 to about 76, the silver-to-tissue ratio therefore keeps growing, to 102.7 at 30 keV, 120.0 at 35 keV and a maximum of about 139 at 40–45 keV. The fraction of the energy fluence above the Ag K-edge is thus the key beam characteristic for silver, and its determination for a copper-anode tube is a central task of this work.

## 1.3. High-Z radiosensitizers: from iodine contrast to metal nanoparticles

Spiers first analysed the dose to soft tissue near bone in kilovoltage beams, combining enhanced energy absorption in the high-Z medium with electron transport into adjacent tissue [@spiers1949], and Das and Chopra quantified the backscatter dose perturbation at tissue–high-Z interfaces [@das1995]. The first systematic studies used iodinated contrast media, which sensitized cultured mammalian cells to x-rays but not to gamma rays [@matsudaira1980], pointing to photoabsorption of low-energy photons by iodine. Dose enhancement in iodine-loaded tumours was calculated for kilovoltage x-rays [@mello1983] and for brain-tumour therapy [@solberg1992], and a computed tomography scanner modified for therapy (CTRx) delivered the first such treatment of human brain metastases [@rose1999]. Monochromatic synchrotron beams were used to treat rats bearing iodine-loaded F98 gliomas [@adam2003] and to find the optimal energy for the radiotoxicity of iodinated compounds [@corde2004]; contrast-enhanced kilovoltage RT was analysed by MC [@verhaegen2005], and modified megavoltage beams were combined with iodine and gadolinium contrast [@robar2002].

Platinum drugs add a high-Z atom to a chemotherapeutic agent: cisplatin with monochromatic synchrotron x-rays exploiting the platinum K-edge cured rats bearing radioresistant F98 gliomas [@biston2004]. Pignol et al. analysed inner-shell ionisation and Auger cascades in radiosensitization by iododeoxyuridine, bromodeoxyuridine, platinum salts and gadolinium porphyrins [@pignol2003]; when the heavy atom lies in or near DNA, short-range Auger electrons deposit their energy at this critical target. The subcellular location of the high-Z atom therefore matters, not only its concentration.

The modern stage began when gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) 1.9 nm in diameter markedly improved the one-year survival of tumour-bearing mice irradiated with 250 kVp (kilovolt peak) x-rays [@hainfeld2004], and the parameters governing nanoparticle efficacy (material, size, coating, photon energy) have since been analysed systematically [@retif2015]. AuNP radiosensitization was shown in vitro with superficial kilovoltage x-rays [@rahman2009], orthovoltage and superficial beams for folate-conjugated AuNPs in HeLa cells [@khoshgard2014] and monoenergetic synchrotron beams [@rahman2014]; uptake and radiosensitization depend on particle size [@chithrani2010], and experiments with DNA identified photon energy, size and concentration as the governing parameters [@brun2009]. Reviews [@mesbahi2010; @jain2012; @haume2016; @song2017; @penninckx2020] and a roadmap to clinical use [@schuemann2016] summarise these results.

The physics has been studied mainly by MC simulation. After the first estimate of the macroscopic tumour dose enhancement by AuNPs [@cho2005], the tumour-selective enhancement was quantified for various photon sources [@zhang2009] and therapeutic beams [@kakade2015], and kilovoltage energies proved optimal for AuNP-enhanced arc therapy [@koger2016]. Radiosensitization depends on the photon energy and on nanoparticle size, concentration and location [@lechtman2011]; microscopically, the enhancement is highly localised [@jones2010], and the spatial distribution of the secondary electrons was characterised with Geant4 [@leung2011]. This strong energy dependence makes a reliable incident spectrum a prerequisite of any dose-enhancement calculation.

The biological effect, however, cannot be explained by the increase in the mean dose alone. Radio-enhancement comprises a physical stage (additional photon absorption; emission of photoelectrons, Auger electrons and fluorescence), a chemical stage (additional production of reactive oxygen species, ROS) and a biological stage (oxidative stress, cell-cycle perturbation, inhibition of DNA repair) [@kuncic2018; @her2017; @butterworth2012; @rosa2017]. With a local effect model, McMahon et al. showed that the highly inhomogeneous nanoscale energy deposition near heavy-atom nanoparticles can explain effects exceeding the macroscopic dose enhancement [@mcmahon2011], so the sensitizer enhancement ratio (SER) measured in cells may exceed the macroscopic DEF, which nevertheless remains the baseline for separating the physical contribution from the chemical and biological ones.

Although gold dominates the literature, the best element is not obvious; it has been sought through kerma-based comparisons of nanoparticles of various elements over photon energy [@roeske2007] and through physical modelling [@mcmahon2016]. In the kerma approximation the comparison reduces to the ratio $R(E)$ of the mass energy-absorption coefficient of the element to that of tissue, since for tissue containing a mass fraction $w$ of the element and irradiated by monoenergetic photons of energy $E$ the DEF equals $1 + w\,[R(E) - 1]$ (Section 2.6). {{REF:fig03_ratio}} shows $R(E)$ for Ag, I, Gd, Pt, Au and Bi at 5–45 keV, covering practically the whole spectrum of the 45 kV tube.

{{FIG:fig03_ratio|Ratio of the mass energy-absorption coefficients of Ag, I, Gd, Pt, Au and Bi to that of ICRU-44 soft tissue at 5–45 keV (NIST data). The vertical dashed line marks the Ag K-edge at 25.51 keV.}}

Below about 12 keV the ratios for silver, platinum, gold and bismuth are similar and that for iodine is somewhat higher. Above its L-edges (7.2–8.4 keV) and up to about 13 keV gadolinium leads, absorbing the Cu Kα lines at about 8.0 keV particularly efficiently. The L-edges of platinum, gold and bismuth at about 12–16 keV raise their ratios several-fold, leaving silver the lowest of the six between about 12 and 25.5 keV. Above its K-edge silver first equals and then exceeds gadolinium (K-edge 50.239 keV, above the maximum photon energy) and exceeds iodine up to the iodine K-edge at 33.169 keV. At 40–45 keV iodine, platinum and silver become comparable, while gold and bismuth remain the highest.

Two conclusions follow. First, per unit mass silver should be a weaker physical sensitizer than platinum, gold and bismuth, and its advantage over iodine and gadolinium is limited to energies above its K-edge, so its ranking depends on the spectrum. Second, since its ratio to tissue is only 21–32 between 8 keV and the edge, an interval that includes the Cu K lines at 8.0–8.9 keV, but 76–139 above it, the tube design parameters and especially the filtration, which redistribute the energy fluence relative to 25.514 keV, are expected to be decisive for the DEF, as tested quantitatively in Chapter 3. The arguments that nevertheless favour silver are reviewed in Section 1.4.
