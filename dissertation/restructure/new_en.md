@@p1_1a
The effects of ionising radiation in biological matter develop in successive stages separated by many orders of magnitude in time [@hall2018; @azzam2012]. In the physical stage (about $10^{-18}$–$10^{-15}$ s) photons transfer energy to electrons, which ionise and excite the atoms and molecules along their tracks. In the physico-chemical stage (up to about $10^{-12}$ s) the ionised and excited molecules, mostly water, which forms about 70 % of the mass of soft tissue, dissociate into radicals and ions; the hydroxyl radical ·OH, the hydrated electron and the hydrogen atom are the main products of water radiolysis. In the chemical stage (up to about $10^{-6}$ s) the radicals diffuse and react with each other and with biomolecules, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) are formed in the presence of oxygen. The biological stage, from seconds to years, comprises enzymatic repair, cell-cycle arrest, cell death and, if damage is misrepaired, mutations, carcinogenesis and late tissue effects [@hall2018; @desouky2015].

@@p1_1b
Damage to DNA, the principal target of radiation in the cell, arises in two ways [@hall2018; @lomax2013]. In direct action the DNA molecule itself is ionised or excited by a secondary electron; in indirect action it is attacked by radicals, mainly ·OH, produced in the surrounding water within a few nanometres. For sparsely ionising radiation such as kilovoltage x-rays, about two thirds of the DNA damage is caused indirectly, which is why radical scavengers protect cells and oxygen, which fixes radical damage, sensitizes them (the oxygen effect) [@hall2018]. Oxidative stress does not end with irradiation: perturbed mitochondrial metabolism and inflammatory responses keep the levels of ROS elevated for long periods, and redox signalling can spread damage to non-irradiated bystander cells [@azzam2012; @desouky2015].

@@h1_2
## 1.2. DNA damage, mutations and chromosomal aberrations

@@p1_2a
DNA damage is a change in the chemical structure of DNA, whereas a mutation is a change in its base sequence; damage leads to a mutation when it is repaired inaccurately or replicated before repair [@jackson2009]. Ionising radiation produces single-strand breaks (SSBs), double-strand breaks (DSBs), damaged or lost bases, DNA–protein crosslinks and inter- and intra-strand crosslinks [@ward1988; @lomax2013]. One gray produces in a mammalian cell about 1000 SSBs and about 40 DSBs [@ward1988; @hall2018]. A distinctive feature of ionising radiation is clustered damage, two or more lesions within one or two helical turns formed by a single track; such sites, including complex DSBs, are difficult to repair and largely determine cell killing, which radiotherapy exploits [@lomax2013].

@@p1_2b
Misrepaired or unrepaired lesions change the DNA sequence: deletions, duplications, inversions, insertions and translocations of DNA segments [@jackson2009; @obe2002]. Mutations may be silent, may alter the encoded protein or may inactivate a gene; inactivation of the tumour suppressor gene *TP53*, whose product p53 activates DNA repair, arrests the cell cycle and triggers apoptosis when the damage is irreparable, leaves the cell vulnerable to malignant transformation [@lane1992]. At the chromosome level, misrejoined DSBs give the microscopically visible structural chromosomal aberrations (deletions, rings, dicentrics, translocations, inversions), whereas numerical aberrations change the number of chromosomes [@obe2002]. The yield of dicentric chromosomes in lymphocytes increases with dose and is used for biological dosimetry [@hall2018].

@@h1_3
## 1.3. Effects of radiation on mitochondria and DNA repair

@@p1_3a
Mitochondria contain their own circular DNA (mtDNA) in several copies. Lacking protective histones and having a limited repair capacity, mtDNA suffers strand breaks, base damage and large deletions after irradiation and accumulates mutations more readily than nuclear DNA [@kam2013]. Radiation also changes the mtDNA copy number, the activity of the electron transport chain and oxidative phosphorylation; since the electron transport chain is the main intracellular source of ROS, damaged mitochondria sustain the oxidative stress that contributes to delayed and persistent radiation effects [@kam2013; @azzam2012]. Mitochondria are therefore regarded as a second, extranuclear target of radiation, relevant also to nanoparticles that act through ROS [@kam2013].

@@p1_3b
The cell responds to DNA damage with the DNA-damage response, a signalling network that detects lesions, arrests the cell cycle and activates repair or, if repair fails, apoptosis or senescence [@jackson2009]. Base damage and SSBs are removed by base excision repair; bulky lesions by nucleotide excision repair; replication errors by mismatch repair. DSBs, the most lethal lesions, are repaired by non-homologous end joining, active throughout the cell cycle but error-prone, and by homologous recombination, accurate but limited to the S and G2 phases, when a sister chromatid is available [@santivasi2014; @jackson2009]. Differences in these pathways between tumour and normal cells, and their inhibition, are used to widen the therapeutic window of radiotherapy [@santivasi2014; @lomax2013], and a radiosensitizer that increases the number of lesions or impairs their repair acts at this stage.

@@h1_4
## 1.4. High-Z radiosensitizers: from iodine contrast to metal nanoparticles

@@h1_5
## 1.5. Silver-based nano-radiosensitizers

@@p1_5a
Nano-sensitizers are classified by their main element into noble-metal (Au, Ag, Pt), lanthanide (Gd, Hf), other heavy-metal (Bi, W) and non-metal materials, and by their mechanism into physical (increased energy absorption), chemical (catalytic production of ROS) and biological (cell-cycle redistribution, inhibition of DNA repair, induction of autophagy and apoptosis) sensitizers [@zhang2022; @liu2018t]. Their efficacy depends on size, shape, surface coating, concentration and location as well as on the photon energy [@zhang2022]; for gold, for example, nanotriangles radiosensitized glioblastoma cells and tumours in mice [@bhattarai2017]. Silver nanoparticles combine a moderately high atomic number with a pronounced chemical activity: polyvinylpyrrolidone-coated AgNPs activated autophagy through elevated ROS rather than through released silver ions [@zhu2017], gallic-acid-coated AgNPs suppressed radiation-induced epithelial–mesenchymal transition in lung cancer cells [@sunilgowda2018], and conjugation with the aptamer AS1411 and verapamil increased the accumulation of AgNPs in glioma cells and their radiosensitizing effect [@zhao2021].

@@p2_0
This chapter considers the interactions through which kilovoltage photons transfer energy to biological matter and to silver: the physical processes and their dependence on energy and atomic number (Section 2.1), the cross-sections of silver (Section 2.2) and the attenuation and energy-absorption coefficients of soft tissue, brain, bone and water, which determine the absorbed dose and the dose enhancement factor (DEF) (Section 2.3). Coefficients of the International Commission on Radiation Units and Measurements (ICRU) reference media are used throughout.

@@h2_1
## 2.1. Physical processes of photon interaction with matter

@@h2_1_1
### 2.1.1. The photoelectric effect

@@p2_11a
In the photoelectric effect the photon is absorbed by an atom as a whole and a bound electron of shell $i$ is ejected with the kinetic energy

$$
T_e = E - E_i
$$ {#eq:photo_energy}

where $E$ is the photon energy and $E_i$ the binding energy of the shell, the recoil energy of the atom being negligible [@podgorsak2016; @attix1986]. Absorption by a free electron is forbidden by energy and momentum conservation, so the effect is strongest for the most tightly bound accessible electrons: when $E$ exceeds the K-shell binding energy, about 80 % of photoabsorptions occur in the K shell [@podgorsak2016]. At kilovoltage energies the photoelectrons are emitted mainly at large angles to the photon direction [@chernyaev2004].

@@h2_1_2
### 2.1.2. Compton and Rayleigh scattering

@@p2_12a
In incoherent (Compton) scattering the photon transfers part of its energy to an electron that can be regarded as free and at rest; energy and momentum conservation give the Compton shift [@compton1923]

$$
\lambda' - \lambda = \frac{h}{m_ec}\left(1 - \cos\vartheta\right)
$$ {#eq:compton_shift}

where $\lambda$ and $\lambda'$ are the photon wavelengths before and after scattering, $h$ the Planck constant, $m_e$ the electron mass, $c$ the speed of light and $\vartheta$ the scattering angle; $h/m_ec$ = 2.426 pm is the Compton wavelength. The angular distribution follows the Klein–Nishina differential cross-section per electron [@klein1929; @attix1986]

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\mathrm{KN}}}{\mathrm{d}\Omega} = \frac{r_e^{2}}{2}\left(\frac{E'}{E}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{E}{E'} + \frac{E'}{E} - \sin^{2}\vartheta\right)
$$ {#eq:kn}

where $r_e$ is the classical electron radius and $E$ and $E'$ the photon energies before and after scattering. Since the cross-section is per electron, the Compton mass coefficient is proportional to the number of electrons per gram, $Z/A$, which is about 0.5 for all elements except hydrogen; it therefore depends only weakly on $Z$ [@podgorsak2016].

@@h2_1_3
### 2.1.3. Pair production

@@p2_13a
In the Coulomb field of a nucleus a photon can be converted into an electron–positron pair if its energy exceeds the threshold $2m_ec^{2}$ = 1.022 MeV; in the field of an atomic electron (triplet production) the threshold is $4m_ec^{2}$ = 2.044 MeV [@podgorsak2016; @hubbell2006]. The atomic cross-section of pair production grows approximately as $Z^{2}$ and with photon energy, and it dominates in high-Z materials above about 5 MeV [@podgorsak2016]. For the 45 kV tube studied here pair production is energetically impossible; it matters only for the comparison with megavoltage beams, in which Compton scattering and pair production, both weakly dependent on $Z$ per unit mass, make the physical dose enhancement by high-Z elements small [@cho2005].

## 2.2. Cross-sections of photon interaction with silver

@@p2_2a
{{REF:fig24_partial}} compares the partial mass cross-sections of soft tissue and silver, calculated with the xraylib library [@schoonjans2011] by the mixture rule. In ICRU-44 soft tissue the photoelectric effect provides 93 % of the interactions at 10 keV but only 40 % at 30 keV and 17 % at 45 keV; incoherent scattering overtakes it at 28.4 keV, and coherent scattering contributes at most about 12 %. Soft tissue, brain and water have practically the same coefficients: their $\mu_{en}/\rho$ differ by less than 4 % at 8–40 keV, and incoherent scattering overtakes photoabsorption at 28.4 keV in tissue and brain and at 27.9 keV in water. ## 3.1. X-ray tube

@@h3_1_1
### 3.1.1. Design and technical parameters of an x-ray tube with a copper anode

@@p3_1a
Anodes are built either as massive (reflection) anodes or as transmission anodes [@bushberg2012]. In a massive anode the target, of a material with a high melting point and low vapour pressure (tungsten for bremsstrahlung tubes; chromium, iron, copper, molybdenum or silver for characteristic radiation), is embedded in a body of oxygen-free copper that removes the heat, and the working beam leaves through the irradiated face; when the target and the body are of the same metal, as in the copper anode studied here, the anode is homogeneous. In a transmission anode a thin target is deposited on a substrate, usually beryllium, that also serves as the exit window, and the beam axis coincides with the electron beam. The photons generated at the mean electron penetration depth $x_1$ are attenuated in the anode over the layer

$$
x_2 = \begin{cases} X - x_1, & \text{transmission anode},\\ x_1\cos\alpha/\cos\beta, & \text{massive anode}, \end{cases}
$$ {#eq:anode_layer}

where $X$ is the thickness of a transmission anode, $\alpha$ the angle between the electron beam and the normal to the target surface and $\beta$ the angle between the normal and the direction of the extracted beam. In the reflection tube of {{REF:fig01_tube}} the electron beam is perpendicular to the central ray, so $\alpha = \theta$ and $\beta = 90° - \theta$, and {{REF:eq:anode_layer}} gives $x_2 = x_1\cos\theta/\sin\theta$, the exit path $d/\sin\theta$ of a photon produced at the depth $d = x_1\cos\theta$ below the surface used in Section 3.2.3.
