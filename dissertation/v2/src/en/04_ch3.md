# CHAPTER 3. RESULTS OF SPECTRUM MODELLING AND DOSE‑ENHANCEMENT CALCULATIONS

This chapter considers the x-ray tube as a source of photon radiation, the calculation of its spectrum with the Kramers formulas and the DEF of soft tissue containing silver and, for comparison, samarium; the approach follows the works of the research group [@abdullaeva2022; @abdullaeva2024].

## 3.1. X-ray tube

The use of an x-ray tube as a source of photon radiation approximately satisfies the requirement of beam monochromaticity: the characteristic lines of the anode are nearly monochromatic, and the continuous spectrum can be narrowed by filters. For this purpose, x-ray microfocus devices intended for scientific research, x-ray diagnostics and radiography can be used. The source of x-rays in such a device is the BS-15 x-ray tube (or its analogue), whose anode target is made of tungsten (W), molybdenum (Mo), copper (Cu) or silver (Ag). The main technical characteristics of the device are listed in {{REF:tab:bs15}}.

The functional capabilities and technical level of x-ray equipment are largely determined by the parameters of its radiation source, the most important of which is the radiation spectrum. However, data on the spectra of x-ray tubes are often missing and are replaced by qualitative estimates of the half-value layer of the dose rate in a standard material and of the "effective" and mean energies. This problem can be solved by calculating the radiation spectrum of the tube from its design and technical parameters.

By adding the bremsstrahlung and characteristic components, an idealised x-ray spectrum is obtained that depends on the anode current, the accelerating voltage and the target material. To determine the true spectrum of the tube, the attenuation of the radiation in the target material, the output window, the filter and the medium between the filter and the irradiated sample must be taken into account. The mechanism of x-ray generation in a tube with a transmission anode is shown in {{REF:f3_mechanism}}, where $x_1$ is the average penetration depth of electrons into the target, $x_2$ the thickness of the target layer in which the radiation is attenuated and $x_3$ the thickness of the output window.

{{FIG:f3_mechanism|Mechanism of x-ray generation in a tube with a transmission anode: x₁ – average penetration depth of electrons into the target; x₂ – thickness of the target layer attenuating the radiation; x₃ – thickness of the output window}}

The electrons with the energy $E_0 = eU$ lose their energy in a thin surface layer of the target. The x-rays generated at depths up to $x_1$ are emitted in all directions, and those emitted in the direction of the beam cross the remaining target thickness $x_2$ and the window $x_3$. Since the electron range is much smaller than the target thickness, the target of a transmission tube acts at the same time as the first filter of the beam (Section 3.3). In general, the attenuation of the x-ray flux by a layer of matter is described by

$$
I(E) = I_0(E)\,e^{-\mu(E)\,d}
$$ {#eq:att3}

where $I_0(E)$ is the spectrum before attenuation in the layer, $\mu(E)$ the linear attenuation coefficient for photons of energy $E$ and $d$ the thickness of the layer. The experimental data on the maximum penetration depth of electrons into the target are described most accurately by the Kanaya–Okayama expression [@kanaya1972]:

$$
R_0 = \frac{0.0276\,A\,E_0^{1.67}}{\rho\,Z^{0.889}}
$$ {#eq:ko}

where $R_0$ is in µm, $E_0$ is the energy of the primary electrons in keV, $\rho$ the density of the material in g/cm³, $A$ its atomic weight in g/mol and $Z$ its atomic number. For copper ($A$ = 63.546 g/mol, $\rho$ = 8.96 g/cm³, $Z$ = 29) at $E_0$ = 45 keV, {{REF:eq:ko}} gives $R_0$ = 5.7 µm, close to the CSDA range of 6.5 µm calculated from the Bethe formula (Section 2.2). The range increases with the electron energy as $E_0^{1.67}$ and decreases with the density of the target.

The thickness of the layer in which the radiation is attenuated is determined by the design of the anode. Structurally, anodes are made massive or transmission (shoot-through) ({{REF:f3_anodes}}). With a massive anode ({{REF:f3_anodes}}, a) the working beam of the tube can be formed within the range of angles $\varphi$ = 0–87°. Such an anode usually consists of an anode body 4 and a target 3 (composite anode). The material of the anode body must have a high thermal conductivity, since the heat is removed to the cooling device through the anode body. Most often the anode body is made of oxygen-free copper, which has a fairly high melting point (1358 K), good vacuum properties and a high heat capacity and thermal conductivity.

The target must have a high melting point and a low vapour pressure at high temperatures. In tubes intended for producing bremsstrahlung, targets are made of tungsten (melting point about 3700 K). To obtain characteristic radiation of a certain hardness, targets are made of various materials (chromium, iron, copper, molybdenum, silver, etc.). In some cases there is no separate target, and its function is performed by the surface of the anode body (homogeneous anode).

A transmission anode is used in x-ray tubes, as a rule, when the axis and direction of the working beam must coincide with those of the electron beam ({{REF:f3_anodes}}, b). Transmission anodes can also be composite or homogeneous. A composite anode includes a fairly thin substrate and a target. The substrate must transmit the working beam with low attenuation; in many cases it also serves as the output window of the tube and must then be vacuum-tight. As a rule, the substrates of transmission anodes are made of beryllium.

{{FIG:f3_anodes|Anode designs: a – massive; b – transmission (shoot-through); 1 – electron beam; 2 – x-ray radiation; 3 – target; 4 – anode body (substrate)}}

Thus, for a transmission anode the thickness of the attenuating layer is

$$
x_2 = X - x_1
$$ {#eq:x2_trans}

where $X$ is the thickness of the anode and $x_1$ the depth of electron penetration into the anode. For a massive anode the thickness of the attenuating layer is

$$
x_2 = x_1\,\frac{\cos\alpha}{\cos\beta}
$$ {#eq:x2_mass}

where $\alpha$ is the angle between the electron beam and the normal to the target surface and $\beta$ the angle between the normal and the direction in which the x-ray beam is taken off.

The two designs differ in the path of the photons in the anode material ({{REF:f3_anodes}}). In a massive anode the photons leave through the irradiated surface, and their path {{REF:eq:x2_mass}} is of the order of the electron range, i.e. a few micrometres. Self-absorption is therefore important only for soft photons and at grazing take-off angles: as $\beta \to 90°$, $\cos\beta \to 0$ and the path grows without limit, which causes the heel effect (Section 2.1). In a transmission anode the photons must cross the whole remaining thickness {{REF:eq:x2_trans}}, about 95 µm for a 100 µm copper target, so the target strongly filters the beam. Its thickness must exceed the electron range $R_0$, so that the electrons are stopped, but it should be as small as possible, so that the useful radiation is transmitted.

Having calculated the radiation spectrum of the x-ray tube, i.e. the number of photons in the radiation flux as a function of their energy, one obtains the total intensity (energy flux) of the radiation:

$$
I = \int_{0}^{E_0} N_T(E)\,E\,\mathrm{d}E + \sum_{q} N_{X_q}\,E_{X_q}
$$ {#eq:int_total}

where $N_T(E)$ is the bremsstrahlung spectrum (the number of photons per unit energy interval), $N_{X_q}$ the number of photons of the characteristic line $q$ and $E_{X_q}$ their energy.

It should be noted that with increasing tube voltage the radiation intensity increases, and not only the quantity but also the quality of the x-rays, i.e. their energy spectrum, changes. The x-ray beam has a continuous energy spectrum with a wide range of energies, and photons of different energies pass through matter differently. Photons with energies below 10 keV are practically unable to penetrate a 1-cm layer of muscle: almost all of them are absorbed by the biological tissue. In the range 10–25 keV the penetrating ability increases rapidly with energy; the transmission of 1 cm of soft tissue is 0.3 % at 10 keV, 17 % at 15 keV, 42 % at 20 keV and 57 % at 25 keV. Low-energy photons (below 20 keV) therefore create an additional radiation load on the patient and should be removed from the spectrum.

Filters made of various materials can be used for this purpose. In a filter the x-rays are attenuated unevenly: the number of low-energy photons decreases more than the number of high-energy photons. The unevenness of attenuation depends on the material and thickness of the filter, which should be chosen so that the spectrum at high energies ($E \ge$ 50 keV) changes insignificantly. Irradiation with filters is widely used in x-ray therapy and avoids radiation burns of the skin. In deep x-ray therapy, filters of copper, tin and lead 0.5–2 mm thick are used, and in superficial x-ray therapy aluminium filters 1–4 mm thick. When an x-ray tube is used as the source of photon radiation in photon activation therapy, aluminium filters are applicable, their thickness depending on the spectrum of the tube.

The selective action of filters follows from the energy dependence of photoelectric absorption, which per unit mass is roughly proportional to $Z^4/E^3$ (Section 2.7). A filter of thickness $d$ multiplies the spectrum by $e^{-\mu(E)\,d}$, which is small for soft photons and close to unity for hard ones, so the mean energy of the beam increases (beam hardening). Aluminium ($Z$ = 13), whose K-edge (1.56 keV) lies below the useful energy range, attenuates the spectrum monotonically and does not create transmission windows at absorption edges.

## 3.2. Calculation of the x-ray tube spectrum

The energy distribution of the photons (spectrum) produced in x-ray generators, or x-ray tubes, depends on the current, the voltage and the target/filter combination, and the dose in turn depends on the spectrum [@campillo2021]. Knowledge of the spectral distribution of the x-rays emitted from an x-ray tube is important for the quantitative determination of the radiation dose in medical imaging. This has motivated the development of various computational approaches for predicting the x-ray emission, including MC simulation of coupled electron–photon transport. Although the MC method is probably the most complete method available, it is impractical for specific applications because of the heavy computational load of simulating the electron transport in the x-ray target (the anode of the tube). Alternative methods that use efficient deterministic solutions for the electron penetration and the resulting x-ray production have therefore found common practical use. Kramers, and Bethe and Heitler, developed analytical x-ray emission models from the principles of classical electrodynamics and quantum mechanics, respectively. Their results were valid strictly for thin x-ray targets, since the filtration of bremsstrahlung by the overlying target layer was not considered; Kramers' model was later modified to extend its application to conventional thick-target x-ray tubes [@omar2020a].

In this work, the photon spectrum of the 45 kVp x-ray tube (BS1) with a thin anode (x-ray target) is modelled. To calculate the spectrum of the x-ray tube, the Kramers formulas were used [@kramers1923; @abdullaeva2024]: the bremsstrahlung x-ray spectrum

$$
\frac{\partial\Psi_X(E)}{\partial E} = k_1\,i_A\,Z\left(\frac{E_0}{E} - 1\right)
$$ {#eq:kr_br}

and the characteristic x-ray spectrum

$$
\Psi_{X_q} = k_2\,\frac{i_A\,G\,\gamma\,\omega_q\,p}{Z}\left(\frac{E_0}{E_q} - 1\right)^{1.67}
$$ {#eq:kr_ch}

Here $k_1$ = 8.8×10⁸ and $k_2$ = 5×10¹⁴ are constants; $Z$ is the atomic number of the chemical element of the target; $i_A$ the current of the x-ray tube; $E_0$ the energy of the electron beam, determined by the tube voltage; $E$ the photon energy; $E_q$ the ionisation energy of the $q$-shell; $\omega_q$ the fluorescence yield of the $q$-shell; $p$ the fraction of the given characteristic line in its series; $G = 1 - (7Z - 80)/(14Z - 80)$; $\gamma$ = 3.8×10⁻² for the K-series and $\gamma$ = 0.11 for the L-series of characteristic radiation. By adding the bremsstrahlung and characteristic spectra, an idealised x-ray tube spectrum is obtained, which is then attenuated in the target, the window and the filters according to {{REF:eq:att3}}.

Expression {{REF:eq:kr_br}} is Kramers' law: the number of bremsstrahlung photons per unit energy is proportional to the atomic number of the target and to the tube current, decreases with photon energy as $E_0/E - 1$ and vanishes at the maximum photon energy $E = E_0 = eU$ (the Duane–Hunt limit). The corresponding energy spectrum, $E\,\partial\Psi_X/\partial E = k_1 i_A Z (E_0 - E)$, decreases linearly from low energies to $E_0$. Characteristic radiation {{REF:eq:kr_ch}} is produced only when the electron energy exceeds the ionisation energy of the shell. For the K-series of copper ($E_K$ = 8.979 keV) at 45 kV the overvoltage ratio $E_0/E_K$ is 5.0, the factor $(E_0/E_K - 1)^{1.67}$ equals 10.2 and $G$ = 0.62. The K-shell fluorescence yield of copper is about 0.45, so more than half of the K vacancies relax by Auger emission without producing x-rays.

The Kramers formulas used in this chapter and the DM of Chapter 2 describe the same physical processes at different levels of detail. The DM follows the slowing-down of the electrons in a thick reflection target and uses the Bethe–Heitler cross-section with the Elwert factor and the Gryziński K-shell cross-section, so that it reproduces the spectral shape of SpekPy. The Kramers formulas give the bremsstrahlung and characteristic yields in closed form with empirical constants. They are convenient for a transmission tube, whose spectrum is determined mainly by the attenuation in the target and filters ({{REF:eq:att3}}). Since the DEF depends only on the spectral shape (Section 2.6), this simpler description is sufficient to show the effect of the anode self-filtration and of the added filters.

The possibility of radio-enhancement in soft tissue in the presence of heavy elements irradiated with the obtained x-ray tube spectrum is assessed by the DEF calculated as [@abdullaeva2022]

$$
\mathrm{DEF} = 1 + \frac{k_Z\int_{0}^{E_{\max}}\Psi'(E)\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_Z\mathrm{d}E}{\int_{0}^{E_{\max}}\Psi'(E)\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_{\mathrm{s.t.}}\mathrm{d}E}
$$ {#eq:def3}

Here $(\mu_{en}/\rho)_Z$ and $(\mu_{en}/\rho)_{\mathrm{s.t.}}$ are the mass energy-absorption coefficients of the element with atomic number $Z$ and of soft tissue; $k_Z$ is the mass fraction of the element $Z$ in soft tissue; $\Psi'(E)$ is the differential (spectral) energy fluence of the x-ray beam; and the limits of integration correspond to the minimum and maximum energies of the spectrum.

The ratio of the integrals in {{REF:eq:def3}} is the dose-weighted mean $\langle R\rangle$ of the ratio $R(E) = (\mu_{en}/\rho)_Z/(\mu_{en}/\rho)_{\mathrm{s.t.}}$ (cf. {{REF:eq:ratio_avg}}), so $\mathrm{DEF} = 1 + k_Z\langle R\rangle$ increases linearly with the concentration of the element, with the slope $\langle R\rangle$. Expression {{REF:eq:def3}} adds the energy absorbed by the element to that absorbed by the whole tissue mass. With the exact mixture rule {{REF:eq:mixture}}, in which the element replaces the same mass of tissue, $\mathrm{DEF} = 1 + k_Z(\langle R\rangle - 1)$ ({{REF:eq:def_lin}}). The difference, $k_Z$, does not exceed 0.05 for the concentrations considered here and is small compared with the enhancement itself.

## 3.3. DEF for biological tissue with Ag

The photon spectrum of the 45 kVp x-ray tube with the Cu target was modelled. In this study the thickness of the Cu anode is 100 µm, the thickness of the Be window 40 µm and the anode current 0.1 mA. The spectra of the x-ray tube were calculated for thin (30 µm) and thick (2.11 mm) aluminium filters ({{REF:f3_spectra}}). Without filters, the photon intensity is much higher in the energy range below 20 keV. As noted in Section 3.1, photons with energies below 10 keV cannot penetrate a 1-cm layer of muscle (almost all of them are absorbed by biological tissue), and in the range 10–25 keV the penetrating ability increases rapidly with energy. Low-energy photons (below 20 keV) therefore create an additional radiation dose to the patient and should be removed from the spectrum.

{{FIG:f3_spectra|X-ray tube spectra (45 kVp, 100 µm Cu transmission anode, 40 µm Be window, 0.1 mA) without filter and with 30 µm and 2.11 mm aluminium filters}}

The spectrum in {{REF:f3_spectra}} consists of two parts separated by a gap at about 9.5–14 keV. The narrow peak at 8–9 keV is formed by the characteristic Kα (8.05 keV) and Kβ (8.91 keV) lines of copper together with the bremsstrahlung just below the copper K-edge (8.98 keV). The broad hump between 15 and 45 keV is the bremsstrahlung continuum. Both features result from the self-filtration of the transmission anode: the photons generated in the surface layer of about 5.7 µm ({{REF:eq:ko}}) cross about 95 µm of copper ({{REF:eq:x2_trans}}). Below its K-edge copper is relatively transparent to its own lines (transmission 1.2 % at 8.05 keV and 3.6 % at 8.91 keV), and the lines are intense enough to form the highest peak of the spectrum. Above the K-edge the attenuation coefficient of copper increases 7.3-fold, and the transmission drops to 10⁻¹⁰–10⁻⁸ at 9–10 keV and about 10⁻⁵ at 12 keV, which produces the gap. With increasing energy the transmission rises to 0.2 % at 15 keV, 5.6 % at 20 keV, 39 % at 30 keV and 66 % at 40 keV, whereas the Kramers intensity {{REF:eq:kr_br}} decreases as $E_0/E - 1$ and vanishes at $E_0$ = 45 keV. The product of these opposite dependences gives the maximum of the continuum at about 27–28 keV. The 40 µm beryllium window transmits more than 99 % of the photons above 8 keV and does not change the shape of the spectrum.

With the 30 µm aluminium filter the continuum above 15 keV is practically unchanged (transmission 94–99 %), whereas the Cu K-line peak is reduced by about a quarter (transmission 67 % at 8.05 keV and 74 % at 8.91 keV). The 2.11 mm aluminium filter removes the characteristic lines completely (transmission below 10⁻⁹) and strongly attenuates the soft part of the continuum: it transmits 1.1 % of the photons at 15 keV, 14 % at 20 keV and 35 % at 25 keV, but 53 % at 30 keV and 72 % at 40 keV. As a result, the maximum of the spectrum shifts from about 27.5 keV to 30–33 keV, and most of the remaining photons have energies above the silver K-edge (25.514 keV). The beam is hardened at the cost of a lower intensity, which must be compensated by a longer exposure. The transmissions that determine the shape of the spectra are summarised in {{REF:tab:transm}}.

To understand whether radio-enhancement by x-rays with such a spectrum ({{REF:f3_spectra}}) is possible, and in the presence of which elements, the excitation energies of the K-shell should be considered first. For the elements with atomic numbers between 30 and 65, the binding energies of the K-shell lie between 10 and 50 keV ({{REF:f3_ek}}). The excitation energies of the different thresholds are all the more important the heavier the material, owing to the increase of the total photoelectric cross-section. The photoelectric effect induces above all an attenuation of the primary radiation. As shown in Sections 1.2 and 2.7, this attenuation depends very strongly on the energy of the radiation and especially on the atomic number $Z$ of the material. For photon energies below 500 keV, the introduction of a high-Z compound into the tissues makes it possible to improve the radiological contrast significantly, owing to the difference in photon absorption between these elements and the biological matter.

{{FIG:f3_ek|K-shell excitation (binding) energies of the elements with atomic numbers between 30 and 65; the horizontal line marks the maximum photon energy of the 45 kVp tube (data of [@schoonjans2011])}}

The K-shell binding energy increases approximately as $(Z - 1)^2$ (Moseley's law), from 9.66 keV for zinc ($Z$ = 30) to 52.0 keV for terbium ($Z$ = 65), because the K electrons are screened by only about one other electron and feel almost the whole nuclear charge. For the 45 kVp tube, K-shell photoabsorption is therefore possible only in elements with $Z \le 60$ (neodymium, $E_K$ = 43.57 keV). For promethium ($E_K$ = 45.18 keV) and heavier elements, including samarium ($E_K$ = 46.83 keV), the K-edge lies above the maximum photon energy, and only the L- and M-shells can be ionised. The silver K-edge (25.51 keV) lies just below the maximum of the filtered spectrum (27–33 keV), so most photons of the hardened beam can be absorbed by silver in the K-shell, where the absorption is strongest.

The variations of the total mass attenuation coefficients of the pure elements were characterised on either side of their absorption thresholds: the amplitude of the discontinuities of the total mass attenuation coefficient was evaluated for the K, L1, L2 and L3 shells ({{REF:f3_discont}}). For a given element, the absolute increase of the total mass attenuation coefficient is the larger, the lower the threshold energy, i.e. the less bound the electrons of the shell. This is mainly due to the greater value of the total mass attenuation coefficient at low energies ({{REF:f3_discont}}, a). However, to characterise the reaction probability, the ratio of the total mass attenuation coefficients just above and just below the K, L1, L2 and L3 thresholds of the various elements must be considered, and then the most tightly bound shells prove to be the most favourable ({{REF:f3_discont}}, b). Thus, for the whole set of elements, a photon with an energy a little higher than the K threshold has a probability of being absorbed 4.8 to 7.2 times higher than a photon with a slightly lower energy. This factor does not exceed 1.5 for the L1 and L2 shells and lies between 2.6 and 4.7 for the L3 shell.

{{FIG:f3_discont|Discontinuities of the total mass attenuation coefficient at the K, L1, L2 and L3 thresholds for the elements with Z = 30–65: (a) amplitude of the discontinuity (μ/ρ)E+ − (μ/ρ)E−; (b) gain (μ/ρ)E+/(μ/ρ)E− at the passing of the thresholds (data of [@nist_xraymass])}}

The absolute jumps ({{REF:f3_discont}}, a) decrease with $Z$ by more than an order of magnitude between $Z$ = 30 and 65 (at the K-edge from about 210 to 14 cm²/g), because the edges of heavier elements lie at higher energies, where the photoabsorption per unit mass is smaller. The L-edges lie at energies about 7–10 times lower than the K-edge, and their absolute jumps are correspondingly larger. The relative jump ({{REF:f3_discont}}, b) shows the share of the shell in the photoabsorption just above its edge. A K-edge jump ratio $r_K$ means that the fraction $(r_K - 1)/r_K$ of the photons absorbed just above the edge interacts with the two K electrons: 0.83 for silver ($r_K$ = 5.8) and 0.80 for samarium ($r_K$ = 5.0), in agreement with the estimate {{REF:eq:sig_ph_tot}}, by which the K-shell accounts for 80 % of the photoeffect. The ratio decreases slowly with $Z$ because the outer shells take an increasing part of the absorption. Of the L subshells, L3 with four 2p₃/₂ electrons gives the largest jump (3.3 for silver and 2.7 for samarium), whereas the jumps at L2 (two 2p₁/₂ electrons, about 1.4) and L1 (two 2s electrons, about 1.15) are small. For silver the K-edge at 25.51 keV thus gives the largest relative increase of absorption, and this edge lies within the spectrum of {{REF:f3_spectra}}. Compared with the heavy elements, the Compton effect becomes dominant at lower energies in soft tissue, which consists of light elements. This early contribution of Compton scattering changes the energy dependence of the total mass attenuation coefficient of soft tissue with respect to the heavier elements. The slowing of the decrease of the total attenuation coefficient, observed as early as 20 keV for soft tissue, is shifted to energies of the order of 500 keV for heavy materials, owing to the great predominance of the photoelectric effect at lower energies. It is therefore important to consider not only the absolute contribution of the heavy element to the photoelectric effect but also the gain in absorption compared with the biological matter. In this respect, the optimal energy to maximise the difference in absorption from soft tissue is located just above the absorption thresholds. {{REF:f3_ratio_agsm}} shows the ratio of the mass energy-absorption coefficients of Ag and Sm to that of soft tissue for photon energies from 1 to 1000 keV. Whereas the energy just above the K thresholds of these elements indeed seems optimal to strengthen the absorption of photons compared with soft tissue, this does not apply exactly to the L thresholds, since above them the ratio keeps increasing and its optimum appears at higher energies, especially for Ag. It also appears in {{REF:f3_ratio_agsm}} (a, b) that Ag and Sm reach the maximum of the ratio $(\mu_{en}/\rho)_Z/(\mu_{en}/\rho)_{\mathrm{s.t.}}$ differently near the K threshold, whereas after passing the K threshold the maxima are similar. The ratio gains more at the L thresholds of Sm owing to the higher atomic number of this element.

{{FIG:f3_ratio_agsm|Ratio of the mass energy-absorption coefficients of pure Ag (a) and Sm (b) to that of soft tissue for photon energies between 1 and 1000 keV}}

The shape of these curves follows from the different energy dependences of the energy absorption in the heavy element and in tissue. Between the absorption edges the ratio increases slowly with energy, because the photoabsorption of the light elements of tissue decreases with energy faster than that of the heavy element. At each edge the ratio jumps, but by less than the attenuation coefficient: at the K-edge the ratio increases about 2.4-fold for silver and 1.8-fold for samarium, against 5.8- and 5.0-fold jumps of $\mu/\rho$ ({{REF:f3_discont}}). The reason is that $\mu_{en}/\rho$ excludes the energy carried away by fluorescence photons: 83 % of the K vacancies in silver and 93 % in samarium are filled with emission of K x-rays (Kα of 22.2 keV for Ag and 40.1 keV for Sm), which escape from the absorption site. Above about 30–50 keV the energy-absorption coefficient of tissue decreases ever more slowly, because Compton scattering contributes an increasing part of it, while the photoabsorption of the heavy element continues to fall. The ratio therefore reaches a maximum of about 150 at 40–50 keV for Ag and about 160 near 60 keV for Sm and then decreases steeply. At 1 MeV, where Compton scattering dominates in both media, the ratio is 0.85–0.95. It tends to the ratio of the numbers of electrons per unit mass ($Z/A$ = 0.44 for Ag and 0.41 for Sm against 0.55 for soft tissue), slightly increased by the residual photoabsorption of the heavy element.

Within the 45 kVp spectrum the ratio for Ag rises from about 20 at the Cu K lines (8 keV) and about 30–35 just below 25.5 keV to 85–150 above the K-edge. For Sm it is about 40 at 8 keV, because its L-edges (6.72–7.74 keV) lie just below the Cu K lines, and increases only to about 85 at 30–40 keV, since its K-edge (46.83 keV) lies outside the spectrum. Samarium therefore absorbs the soft part of the spectrum, in particular the Cu K lines, more effectively than silver, whereas silver is more effective in the hard part above 25.5 keV.

For the calculated x-ray spectra ({{REF:f3_spectra}}), the DEF values were determined with {{REF:eq:def3}} and are presented in {{REF:f3_def}}. The DEF values grow linearly with increasing $k_Z$, the fraction of the element $Z$ in soft tissue. The use of filters for the x-ray tube spectrum can increase the DEF value. This is achieved by removing from the spectrum the energy region in which the ratio $(\mu_{en}/\rho)_Z/(\mu_{en}/\rho)_{\mathrm{s.t.}}$ is lower.

The linearity follows from {{REF:eq:def3}}: the energy absorbed by the element is proportional to its mass fraction, and the slope of each line equals the spectrum-averaged ratio $\langle R\rangle$ ({{REF:tab:def_vals}}). With the thin 30 µm filter the spectrum retains the intense Cu K lines, for which the ratio is only about 20 for silver, and the slope is about 40: the DEF for 1 % Ag is 1.40. The 2.11 mm filter removes the lines and the photons below about 20 keV and leaves mainly photons above the Ag K-edge, where the ratio is 85–150. The slope increases to about 98 and the DEF for 1 % Ag to 1.98, i.e. the enhancement $\mathrm{DEF} - 1$ increases 2.45-fold.

For samarium the effect of filtration is weaker (the slope increases from about 56 to 81), and the order of the two elements is reversed. With the thin filter Sm gives the larger DEF (1.56 against 1.40 at $k_Z$ = 0.01), because its L-edges lie just below the Cu K lines, which are absorbed by Sm about twice as strongly as by Ag. With the thick filter Ag is more effective (1.98 against 1.81), because its K-edge lies inside the hardened spectrum, whereas the K-edge of Sm (46.83 keV) lies above the maximum photon energy of 45 keV. The DEF thus depends on the position of the absorption edges of the element relative to the spectrum; for silver, filtration that concentrates the spectrum above 25.5 keV is the most effective.

Because the DEF is linear in $k_Z$, the slopes $\langle R\rangle$ in {{REF:tab:def_vals}} also give the enhancement at lower silver concentrations. With the 2.11 mm Al filter a silver mass fraction of 0.5 % gives DEF ≈ 1.49 and 0.1 % gives DEF ≈ 1.10; with the 30 µm filter the corresponding values are about 1.20 and 1.04. Hard filtration thus increases the enhancement $\mathrm{DEF} - 1$ 2.45-fold at any silver content.


## 3.4. Discussion of the results

The obtained results prove the possibility of realising the radiosensitization effect with Ag and Sm and kilovoltage x-ray tubes. Such x-ray tubes, widely used in medicine, give hope for a first step towards solving the problem of the clinical application of radiosensitizers in photon therapy.

At the same time, it is important to underline that the effects occurring after the ionisation of heavy-element atoms should be carefully analysed. The ionisation of an inner shell can trigger a multistep decay of the resulting excited state. Firstly, an atom transformed into a highly charged ion can distort and destroy chemical bonds with its electric field. Secondly, photons and electrons emitted from deeper shells at the first stage of the atomic decay can ionise neighbouring atoms, thereby giving rise to new cascade processes. All these factors should be considered when determining the absorbed dose, as well as in evaluating the relative biological effectiveness of the partial components of the absorbed dose. To solve the latter problem, the inhomogeneity of the microdistribution of the absorbed dose in the biological medium, formed when atoms of heavy elements are activated by photons, must be estimated correctly.

The atomic data of silver illustrate the scale of these processes. About 83 % of the K vacancies are filled with emission of Kα (22.2 keV) and Kβ (24.9 keV) photons, which have mean free paths of about 1.5–2 cm in soft tissue and carry their energy away from the silver atom. The remaining 17 % of the K vacancies and most of the subsequent L-shell vacancies (L3 fluorescence yield 0.05) relax by Auger transitions. The emitted Auger and photoelectrons deposit their energy at distances from nanometres to tens of micrometres from the atom, so the microscopic dose distribution is much more inhomogeneous than the macroscopic DEF suggests (Section 2.6). The calculated DEF is therefore the physical baseline of the effect, to which chemical and biological contributions of the silver compounds and nanoparticles may be added.

The calculations also have limitations that should be kept in mind. The spectra were obtained with the Kramers formulas, whose constants are empirical, and the attenuation in the anode was calculated along the beam axis for an average generation depth. The DEF was calculated in the kerma approximation for a homogeneous distribution of the element, assuming charged-particle equilibrium and neglecting scattered radiation and the reabsorption of fluorescence photons in the sample. These assumptions are adequate for comparing filters and elements, since the comparison depends on the spectral shape. However, the absolute DEF of a particular sample, especially at high concentrations and in thick samples, where beam hardening and self-shielding occur (Section 2.6), should be verified by Monte Carlo calculations and dosimetric measurements.

## 3.5. Conclusions to Chapter 3

1. The spectrum of a 45 kVp tube with a 100 µm Cu transmission anode and a 40 µm Be window, calculated with the Kramers formulas and attenuation in the anode, window and filters, consists of the Cu K lines (8.05 and 8.91 keV) and a bremsstrahlung continuum at 15–45 keV with a maximum at about 27–28 keV. The self-filtration of the anode (about 95 µm of Cu) suppresses the photons between the Cu K-edge (8.98 keV) and about 14 keV.
2. The 30 µm Al filter practically does not change the spectrum above 15 keV, whereas the 2.11 mm Al filter removes the Cu K lines and the photons below about 15–20 keV and shifts the maximum to 30–33 keV, concentrating the spectrum above the Ag K-edge at the cost of a lower intensity.
3. The K-shell binding energies of the elements with $Z$ = 30–65 lie between 9.7 and 52 keV, and for the 45 kVp tube K-shell absorption is possible only for $Z \le 60$. The relative jump of the mass attenuation coefficient is 4.8–7.2 at the K-edge (5.8 for silver), 2.6–4.7 at the L3 edge and below 1.5 at the L1 and L2 edges.
4. The ratio of the mass energy-absorption coefficients of Ag and soft tissue rises from about 20–35 between 8 and 25.5 keV to 85–150 above the K-edge, with a maximum at 40–50 keV. For Sm it is about 40–85 within the 45 kVp spectrum, since its K-edge (46.83 keV) lies above the maximum photon energy.
5. The DEF grows linearly with the mass fraction $k_Z$ of the element. With the 2.11 mm Al filter the DEF of tissue containing 1 % Ag increases from 1.40 to 1.98 (5.88 at $k_Z$ = 0.05). With the thin filter Sm is more effective than Ag (1.56 against 1.40 at $k_Z$ = 0.01), and with the thick filter Ag is more effective (1.98 against 1.81). Filtration that removes the photons below the Ag K-edge is therefore the main means of increasing the dose enhancement by silver.
