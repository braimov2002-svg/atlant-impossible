# CHAPTER 2. PHYSICAL MODEL OF THE X-RAY SPECTRUM AND METHODS OF DOSE-ENHANCEMENT CALCULATION

This chapter describes the semi-analytical x-ray spectrum model of this work, the developed model (DM), and the methods used to calculate the dose enhancement factor (DEF). Sections 2.1–2.5 follow the radiation from the tube to the sample; Sections 2.6–2.9 treat the DEF, its Monte Carlo (MC) verification, the data and software, and radiation safety. All formulas are given as implemented in the code of the DM (Appendix A), with energies in keV and lengths in cm unless stated otherwise.

## 2.1. Design and technical parameters of an x-ray tube with a copper anode

In an x-ray tube, electrons emitted thermionically by the heated cathode filament are shaped by a focusing cup into a beam that strikes a small area of the anode, the focal spot [@bushberg2012]. Accelerated by the tube voltage $U$, they reach the anode with the kinetic energy $T_0 = eU$, where $e$ is the elementary charge; in the baseline configuration $T_0$ = 45 keV. Most of this energy is converted into heat, and only a small fraction is emitted as bremsstrahlung and characteristic x-rays [@podgorsak2016]. The number of emitted photons is proportional to the product of the tube current and the exposure time (mAs), whereas the spectral shape does not depend on the current, so all spectra in this work are normalised to 1 mAs.

In the reflection-target tube considered here, the useful beam leaves the anode through the face on which the electrons are incident ({{REF:fig01_tube}}), and the anode is thick enough to stop every electron. The anode angle $\theta$ is the angle between the anode surface and the central ray; since the electron beam is perpendicular to the central ray, the electrons strike the anode at $\theta$ to the surface normal, and the photons on the central ray leave the surface at the take-off angle $\theta$. A small anode angle reduces the effective focal spot (line-focus principle), but photons emitted towards the anode side of the field travel longer paths in the anode, so that the beam there is weaker and harder (heel effect) [@bushberg2012]. The DM calculates the spectrum on the central ray, where $\theta$ enters through the electron penetration and the photon self-absorption (Sections 2.2 and 2.3). The photons leave the envelope through a thin beryllium window, which attenuates soft x-rays weakly while remaining strong and vacuum-tight and which, together with the self-absorption in the anode, forms the inherent filtration. Added filters, metal foils outside the window, remove the low-energy part of the spectrum and harden the beam [@khan2014]. The photons then cross an air gap to the sample at the focus–sample distance $r$ (labelled d in {{REF:fig01_tube}}; $d$ denotes the electron depth of Section 2.2).

{{FIG:fig01_tube|Schematic diagram of the modelled reflection-target x-ray tube with a copper anode and of the irradiation geometry: photons emitted along the central ray at the anode angle θ pass through the beryllium window, the added filter and the air gap to the sample of tissue containing Ag. The focus–sample distance, marked d in the figure, is denoted r in the text.}}

Copper-anode tubes are standard sources for x-ray diffraction (XRD), which uses the intense Cu Kα line (wavelength 0.154 nm) and suppresses the Kβ line with a thin nickel filter whose K absorption edge lies between the two lines [@cullity2001]. For irradiations with silver, however, the most useful photons lie above the Ag K-edge at 25.514 keV (Section 1.4), in the bremsstrahlung continuum that is merely a background in diffraction. The whole spectrum must therefore be known and, as shown in Chapter 3, shaped by filtration; since most published spectrum models were developed for tungsten anodes (Section 1.5), a dedicated model of the copper-anode tube is required. The design and technical parameters of the modelled tube, with the baseline values and the ranges studied in Chapter 3, are listed in {{REF:t2_1}}. The tube voltage, anode material, anode angle, window and added filters change the shape of the spectrum, whereas the distance acts through the inverse-square law and the air attenuation. Under a constant potential all electrons reach the anode with the same energy $T_0$; a voltage ripple would produce a weighted superposition of such spectra.

## 2.2. Electron slowing-down in the anode

Electrons of a few tens of keV lose energy in the anode mainly by ionisation and excitation of the atoms, while elastic scattering by the nuclei changes their direction; radiative losses are small and are neglected. In the continuous-slowing-down approximation (CSDA) used in the DM, the mass collision stopping power is given by the Bethe formula [@bethe1930] in the form of ICRU Report 37 [@icru37]:

$$
\frac{S(T)}{\rho} = \frac{\kappa}{2}\,\frac{Z}{A}\,\frac{1}{\beta^{2}}\left[\ln\frac{\tau^{2}(\tau + 2)}{2\,(I/m_ec^{2})^{2}} + F^{-}(\tau)\right]
$$ {#eq:bethe}

where $S(T)$ is the linear collision stopping power for an electron with kinetic energy $T$, $\rho$ is the density of the anode, $\kappa = 4\pi N_A r_e^{2} m_ec^{2} = 0.307075$ MeV cm²/mol, $N_A$ is the Avogadro constant, $r_e$ is the classical electron radius, $m_ec^{2}$ = 510.999 keV is the electron rest energy, $Z$ and $A$ are the atomic number and the molar mass (g/mol) of the anode material, $\tau = T/m_ec^{2}$, $\beta$ is the electron velocity in units of the speed of light, $\beta^{2} = 1 - (1 + \tau)^{-2}$, $I$ is the mean excitation energy, and $F^{-}(\tau)$ is the electron term derived from the Møller cross-section:

$$
F^{-}(\tau) = 1 - \beta^{2} + \frac{\tau^{2}/8 - (2\tau + 1)\ln 2}{(\tau + 1)^{2}}
$$ {#eq:fminus}

For copper $I$ = 322 eV is used; the density-effect correction, negligible below 100 keV, and the shell corrections, which affect only the lowest electron energies, are omitted. As a check, the same code gives 6.603 MeV cm²/g for water at 50 keV, identical to the NIST ESTAR value [@nist_estar]. The path length travelled by an electron while slowing down from $T_0$ to $T$ is

$$
s(T) = \int_{T}^{T_0} \frac{\mathrm{d}T'}{S(T')}
$$ {#eq:csda}

where $T'$ is the variable of integration; the integral is evaluated by the trapezoidal rule with a step of 0.02 keV from $T_{\min}$ = 1 keV to $T_0$. The path $s(T_{\min})$ is practically the CSDA range, 6.48 µm for 45 keV electrons in copper. If the logarithm in {{REF:eq:bethe}} is regarded as constant and $\beta^{2} \approx 2T/m_ec^{2}$, then $S \propto 1/T$ and {{REF:eq:csda}} gives $T_0^{2} - T^{2} \propto s$, the empirical Thomson–Whiddington law [@whiddington1912].

Multiple elastic scattering makes the trajectories strongly curved: the electrons diffuse into the target and many are backscattered, so the CSDA path is not the depth at which the photons are produced. Instead of the MC-derived penetration distributions of Poludniowski and Evans [@poludniowski2007a], the DM uses one mean depth for each electron energy, the projected depth

$$
d(T) = \eta\, s(T)\cos\theta
$$ {#eq:depth}

where $d(T)$ is the depth below the anode surface, measured along the normal, at which the electron has slowed down to the energy $T$, $\eta$ is the electron depth factor ($\eta = 1$ corresponds to straight-line motion, $\eta < 1$ accounts for diffusion), and $\theta$ is the anode angle, equal to the angle of incidence measured from the surface normal. This single adjustable parameter of the DM was fitted to the bremsstrahlung shape calculated with SpekPy [@poludniowski2021] (Section 2.8), giving $\eta$ = 0.40 and a maximum projected depth of 2.54 µm for 45 keV electrons at $\theta$ = 12°. Electron backscattering, which concerns about 30 % of the electrons in copper, is not modelled; this contributes to the overestimate of the absolute output (Section 3.1).

Panel (a) of {{REF:fig04_electron}} shows the stopping power and the projected depth for the baseline conditions. Because the stopping power rises steeply as the electron slows down, the depth grows rapidly as the energy falls from 45 to about 20 keV and then approaches its maximum slowly. Photons with energies close to $T_0$ are therefore emitted only near the surface, whereas soft photons are produced along the whole path, including its deepest part, and are the most affected by self-absorption in the anode. Panel (b) is discussed in Section 2.4.

## 2.3. Bremsstrahlung production in a thick target

The classical theory of the continuous spectrum proposed by Kramers [@kramers1923] leads to the cross-section implemented in the DM as

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\mathrm{Kr}}}{\mathrm{d}E} = \frac{16\pi}{3\sqrt{3}}\,\frac{\alpha Z^{2} r_e^{2}}{\beta_0^{2}\,E}
$$ {#eq:kramers}

where $\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\mathrm{Kr}}/\mathrm{d}E$ is the cross-section per atom per unit photon energy (cm²/keV) for $E < T$ and zero otherwise, $\alpha$ is the fine-structure constant, and $\beta_0$ is the velocity of the electron with energy $T$ in units of the speed of light. With $\beta_0^{2} \approx 2T/m_ec^{2}$ and $S \propto 1/T$, integration over the slowing-down gives a number spectrum proportional to $Z(T_0 - E)/E$, i.e. an energy spectrum falling linearly to zero at $T_0$; this is Kramers' law, the starting point of the semi-empirical models (Section 1.5). The DM keeps the Kramers cross-section only as an option and by default uses the non-relativistic, unscreened Born-approximation cross-section of Bethe and Heitler [@bethe1934], as compiled by Koch and Motz [@koch1959]:

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\mathrm{B}}}{\mathrm{d}E} = \frac{16}{3}\,\frac{\alpha Z^{2} r_e^{2}}{\beta_0^{2}\,E}\,\ln\frac{p_0 + p}{p_0 - p}
$$ {#eq:bhnr}

where $p_0$ and $p$ are the electron momenta before and after the emission, $p_0c = \sqrt{T(T + 2m_ec^{2})}$ and $pc = \sqrt{(T - E)(T - E + 2m_ec^{2})}$; in the non-relativistic limit the factor $1/\beta_0^{2}$ equals the factor $1/p_0^{2}$ of the original formula with momenta in units of $m_ec$. The Born approximation requires $2\pi Z\alpha/\beta_0 \ll 1$ and $2\pi Z\alpha/\beta \ll 1$, where $\beta$ is the electron velocity after the emission (kinetic energy $T - E$) in units of the speed of light [@koch1959]. For copper ($Z\alpha \approx 0.21$, $\beta_0 \le 0.4$) the parameter $2\pi Z\alpha/\beta_0$ exceeds 3 even for a 45 keV electron. The resulting error is partly corrected by the Elwert factor, which approximately accounts for the Coulomb distortion of the electron wave functions [@elwert1939]:

$$
f_E = \frac{\beta_0}{\beta}\,\frac{1 - \exp(-2\pi Z\alpha/\beta_0)}{1 - \exp(-2\pi Z\alpha/\beta)}
$$ {#eq:elwert}

With $\beta_0$ and $\beta$ as defined above, the DM uses the product $\mathrm{d}\sigma/\mathrm{d}E = f_E\,\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\mathrm{B}}/\mathrm{d}E$, designated BH-E. As $E$ approaches $T$, the momentum $p$ tends to zero and the Born cross-section vanishes, while $f_E$ grows as $1/\beta$, so the BH-E cross-section remains finite at the high-energy end of the spectrum. The tabulated cross-sections of Seltzer and Berger [@seltzer1986] include atomic screening and electron–electron bremsstrahlung, which BH-E neglects. These simplifications contribute to the overestimate of the absolute yield of hard bremsstrahlung relative to SpekPy, whereas the DEF depends only on the spectral shape, which is reproduced at 45 kV with a root-mean-square deviation of 2.46 % after the fit of $\eta$ (Section 3.1).

In a thick target the emitted spectrum is the sum of the thin-target spectra produced along the slowing-down path. In the CSDA the electron passes $n_a\,\mathrm{d}T/S(T)$ atoms per unit area while its energy decreases by $\mathrm{d}T$, and a photon produced at the depth $d(T)$ is attenuated along the exit path $d(T)/\sin\theta$. Assuming isotropic emission, the number of bremsstrahlung photons per unit energy interval, per steradian and per incident electron emitted along the central ray is

$$
N_b(E) = \frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{E}^{T_0} \frac{n_a}{S(T)}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma}{\mathrm{d}E}(T, E)\,\exp\left[-\frac{\mu(E)\,d(T)}{\sin\theta}\right]\mathrm{d}T
$$ {#eq:thick}

where $n_a = \rho N_A/A$ is the number of atoms per unit volume of the anode, $\mathrm{d}\sigma/\mathrm{d}E$ is the BH-E cross-section, and $\mu(E)$ is the linear attenuation coefficient of the anode material from the xraylib library [@schoonjans2011]. Without the exponential factor and the factor $1/4\pi$, the same integral gives the generated spectrum $N_g(E)$, i.e. the photons produced in all directions, which is used in Section 2.4. The integrals are evaluated by the trapezoidal rule, with photon bins of 0.05 keV from 1 keV to $T_0$; a similar formulation with MC-derived electron penetration data underlies SpekCalc [@poludniowski2007a; @poludniowski2007b]. The exponential factor describes the self-absorption in the anode, which grows as the anode angle decreases and is strongest for soft photons and for photons just above the copper K-edge, where $\mu$ increases abruptly. Although the bremsstrahlung from a single interaction is anisotropic, multiple scattering in a thick target randomises the electron directions and smooths the angular distribution; the isotropic approximation, which more detailed models avoid [@omar2020a], nevertheless remains one of the limitations of the DM (Section 3.11).

## 2.4. Characteristic radiation

An electron with an energy above the K-shell binding energy $E_K$ (8.979 keV for copper) can ionise the K shell. The vacancy is filled from an outer shell, and the transition energy is emitted either as a characteristic photon, with the probability given by the fluorescence yield $\omega_K$ (0.454 for copper), or as an Auger electron [@bambynek1972; @krause1979]. The DM includes the four strongest lines, K$\alpha_1$ (K–L$_3$ transition, 8.048 keV), K$\alpha_2$ (K–L$_2$, 8.028 keV), K$\beta_1$ (K–M$_3$) and K$\beta_3$ (K–M$_2$), the last two at 8.905 keV, with relative intensities $p_j$ (radiative rates normalised over the four lines) of 0.583, 0.299, 0.078 and 0.040. K vacancies are produced directly by electron impact and indirectly by the absorption of bremsstrahlung photons above $E_K$; the copper L lines (below 1 keV) are absorbed in the beryllium window and are not modelled. The K-shell ionisation cross-section is calculated with the binary-encounter formula of Gryziński [@gryzinski1965]:

$$
\sigma_K(T) = \frac{\pi e^{4} n_K}{E_K^{2}}\,\frac{1}{U_K}\left(\frac{U_K - 1}{U_K + 1}\right)^{3/2}\left[1 + \frac{2}{3}\left(1 - \frac{1}{2U_K}\right)\ln\left(2.7 + \sqrt{U_K - 1}\right)\right]
$$ {#eq:gryz}

where $U_K = T/E_K$ is the overvoltage ratio ($\sigma_K = 0$ for $U_K \le 1$), $n_K = 2$ is the number of K-shell electrons, and $\pi e^{4} = 6.5141\times10^{-20}$ cm² keV² in Gaussian units. For copper the cross-section has a broad maximum of about 351 b near 35 keV (panel (b) of {{REF:fig04_electron}}), and integration of $n_a\sigma_K/S$ over the slowing-down gives 0.0159 K ionisations per incident 45 keV electron. Experimental and theoretical cross-sections have been compiled by Llovet et al. [@llovet2014], and the distorted-wave Born calculations of Bote and Salvat are used in the PENELOPE code [@bote2008]. Because the Gryziński formula rises too slowly near the threshold, the DM underestimates the Cu K-line intensity by about 25–43 % relative to SpekPy, whose model of K and L emission [@omar2018] relies on cross-sections of this distorted-wave type; the deficit is largest at low tube voltages (Section 3.1).

The edge and line energies, fluorescence yields and radiative rates are taken from xraylib [@schoonjans2011]; fluorescence yields were compiled by Krause [@krause1979] and evaluated transition energies by Deslattes et al. [@deslattes2003]. The data for the anode, the window, the main filter materials and several high-Z radiosensitizers are collected in {{REF:t2_2}}. Filters of molybdenum, silver and tin, whose K-edges lie close to the Ag K-edge, strongly absorb part of the photons that are most effective for dose enhancement by silver (Section 3.4).

{{TABLE:t2_2}}

The direct yield of line $j$ along the central ray, per steradian and per incident electron, is

$$
N_j^{\mathrm{dir}} = \frac{\omega_K\,p_j}{4\pi}\int_{E_K}^{T_0} \frac{n_a\,\sigma_K(T)}{S(T)}\,\exp\left[-\frac{\mu_j\,d(T)}{\sin\theta}\right]\mathrm{d}T
$$ {#eq:charK}

where $\mu_j = \mu(E_j)$ is the linear attenuation coefficient of the anode at the line energy $E_j$, and the other symbols are as in {{REF:eq:thick}}. Because the K lines lie below the copper K-edge, the anode is relatively transparent to its own lines, which cannot themselves ionise the K shell.

Indirect fluorescence follows the K-shell photoabsorption of bremsstrahlung photons with $E > E_K$. Since the electrons penetrate only a few micrometres, less than the attenuation lengths of photons above the K-edge, the bremsstrahlung is represented by a point source on the anode surface (the surface-source approximation), from which half of the isotropically emitted photons enter the anode. Let $u = \cos\vartheta$, where $\vartheta$ is the angle between the photon direction and the inward normal; $u$ has the probability density 1/2 on $-1 \le u \le 1$. A photon with $u > 0$ interacts between the depths $z$ and $z + \mathrm{d}z$ with the probability $(\mu/u)\exp(-\mu z/u)\,\mathrm{d}z$, where $\mu = \mu(E)$, and averaging over the directions gives the probability density of the interaction depth per emitted photon:

$$
P(z) = \frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1} \frac{\mu}{u}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mu z/u}\,\mathrm{d}u = \frac{\mu}{2}\,\mathrm{E}_1(\mu z)
$$ {#eq:absdepth}

where the substitution $t = 1/u$ has been made and $\mathrm{E}_1(x) = \int_{1}^{\infty} t^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{-xt}\,\mathrm{d}t$ is the exponential integral. Since $\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{E}_1(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = 1$, $P(z)$ integrates to 1/2: every photon entering the semi-infinite anode interacts in it. A fraction $\tau_K(E)/\mu(E)$ of these interactions are K-shell photoabsorptions, where $\tau_K(E)$ is the linear K-shell photoelectric coefficient of copper (the density multiplied by the partial K-shell photoionisation mass coefficient from xraylib) and $\mu(E)$ is the total linear attenuation coefficient. Each vacancy produces a photon of line $j$ with the probability $\omega_K p_j$, and this photon, emitted from the depth $z$ along the central ray, is attenuated by the factor $\exp(-b_j z)$, where $b_j = \mu_j/\sin\theta$. The yield of line $j$ per steradian along the central ray per generated bremsstrahlung photon of energy $E$ is therefore

$$
Y_j(E) = \frac{\omega_K\,p_j}{4\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty} \frac{\tau_K(E)}{\mu(E)}\,P(z)\,\mathrm{e}^{-b_j z}\,\mathrm{d}z = \frac{\omega_K\,p_j}{4\pi}\,\frac{\tau_K(E)}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty} \mathrm{E}_1(\mu z)\,\mathrm{e}^{-b_j z}\,\mathrm{d}z
$$ {#eq:indyield}

The remaining integral is a Laplace transform of the exponential integral: interchanging the order of integration gives $\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{E}_1(x)\,\mathrm{e}^{-sx}\,\mathrm{d}x = \int_{1}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t/[t(t + s)] = \ln(1 + s)/s$, and the substitution $x = \mu z$ with $s = b_j/\mu$ yields

$$
\int_{0}^{\infty} \mathrm{E}_1(\mu z)\,\mathrm{e}^{-b_j z}\,\mathrm{d}z = \frac{1}{b_j}\ln\left(1 + \frac{b_j}{\mu}\right)
$$ {#eq:laplace}

Weighting $Y_j(E)$ with the generated spectrum $N_g(E)$ of Section 2.3 and integrating over the photon energies above the K-edge gives the indirect yield per steradian and per incident electron:

$$
N_j^{\mathrm{ind}} = \frac{\omega_K\,p_j}{4\pi}\int_{E_K}^{T_0} N_g(E)\,\frac{\tau_K(E)}{2\,b_j}\,\ln\left[1 + \frac{b_j}{\mu(E)}\right]\mathrm{d}E
$$ {#eq:indirect}

The total yield of line $j$ is $N_j = N_j^{\mathrm{dir}} + N_j^{\mathrm{ind}}$. For the baseline tube the indirect contribution is 4.5 % of the Kα intensity and 4.8 % of the Kβ intensity; the share is slightly larger for Kβ because the indirect vacancies lie on average deeper than the direct ones and Kβ photons are attenuated less in the anode. Because the indirect contribution is small, the surface-source approximation and the neglect of scattered photons have only a minor effect on the line intensities.

## 2.5. Filtration, beam-quality parameters and air kerma

The attenuation in the beryllium window, the added filters and the air is described by the Beer–Lambert law for narrow-beam geometry, in which scattered photons are assumed not to reach the point of interest:

$$
\mathcal{T}(E) = \exp\left[-\mu_{\mathrm{Be}}(E)\,t_{\mathrm{Be}} - \sum_{i}\mu_i(E)\,t_i - \mu_{\mathrm{air}}(E)\,r\right]
$$ {#eq:filter}

where $\mathcal{T}(E)$ is the transmission at the photon energy $E$, $\mu_{\mathrm{Be}}$, $\mu_i$ and $\mu_{\mathrm{air}}$ are the linear attenuation coefficients of beryllium, of the $i$-th added filter and of air, $t_{\mathrm{Be}}$ and $t_i$ are the thicknesses of the window and of the filters, and $r$ is the focus–sample distance. For the elements, the total mass attenuation coefficients from xraylib, which include coherent scattering, are multiplied by the densities given in {{REF:t2_2}}; for dry air, the coefficients follow from the mixture rule with the NIST data for C, N, O and Ar [@nist_xraymass] and the density $1.205\times10^{-3}$ g/cm³. Treating the whole distance as an air path slightly overestimates the air attenuation. The spectral photon fluence at the sample combines the yields of Sections 2.3 and 2.4 with the transmission and the inverse-square law:

$$
\Phi(E) = \frac{n_e\,Q}{r^{2}}\,\mathcal{T}(E)\left[N_b(E) + \sum_{j} N_j\,\delta(E - E_j)\right]
$$ {#eq:fluence}

where $\Phi(E)$ is the number of photons per unit area and per unit energy interval (cm$^{-2}$ keV$^{-1}$), $Q$ is the exposure in mAs, $n_e = 6.242\times10^{15}$ is the number of electrons per mAs, $N_j$ is the total yield of line $j$, and $\delta$ is the Dirac delta function; the factor $1/r^{2}$ converts the yield per steradian of a point source into the fluence at the distance $r$. In the numerical spectrum each delta function is replaced by $N_j/\Delta E$ in the bin of width $\Delta E$ = 0.05 keV that contains $E_j$. The spectral energy fluence is $\Psi(E) = E\,\Phi(E)$, and the output of the tube is characterised by the air kerma [@attix1986; @icru85]:

$$
K_{\mathrm{air}} = \int_{0}^{T_0} \Phi(E)\,E\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_{\mathrm{air}}\mathrm{d}E
$$ {#eq:kerma}

where $(\mu_{en}/\rho)_{\mathrm{air}}$ is the mass energy-absorption coefficient of dry air, obtained in the same way, and 1 keV/g = $1.602\times10^{-13}$ Gy. Strictly, {{REF:eq:kerma}} gives the collision kerma, which for kilovoltage photons in air practically equals the total kerma, because the radiative losses of the secondary electrons are negligible [@attix1986]. With the coefficient of ICRU-44 soft tissue [@icru44] instead of air, the same expression gives the tissue kerma at the sample surface without backscatter, which is used in Section 3.6 to estimate exposure times. For the SpekPy spectra, which are filtered with the attenuation data of SpekPy itself, the air kerma calculated by SpekPy is used, whereas the half-value layers (HVL) and mean energies of all copper-anode spectra are evaluated with the same routines for both models.

The quality of kilovoltage beams is specified primarily by the first HVL, the thickness of a reference absorber, usually aluminium or copper, that reduces the air kerma of a narrow beam to one half [@ma2001; @iaea_trs398; @khan2014]; standard radiation qualities of diagnostic beams are defined in the same way [@iec61267]. The first HVL in aluminium, HVL1 = $x_1$, is found from the condition

$$
\int_{0}^{T_0} \Phi(E)\,E\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_{\mathrm{air}}\exp\left[-\mu_{\mathrm{Al}}(E)\,x_1\right]\mathrm{d}E = \frac{1}{2}\,K_{\mathrm{air}}
$$ {#eq:hvl}

where $\mu_{\mathrm{Al}}(E)$ is the linear attenuation coefficient of aluminium. The thickness $x_2$ that reduces the air kerma to one quarter is found in the same way, and HVL2 = $x_2 - x_1$; both equations are solved by bisection. The homogeneity coefficient $h$ = HVL1/HVL2 equals unity for a monoenergetic beam and is normally smaller for a polyenergetic one, because the first layers of the absorber harden the beam. The effective energy $E_{\mathrm{eff}}$, the energy of the monoenergetic beam with the same HVL1, is found from $\mu_{\mathrm{Al}}(E_{\mathrm{eff}}) = \ln 2/\mathrm{HVL1}$ by Brent's method. The mean energy of the photon fluence spectrum is

$$
\bar{E} = \frac{\int_{0}^{T_0} E\,\Phi(E)\,\mathrm{d}E}{\int_{0}^{T_0} \Phi(E)\,\mathrm{d}E}
$$ {#eq:emean}

where both integrals include the characteristic lines. For dose enhancement by silver, the fraction of photons above the Ag K-edge, $f_{\mathrm{Ag}} = \int_{E_{K,\mathrm{Ag}}}^{T_0}\Phi(E)\,\mathrm{d}E \big/ \int_{0}^{T_0}\Phi(E)\,\mathrm{d}E$ with $E_{K,\mathrm{Ag}}$ = 25.514 keV, and the corresponding energy-fluence fraction are also evaluated; they describe the part of the spectrum in which silver absorbs most strongly relative to tissue (Sections 1.2 and 1.3). Like the DEF, the HVLs and the mean energy depend only on the shape of the spectrum and are insensitive to errors in the absolute output.
