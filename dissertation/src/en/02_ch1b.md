## 1.4. Silver and silver nanoparticles as radiosensitizers

Silver ($Z = 47$) lies between the light elements of soft tissue and the heavy elements, such as gold ($Z = 79$), that dominate the literature on high-Z radiosensitizers. Reviews cover the synthesis, properties and therapeutic applications of silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) [@wei2015] and their antimicrobial and anticancer uses [@burdusel2018]; AgNPs are cheaper than gold and can be made by green synthesis, for example in egg white [@lu2012]. For radiotherapy, two properties of silver matter: its biological activity and the position of its K-edge within kilovoltage tube spectra. The former is linked to toxicity: AgNPs are cytotoxic and genotoxic in human cells, inducing ROS, DNA damage and cell-cycle arrest with mitochondrial involvement [@asharani2009]. Such effects belong to the chemical and biological stages of radio-enhancement (Section 1.3) [@kuncic2018; @rosa2017], so AgNPs may sensitize cells beyond their physical dose enhancement. Their cytotoxicity, however, makes selective tumour accumulation a prerequisite of therapy, and the effect of dose enhancement on healthy tissues must be assessed [@caglar2024].

Experimental evidence is summarised in {{REF:tab:lit_ag}}: since the early report by Xu et al. [@xu2009], glioma has been the most studied model [@liu2013; @liu2016; @liu2018; @zhao2019], including radioresistant hypoxic cells [@hall2018]; breast [@swanner2015] and colorectal [@habiba2019] cancers have also been used. The studies differ in particles, coatings, biological models and irradiation conditions and are not quantitatively comparable. Because the physical dose enhancement depends strongly on the spectrum (Section 1.3), an enhancement ratio reported without the spectrum cannot be split into physical and non-physical parts.

The reported advantage of AgNPs over AuNPs [@liu2016] is instructive. Per unit mass, silver and gold absorb photon energy comparably below about 12 keV, but gold absorbs more strongly between about 12 and 45 keV ({{REF:fig03_ratio}}), and in megavoltage beams the macroscopic enhancement is much smaller even for gold [@cho2005] because Compton scattering, whose mass coefficient depends only weakly on $Z$, dominates [@podgorsak2016]. A larger macroscopic physical dose enhancement is thus unlikely to explain the advantage of silver, either below 45 keV or at megavoltage energies. This points to substantial contributions from the chemical and biological activity of silver [@asharani2009] and from nanoscale energy deposition [@mcmahon2011]; confirming this requires quantifying the physical part for a well-defined beam.

Dosimetric studies of silver are fewer than biological ones: MC simulations addressed AgNPs in a brain tumour during brachytherapy [@taha2019], surrounding healthy tissues [@caglar2024] and silver versus gold in layered skin [@esitmez2026]; dose enhancement was also measured in AgNP-enriched dose gels [@merkis2023], and its depth dependence in orthovoltage beams was studied for nanoparticles [@chow2023]. A systematic analysis of how kilovoltage tube design and filtration determine the DEF of silver-loaded tissue has not been found in the literature reviewed.

The choice of beam follows from the atomic data of silver ({{REF:t2_2}}): K-edge 25.514 keV, L3 edge 3.351 keV and Kα1 line 22.163 keV. With $\omega_K = 0.831$, only about 17 % of K vacancies relax by Auger transitions that deposit their energy locally [@krause1979; @bambynek1972]; the other 83 % emit mainly Kα photons that carry their energy away (Section 1.2). Nevertheless, the silver-to-tissue ratio of $\mu_{en}/\rho$ is 76–139 above the edge, against 21–32 between 8 keV and the edge (Sections 1.2 and 1.3). Unlike the gold K-edge (80.725 keV), usable only above about 81 kV, the silver K-edge lies inside the spectrum of any tube operated above about 26 kV. The most favourable spectra therefore carry much of their energy fluence between the Ag K-edge and the end-point energy (45 keV here), far above the Cu K lines at 8.0–8.9 keV that dominate the photon fluence of the unfiltered copper-anode beam.

## 1.5. Methods of modelling x-ray tube spectra

Measuring the spectrum needed for the DEF with semiconductor detectors [@birch1979] requires detector-response corrections and is impractical for every configuration, so parametric studies use calculated spectra; four groups of methods are compared in {{REF:tab:methods}}. The empirical model TASMIP (tungsten anode spectral model using interpolating polynomials) interpolates measured tungsten-anode spectra from 30 to 140 kV [@boone1997], and the spektr 3.0 tool implements a similar interpolation model [@punnoose2016]. Such models are tied to the anode material, angle and inherent filtration of the measured tubes: added filtration can be modelled as attenuation, but the anode material and angle, studied in this work, cannot.

Semi-empirical models go back to Kramers' theory, in which the energy emitted from a thick target per unit photon-energy interval is proportional to $Z(T_0 - E)$, where $Z$ is the target atomic number, $T_0$ the initial electron kinetic energy and $E$ the photon energy [@kramers1923; @podgorsak2016]. Birch and Marshall compared their model with Ge(Li) detector measurements [@birch1979], and Tucker, Barnes and Chakraborty proposed the TBC model for tungsten [@tucker1991]. Such models combine a simplified bremsstrahlung cross-section with approximate electron penetration and target self-absorption; they show the dependence on voltage, anode angle and filtration explicitly, but their fitted parameters are target-specific, usually for tungsten.

Physics-based deterministic models replace most empirical parameters with theoretical or MC-derived data. MC-derived electron-penetration characteristics [@poludniowski2007a] were used to calculate x-ray production and filtration in the target [@poludniowski2007b] and implemented for tungsten in SpekCalc [@poludniowski2009]. SpekPy v2, its successor, offers several physics models, including the casim model used here, and several anodes, including copper [@poludniowski2021], available for 10–50 kV in version 2.5.4 [@spekpy_soft]. It builds on models of K and L emission [@omar2018] and of bremsstrahlung energy and angular distributions [@omar2020a], validated from 20 to 300 kV [@omar2020b] and against measured spectra and HVLs [@bujila2020].

MC simulation of coupled electron–photon transport in the tube gives the most complete description, for example MCNP4C for diagnostic and mammography spectra [@ay2004] or EGS4/BEAM for kilovoltage radiotherapy units [@verhaegen1999]; other general-purpose codes are EGSnrc [@kawrakow2000], Geant4 [@agostinelli2003] and PENELOPE [@salvat2019]. It includes electron backscattering, the bremsstrahlung angular distribution and the real tube geometry, but is slow and needs detailed geometric data, so it suits benchmarks rather than extensive parametric studies.

| Approach | Examples | Advantages | Limitations |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Empirical | TASMIP [@boone1997]; spektr 3.0 [@punnoose2016] | Fast; accurate within the data range | Fixed anode material, angle and filtration |
| Semi-empirical | Kramers [@kramers1923]; Birch–Marshall [@birch1979]; TBC [@tucker1991] | Simple; explicit parameter dependence | Simplified physics; fitted parameters, mostly tungsten |
| Physics-based deterministic | SpekCalc [@poludniowski2009]; SpekPy v2 [@poludniowski2021]; developed model (this work) | Fast; physically founded; few parameters | Approximate electron transport; limited anodes and voltages |

In kilovoltage dosimetry, beam quality is specified by the HVL together with the tube voltage [@hill2014], as in the protocol of the American Association of Physicists in Medicine for 40–300 kV beams (AAPM TG-61) [@ma2001], the TRS-398 code of practice of the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) [@iaea_trs398] and the standard radiation qualities of the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC 61267) [@iec61267]. The HVL is thus the natural quantity for comparing models and measurements. It does not, however, fix the energy-fluence fraction above the Ag K-edge, so beams with similar HVLs can give different DEFs for silver, as Chapter 3 shows for filters with absorption edges ({{REF:t3_4}}).

Most of these models were developed for tungsten, the usual target of medical tubes [@bushberg2012]. Copper-anode tubes serve mainly x-ray diffraction, where Cu Kα is the working line, Kβ is suppressed by a nickel filter [@cullity2001] and the bremsstrahlung continuum is unwanted background. For silver dose enhancement, by contrast, the continuum is the useful part, since only bremsstrahlung photons can exceed the Ag K-edge; yet the full spectrum of copper-anode tubes and its dependence on tube parameters have received much less attention. This work therefore constructed a semi-analytical physics-based model of the copper-anode tube, the developed model (DM), combining the Bethe collision stopping power in the continuous slowing-down approximation (CSDA) [@bethe1930; @icru37], the non-relativistic Bethe–Heitler bremsstrahlung cross-section with the Elwert factor [@bethe1934; @elwert1939; @koch1959] and the Gryziński K-shell ionisation cross-section [@gryzinski1965], with one adjustable parameter, the electron depth factor (Chapter 2). All dependences on tube parameters remain explicit.

## 1.6. Conclusions to Chapter 1

The physical dose enhancement by a high-Z element in kilovoltage beams is governed by its mass energy-absorption coefficient relative to that of tissue, which for silver rises sharply above the K-edge at 25.514 keV; the physical efficacy of silver is thus set mainly by the fraction of the energy fluence above this edge, and a DEF is meaningful only together with its spectrum. AgNPs radiosensitize glioma, breast and colorectal cancer models, in one study more effectively than AuNPs, and are themselves cytotoxic and generate ROS. However, the macroscopic physical contribution of silver for a well-characterised kilovoltage beam, needed to separate physical from chemical and biological effects, has not been determined systematically.

Spectrum models have been developed mainly for tungsten anodes. This work therefore develops a semi-analytical model of the 45 kV copper-anode tube spectrum, validates it against SpekPy v2, and uses both models to calculate the spectrum versus tube design and filtration and the DEF of silver-loaded soft tissue versus concentration, depth and filtration. The results are verified by MC simulation, silver is compared with other high-Z elements, and the DEF uncertainty is estimated as a basis for practical recommendations.
