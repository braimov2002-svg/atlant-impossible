## 3.7. Depth dependence and self-shielding

The DEF values discussed so far refer to the surface of the medium, whereas in experiments with tissue samples, phantoms or small animals the Ag-loaded target lies beneath tissue that modifies the beam. Two situations were therefore calculated with the SpekPy spectra in the primary-beam kerma approximation (Section 2.6). In the first, a small Ag-loaded region lies at depth $z$ in Ag-free soft tissue, and only the overlying tissue attenuates the beam, as described by equation {{REF:eq:def_depth}}. In the second, the region from the surface to the depth $z$ is uniformly loaded with Ag, which itself contributes to the attenuation, as described by equation {{REF:eq:def_slab}}. Scattered radiation is not included in either case; its role is assessed in Section 3.9.

The DEF of a loaded region at depth $z$ is shown in {{REF:fig18_def_depth}}, and {{REF:t3_8}} also gives the relative tissue dose of the primary beam $D/D_0$, where $D_0$ is the dose at the surface. For all filters the DEF increases monotonically with depth, because the overlying tissue absorbs the low-energy photons preferentially and shifts the energy fluence towards the region above the Ag K-edge. The effect is strongest for the unfiltered beam, for which the DEF for 1 % Ag rises from 1.20 at the surface to 1.34 at 5 mm and 1.56 at 20 mm. This gain is accompanied by a drastic loss of dose: as the Cu K lines and the soft bremsstrahlung are absorbed in the first millimetres, the relative dose falls to 0.328 at 1 mm and 0.0090 at 10 mm. A few millimetres of tissue thus act as an added filter, with the same trade-off as in Section 3.6. With the 1 mm Al filter, which hardens the beam in advance, the DEF increases more moderately (1.48 at the surface, 1.54 at 5 mm and 1.69 at 20 mm), while the relative dose decreases only to 0.642 at 5 mm and 0.439 at 10 mm.

{{FIG:fig18_def_depth|Dose enhancement factor for 1 % Ag versus the depth of the Ag-loaded region in Ag-free ICRU-44 soft tissue, for the 45 kV Cu-anode beam without added filter and with Al 0.5 mm, Al 1.0 mm, Cu 0.1 mm and Sn 0.1 mm filters (SpekPy spectra, primary beam, kerma approximation).}}

{{TABLE:t3_8}}

The Cu and Sn filters represent the two extremes. The beam filtered by 0.1 mm Cu consists of the transmitted Cu Kα line and a hard bremsstrahlung tail (Section 3.4); as the Kα line is absorbed in the first millimetres of tissue, the DEF rises steeply from 1.29 at the surface to 1.77 at 5 mm and 1.90 at 20 mm. Beyond about 2 mm this curve lies above all others, and at 5 mm its DEF approaches the surface value obtained with 0.2 mm Cu (1.80), because both beams then consist essentially of the hard component. The Sn 0.1 mm filter produces the weakest depth dependence, from 1.44 at the surface to 1.57 at 20 mm, because the photons above the Sn K-edge (29.2 keV) have been removed and tissue hardens the remaining narrow band only slightly. The ranking of filters by their surface DEF therefore does not hold for deep-seated targets.

The opposite trend appears when the loaded region extends from the surface ({{REF:fig19_def_slab}}). Here the DEF decreases with depth, because Ag most strongly attenuates the photons above its K-edge that produce the enhancement; at 25.514 keV the mass attenuation coefficient of Ag increases 5.8-fold, from 9.53 to 55.4 cm²/g. With the Al 1.0 mm filter the DEF falls from 1.48 at the surface to 1.34 at 5 mm and 1.21 at 10 mm for 1 % Ag, and from 1.96 to 1.57 and 1.27 for 2 % Ag. Self-shielding thus increases with the concentration, and the linear relation between $\mathrm{DEF}-1$ and $w$ given by equation {{REF:eq:def_lin}} is valid only for thin loaded layers. The Sn-filtered beam suffers more from self-shielding (for 1 % Ag, from 1.44 at the surface to 1.15 at 10 mm), because its spectrum is concentrated just above the Ag K-edge, where the attenuation by Ag is largest. The enhancement in a uniformly loaded region several millimetres thick is therefore non-uniform and, on average, considerably lower than at the surface.

## 3.8. Influence of tube voltage and comparison with other high-Z elements

The dependence of the DEF on the tube voltage $U$ is shown in {{REF:fig20_def_kvp}} and {{REF:t3_10}} for the Al 0.5 mm and Al 1.0 mm filters. For 20–50 kV, where SpekPy supports the Cu anode, SpekPy spectra were used, whereas the points for 60–80 kV are predictions of the DM. Below the Ag K-edge the DEF depends only weakly on the voltage: with Al 1.0 mm it increases from 1.26 at 20 kV to 1.27 at 25 kV, owing to the gradual hardening of the bremsstrahlung. Since the maximum photon energy equals $eU$, where $e$ is the elementary charge, photons above 25.514 keV appear only when the voltage exceeds 25.5 kV, and the slope of both curves increases visibly from 26–27 kV. Between 30 and 50 kV the DEF for Al 1.0 mm rises from 1.31 to 1.52 with a gradually decreasing slope, which near 45 kV is consistent with the change of ±0.009 for ±1 kV found in Section 3.10. Above 50 kV the growth slows down further (1.58 at 60 kV and 1.68 at 80 kV), because the spectrum extends beyond the maximum of the Ag/tissue ratio of the mass energy-absorption coefficients near 40–45 keV. As the DM gives slightly lower DEFs than SpekPy at 45 kV (1.369 versus 1.378 for Al 0.5 mm and 1.464 versus 1.482 for Al 1.0 mm), the values at 60–80 kV are, if anything, underestimated.

To place silver among other candidate radiosensitizers, the DEF was calculated under the same conditions for soft tissue containing 1 % by mass of I, Gd, Pt, Au or Bi ({{REF:fig21_elements}}, {{REF:t3_9}}). Without added filtration all elements give similar DEFs of 1.20–1.33, because the dose is dominated by the Cu K lines and by photons below 15 keV. Gd gives the largest value (1.33), since its L-absorption edges at 7.2–8.4 keV make it a strong absorber of the Cu K lines, whereas the L-edges of Pt, Au and Bi lie above the Cu lines, at about 12–16 keV.

{{FIG:fig21_elements|Dose enhancement factor of soft tissue containing 1 % by mass of Ag, I, Gd, Pt, Au or Bi for the 45 kV Cu-anode beam without added filter and with Al 1.0 mm and Sn 0.1 mm filters (SpekPy spectra, kerma approximation).}}

{{TABLE:t3_9}}

With the Al 1.0 mm filter the picture changes completely: the DEF reaches 2.13 for Pt, 2.16 for Au, 2.23 for Bi and 1.71 for Gd, whereas Ag (1.48) and I (1.46) remain close to each other. As the ratios of the mass energy-absorption coefficients in {{REF:fig03_ratio}} show, above their L-edges the heavy elements absorb more energy per unit mass than Ag over the whole filtered spectrum, including the 15–25 keV region where Ag absorbs only through its L and outer shells. Gd, whose K-edge (50.2 keV) lies above the maximum photon energy, absorbs more than Ag only below the Ag K-edge, but this region still contributes much of the tissue dose of the filtered beam. Iodine behaves like Ag, but its K-edge lies higher, at 33.2 keV. The Sn 0.1 mm filter slightly increases the DEF for Gd, Pt, Au and Bi but decreases it for Ag and I (1.44 for both), because it removes the photons above 29.2 keV, where the Ag/tissue ratio reaches 100–139 and where the iodine K-edge lies. For the heavy elements, whose ratio varies much less across the filtered spectrum than that of Ag, the stronger suppression of low-energy photons by Sn, reflected in its larger HVL (0.90 versus 0.83 mm Al), outweighs this loss.

Per unit mass, therefore, Ag is a weaker physical sensitizer at 45 kV than Gd, Pt, Au and Bi and is comparable to iodine: with Al 1 mm its value of $\mathrm{DEF}-1$ (0.48) is less than half that of Au. The arguments in favour of Ag lie elsewhere. Its K-edge lies inside the 45 kV spectrum, which allows its enhancement to be tuned by filtration; silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) are biologically active and generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) [@asharani2009]; and silver is considerably cheaper than gold or platinum. The choice of Ag must thus be justified by its combined physical and biological action, which is discussed in Section 3.11.

## 3.9. Monte Carlo verification

The analytical DEFs rest on two simplifications: only the primary beam is considered, and the energy of the Ag K-fluorescence photons, which is excluded from the mass energy-absorption coefficient of Ag, is assumed to escape without being absorbed. Both assumptions were tested with the Monte Carlo (MC) code described and verified in Section 2.7. A laterally infinite slab of ICRU-44 soft tissue 30 mm thick was irradiated by a broad parallel beam, and Ag was added to a mass fraction of 1 % in a 10 mm layer at depths of 2–12 mm. For each configuration, $4\times10^{6}$ photon histories in 5 independent batches were tracked with the Woodcock method, including photoelectric absorption with emission and transport of Ag K-fluorescence and Compton and Rayleigh scattering with the xraylib differential cross-sections; the electron energy was deposited locally. The dose was scored in bins of 0.25 mm, and the DEF was obtained as the ratio of the dose profiles with and without Ag for the unfiltered and the Al 1.0 mm-filtered SpekPy spectra. The analytical reference is the primary-beam kerma model for the same geometry, in which the beam is attenuated by tissue and, inside and behind the layer, also by Ag.

The MC and analytical depth profiles of the DEF are compared in {{REF:fig22_mc}}, and {{REF:t3_11}} gives their values averaged over characteristic regions. Both methods reproduce the same shape inside the layer. With Al 1 mm the DEF rises sharply at the entrance and then decreases towards the exit because of self-shielding. For the unfiltered beam a narrow peak at the entrance is followed by a minimum at about 3–4 mm and a subsequent rise, which in the analytical profile forms a broad maximum near 7–8 mm: Ag first strongly attenuates the soft components that still dominate the dose at the entrance, after which beam hardening raises the DEF again. At the layer entrance the two methods agree within 1.2 % for the unfiltered beam (1.168 versus 1.155) and within 3.0 % for Al 1 mm (1.535 versus 1.490). The difference grows with depth to 6.5 % and 10.1 % at the layer exit, and the averages over the whole layer are 1.172 versus 1.116 and 1.447 versus 1.354, respectively, the MC values being higher in all regions.

{{FIG:fig22_mc|Depth profiles of the dose enhancement factor in ICRU-44 soft tissue with a 10 mm layer containing 1 % Ag at 2–12 mm (grey band) for the unfiltered beam (left) and the Al 1.0 mm filter (right): Monte Carlo (solid line; the shaded band shows ±2 standard errors estimated from 5 batches) and the analytical primary-beam kerma model (dashed line).}}

{{TABLE:t3_11}}

Two effects explain this excess. First, part of the Ag K-fluorescence, with energies of about 22 keV, is reabsorbed within the layer: the mean free path of these photons in tissue, about 1.5 cm, is comparable to the layer thickness, so a considerable part of the energy that the kerma approximation treats as escaping is deposited in the layer. Second, scattered photons, which the primary-beam model ignores, contribute to the dose. Both contributions build up with depth, which explains why the difference grows towards the exit; fluorescence emitted backwards also raises the MC DEF slightly above unity in front of the layer for the filtered beam. Behind the layer the dose is reduced to 0.80 of the Ag-free value according to MC and to 0.76 according to the analytical model, because the layer shields the downstream tissue. This shielding protects normal tissue behind a loaded tumour, but it implies an underdosage of tumour regions located behind a heavily loaded region. The analytical model thus underestimates the macroscopic DEF in a thick loaded region by up to 10 % and is conservative, whereas near the entrance of the loaded region the agreement is within 1–3 %.

## 3.10. Uncertainty and sensitivity analysis

The reliability of the central result of this work, DEF = 1.482 for 1 % Ag with the Al 1.0 mm filter, was assessed by a one-at-a-time sensitivity analysis ({{REF:fig23_sensitivity}}, {{REF:t3_12}}). Each input parameter was varied within an interval representing its plausible uncertainty, all other parameters being held at their reference values. The tube voltage was varied by ±1 kV, the anode angle by ±3°, the Be window by ±0.1 mm and the Al filter thickness by ±5 %. The mass energy-absorption coefficient of Ag was varied by ±2 %, and that of the tissue component of the loaded medium by ±1 % with the reference dose unchanged. The spectrum model was represented by the difference between the DM and SpekPy, and the depth factor $\eta$ of the DM was varied by ±25 %, the resulting changes being evaluated relative to the DM value of 1.464.

{{FIG:fig23_sensitivity|Sensitivity of the dose enhancement factor (1 % Ag, Al 1.0 mm filter, DEF = 1.482) to the input parameters: change of the DEF for the lower and the upper value of each parameter.}}

{{TABLE:t3_12}}

The largest change, 0.057, results from the choice of the reference medium: the ICRU four-component tissue gives 1.539 and water 1.496. Both media lack the Na, P, S, Cl and K of ICRU-44 tissue, whose photoabsorption raises its energy absorption at low photon energies, and the weaker absorption of the reference medium increases the DEF. This is a matter of definition rather than of uncertainty and was excluded from the combined uncertainty, but it shows that the reference medium must always be stated. Among the other parameters the spectrum model dominates (0.018); the coefficients of Ag and tissue, the anode angle and the tube voltage contribute 0.009–0.010 each, and the Al thickness and $\eta$ contribute 0.008 and 0.007. A smaller anode angle increases the DEF (1.492 at 9° and 1.476 at 15°) through stronger self-filtration in the anode, whereas the Be window is irrelevant (0.0002), because the Al filter removes the soft photons that the window would attenuate. Treating each maximum change as the half-width of a rectangular distribution, the combined standard uncertainty is

$$
u_c(\mathrm{DEF}) = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{\Delta_i^{2}}{3}}
$$ {#eq:uc_def}

where $\Delta_i$ is the maximum absolute change of the DEF caused by the variation of the $i$-th parameter and $n = 8$ is the number of parameters included (all except the reference medium). This gives $u_c = 0.0165$, i.e. DEF = 1.48 ± 0.02 with the coverage factor $k = 1$, or ± 0.03 with $k = 2$. The uncertainty is more than an order of magnitude smaller than the enhancement itself ($\mathrm{DEF}-1 = 0.48$) and is also smaller than the differences between the main filter options, so the conclusions about filtration are robust. Incorrect interaction data would cause a much larger error: the xraylib 4.3.0 function CS_Energy would give 1.72 instead of 1.48 (Section 2.8).

## 3.11. Discussion

The results agree with the general picture of dose enhancement by high-Z agents established in earlier work. Dose enhancement in iodine-loaded tumours irradiated with kilovoltage x-rays was calculated already in early studies [@mello1983; @solberg1992], and MC estimates for gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) showed that the macroscopic enhancement is much larger for kilovoltage than for megavoltage beams [@cho2005]. The theoretical enhancement from nanoparticles of various elements has been characterised versus photon energy with a kerma-based approach [@roeske2007], as in the present work. The depth dependence of the enhancement in orthovoltage beams has also been studied by MC calculations in phantoms [@chow2023], and the present results (Section 3.7) show for Ag that a single surface value cannot characterise a thick target. The present work adds the case of a low-voltage Cu-anode tube, whose spectrum is dominated by characteristic lines below the Ag K-edge and for which the attainable DEF is determined by filtration rather than by the design of the tube.

The MC studies devoted specifically to silver include the dose enhancement from AgNPs implanted in a brain tumour during brachytherapy [@taha2019], the effect of an Ag-loaded tumour on the surrounding healthy tissues [@caglar2024] and a comparison of Ag and Au nanoparticles in layered skin geometries [@esitmez2026]; the dose enhancement in AgNP-enriched dose gels has also been investigated experimentally [@merkis2023]. Because these studies differ from the present one in radiation source, geometry and concentration, only a qualitative comparison is meaningful. The reduction of the dose behind a 10 mm layer with 1 % Ag to about 0.80 of the Ag-free value concerns the dose to surrounding tissues considered in [@caglar2024], and the DEFs of Au and Ag per unit mass at 45 kV (2.16 versus 1.48 with Al 1 mm) are relevant to the Ag–Au comparison in [@esitmez2026].

A more significant discrepancy appears in comparison with radiobiological studies. AgNPs have been reported to sensitize cancer cells to killing by ionising radiation [@xu2009] and to act as radiation sensitizers for glioma [@liu2013], and they outperformed AuNPs in radiosensitizing U251 glioma cells in vitro and in an intracranial mouse model [@liu2016]. The biological effect is quantified by the sensitizer enhancement ratio (SER), the ratio of the doses that produce the same effect without and with the sensitizer, which, unlike the DEF, reflects all mechanisms of sensitization [@hall2018]. Macroscopic physics predicts the opposite ranking of Ag and Au per unit mass, and at low mass fractions the physical enhancement is small: with Al 1 mm the DEF is only 1.048 for 0.1 % Ag. A direct numerical comparison is not possible, because the biological effect also depends on the radiation quality, the concentration and the cellular localisation of the nanoparticles; nevertheless, the physical results indicate that the reported radiosensitization by AgNPs cannot be explained by macroscopic dose enhancement alone. Two mechanisms described in the literature may account for the difference. First, AgNPs are biologically active and cause ROS formation, DNA damage, cell-cycle arrest and impaired mitochondrial function in human cells [@asharani2009]. Second, the dose enhancement around gold nanoparticles is highly localised [@jones2010], and nanoscale energy deposition near heavy-atom nanoparticles can explain biological effects beyond the macroscopic dose enhancement [@mcmahon2011]. The macroscopic DEF calculated here is thus only the physical baseline of a radio-enhancement that also has chemical and biological stages [@kuncic2018].

The results are subject to several limitations. The DEF was calculated in the kerma approximation with local deposition of the electron energy, which is appropriate at the macroscopic scale but ignores the nanoscale dose heterogeneity around nanoparticles, and Ag was assumed to be distributed homogeneously, whereas nanoparticles accumulate non-uniformly in cells and tissues. The DM assumes isotropic bremsstrahlung emission and neglects electron backscattering from the anode; the latter, together with the approximate Born cross-section, causes an overestimate of the absolute output (DM/SpekPy air-kerma ratio 1.08–1.42), which does not affect the DEF; it also underestimates the Cu K lines by 25–43 %, which is why the main DEF results were obtained with SpekPy spectra. The off-axis variation of the spectrum caused by the heel effect was not modelled. The analytical depth calculations neglect scattered radiation and fluorescence reabsorption, which leads to an underestimate of up to 10 %, and the MC model uses a simple slab geometry. Finally, no spectrum of an actual tube was measured, although SpekPy, used as the reference, has been validated against measured spectra and HVLs [@bujila2020], and no biological response was modelled.

For an experiment on Ag radio-enhancement with a 45 kV Cu-anode tube, the results lead to the following practical recommendations. The beam should be filtered with 1–2 mm of Al (or 0.1–0.3 mm of Ti); with Al this gives DEF 1.48–1.62 for 1 % Ag and exposure times for 2 Gy of 14.6–32.5 s at 40 mA. Thin Cu filters (0.05–0.1 mm) and K-edge filters with edges between 20 and 30 keV (Mo, Ag, Sn) should be avoided, and 0.2 mm Cu, although it gives the largest DEF (1.80), requires 158 s for 2 Gy. The beam quality should be verified by measuring the HVL in accordance with the dosimetry protocol for kilovoltage beams [@ma2001]; the calculated first HVL is 0.83 mm Al with 1 mm Al and 1.27 mm Al with 2 mm Al, and a measured value close to these confirms both the spectrum and the filter thickness. The Ag mass fraction should be at least 0.5 %, for which the DEF with 1 mm Al is 1.24, since at 0.1 % the enhancement is below 5 %. For samples thicker than a few millimetres the DEF should be calculated for the actual depth and loading, and the mass energy-absorption coefficients should be taken from the NIST tables rather than from the xraylib function CS_Energy. Finally, the DEF should always be reported together with the spectrum, or at least the tube voltage, filtration and HVL, as well as the reference medium and the depth.

## 3.12. Conclusions to Chapter 3

The main results of this chapter are as follows.

1. The DM with a single fitted parameter ($\eta = 0.40$) reproduces SpekPy with HVL deviations of −4.6 to −3.5 % for Al-filtered beams and within 1–2 % in DEF, but it underestimates the Cu K lines by 25–43 %.
2. The unfiltered 45 kV spectrum is dominated by the Cu K lines (68.5 % of the photons), and the DEF for 1 % Ag is only 1.20; the tube design parameters change mainly the output, the DEF remaining within 1.19–1.21.
3. Filtration is decisive: the DEF for 1 % Ag is 1.48 with Al 1 mm, 1.62 with Al 2 mm and 1.80 with Cu 0.2 mm, at a 120–1300-fold lower dose rate; at equal transmission Al and Ti are optimal, and K-edge filters with edges at 20–30 keV are unfavourable.
4. The DEF increases with the depth of the loaded region in Ag-free tissue (with Al 1 mm, from 1.48 to 1.69 at 20 mm) and decreases inside a uniformly loaded region because of self-shielding (to 1.21 at 10 mm for 1 % Ag).
5. The DEF increases with tube voltage, most rapidly above the Ag K-edge; per unit mass, Ag is a weaker physical sensitizer than Gd, Pt, Au and Bi and is comparable to iodine.
6. MC calculations confirm the analytical model within 1–10 % (the MC values are higher because of Ag K-fluorescence reabsorption and scatter) and show a dose reduction to about 0.80 behind the loaded layer.
7. The DEF for 1 % Ag with Al 1 mm is 1.48 ± 0.02 ($k = 1$); the choice of the reference medium changes it by up to 0.057 and must be stated.
