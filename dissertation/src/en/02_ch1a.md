# CHAPTER 1. RADIOSENSITIZATION BY HIGH-Z ELEMENTS AND X-RAY SPECTRUM MODELING: LITERATURE REVIEW

This chapter reviews the physical and radiobiological background of the work. It considers the clinical problem that motivates the use of radiosensitizers, the interaction of kilovoltage photons with biological matter and the development of radiosensitization by elements with a high atomic number (high-Z elements), from iodinated contrast agents to metal nanoparticles. It then reviews silver as a radiosensitizer and the methods of modelling x-ray tube spectra. The chapter closes with conclusions that define the tasks of this dissertation.

## 1.1. Radiotherapy and the problem of normal-tissue tolerance

Cancer is a major cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide [@bray2024]. Radiotherapy (RT) is, together with surgery and chemotherapy, one of the principal modalities of cancer treatment [@baskar2012]. An analysis of evidence-based clinical guidelines estimated the optimal rate of RT utilisation at approximately 52 %, so that about half of all cancer patients have an indication for RT [@delaney2005]. The therapeutic action of RT is based on damage to cellular DNA, produced directly by secondary electrons or indirectly by the free radicals formed in the radiolysis of water; double-strand breaks are the most lethal of these lesions [@hall2018]. Because energy deposition by ionising radiation is not selective, the same damage occurs in the healthy tissues traversed by the beam, and their response ultimately limits the dose that can be delivered to the tumour [@hall2018; @baskar2012].

The quantitative expression of this limitation is the therapeutic ratio. The probabilities of tumour control and of normal-tissue complications both increase with dose along sigmoid dose–response curves, and the therapeutic ratio characterises the separation between them [@hall2018]. When the curves lie close together, a dose escalation that would improve local control also produces an unacceptable rate of severe complications, so that the tolerance of the normal tissues and organs near the target limits the prescribed dose. Normal-tissue toxicity is therefore regarded as the main factor limiting the effectiveness of RT [@baskar2012]. The problem is aggravated for radioresistant tumours, in particular those containing hypoxic cells, which are considerably less sensitive to x-rays than well-oxygenated cells because of the oxygen effect [@hall2018].

Several strategies have been developed to widen the therapeutic window. Fractionation of the total dose exploits the differences in repair and recovery between normal and tumour tissues [@hall2018; @baskar2012]. Conformal techniques, such as intensity-modulated and image-guided RT, shape the high-dose region to the target volume and reduce the irradiated volume of normal tissue [@khan2014; @baskar2012]. Neither approach, however, changes the ratio of the energy absorbed per unit mass in tumour cells and in normal cells located in the same region of the beam, for example in normal tissue infiltrated by the tumour. A third strategy acts on this ratio directly: radiosensitizers are agents that increase the effect of radiation on tumour cells more than on normal cells [@hall2018; @wardman2007].

Chemical radiosensitizers include the oxygen mimetics, such as the nitroimidazoles, which imitate the action of oxygen in hypoxic cells [@wardman2007]. Compounds of platinum, gold, ruthenium and other transition metals combine chemical and biological activity with the presence of heavy atoms [@gill2019]. A distinct, essentially physical principle underlies the use of high-Z elements. An element of this kind concentrated in the tumour absorbs a larger fraction of the energy of the incident photons than soft tissue does, so that the tumour dose increases while the dose to the surrounding tissue that does not contain the element remains, to a first approximation, unchanged [@hainfeld2004; @kuncic2018]. The magnitude of this increase is expressed by the dose enhancement factor (DEF), the ratio of the absorbed dose in tissue containing the high-Z agent to that in the same tissue without it (Section 2.6). In contrast to chemical sensitization, this physical component of the effect can be calculated from interaction data once the radiation field is known.

The physical gain from a high-Z element depends strongly on the photon energy. Monte Carlo (MC) calculations for gold nanoparticles showed that the macroscopic dose enhancement is much larger with kilovoltage than with megavoltage x-ray beams [@cho2005], because the strongly Z-dependent photoelectric effect dominates only at low photon energies (Section 1.2). Kilovoltage x-rays are used in superficial and orthovoltage therapy [@khan2014], and the dosimetry of 40–300 kV beams in radiotherapy and radiobiology follows dedicated protocols in which the beam quality is specified by the half-value layer (HVL) [@ma2001; @iaea_trs398]. Since the enhancement is determined by the photon energy distribution, the DEF of an agent cannot be stated without specifying the x-ray spectrum, which depends on the design of the x-ray tube and on its filtration. The present work addresses this problem for silver irradiated with the beam of a 45 kV x-ray tube with a copper anode.

## 1.2. Interaction of kilovoltage photons with biological matter

Below 50 keV, photons interact with matter through photoelectric absorption, incoherent (Compton) scattering and coherent (Rayleigh) scattering; pair production is impossible, because its threshold, $2m_ec^2 = 1.022$ MeV (twice the electron rest energy), lies far above this range [@podgorsak2016; @attix1986]. The mass attenuation coefficient, which describes the attenuation of a narrow beam, is the sum of the partial coefficients of these processes [@attix1986; @andreo2017]:

$$
\frac{\mu}{\rho} = \frac{\tau}{\rho} + \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{coh}}}{\rho} + \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{inc}}}{\rho}
$$ {#eq:mu_sum}

where $\mu$ is the linear attenuation coefficient, $\rho$ is the density of the medium, and $\tau/\rho$, $\sigma_{\mathrm{coh}}/\rho$ and $\sigma_{\mathrm{inc}}/\rho$ are the mass coefficients of photoelectric absorption, coherent scattering and incoherent scattering, all in cm²/g. For a mixture such as soft tissue, the elemental coefficients are weighted by the mass fractions of the elements [@attix1986]; evaluated data are tabulated by the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) [@nist_xraymass; @nist_xcom].

In the photoelectric effect the photon is absorbed by a bound electron, which is ejected with a kinetic energy equal to the photon energy $E$ minus its binding energy. The interaction occurs preferentially with the most tightly bound accessible shell, and between absorption edges the mass photoelectric coefficient varies approximately as [@podgorsak2016]

$$
\frac{\tau}{\rho} \propto \frac{Z^{3}}{E^{3}}
$$ {#eq:tau_scaling}

where $Z$ is the atomic number of the absorbing element; the atomic cross-section varies approximately as $Z^{4}/E^{3}$ [@podgorsak2016; @attix1986]. Per unit mass, a heavy element therefore absorbs photons far more strongly than H, C, N and O, which make up almost the entire mass of soft tissue [@icru44]. This scaling, however, holds only between absorption edges: below the K-edge of a heavy element its K-shell electrons cannot take part in photoabsorption, and the ratio of its coefficient to that of tissue is much smaller than the atomic numbers alone would suggest.

Absorption edges are abrupt increases of $\tau/\rho$ at photon energies equal to the binding energies of the atomic shells [@podgorsak2016]; for silver, the K-edge lies at 25.514 keV and the three L-subshell edges lie between 3.35 and 3.81 keV. The vacancy left by photoabsorption is filled by an electron from an outer shell, and the released energy is emitted either as a characteristic (fluorescence) x-ray or as an Auger electron [@bambynek1972; @krause1979]. The K-shell fluorescence yield $\omega_K$, the probability of radiative relaxation of a K-shell vacancy, increases rapidly with $Z$: it equals 0.039 for aluminium, 0.454 for copper, 0.831 for silver and 0.960 for gold ({{REF:t2_2}}) [@krause1979; @schoonjans2011]. In H, C, N and O, whose K-shell binding energies are below 1 keV, practically all the absorbed energy remains near the interaction site as kinetic energy of electrons. A heavy element, in contrast, re-emits a large part of it as fluorescence: Ag Kα photons (about 22 keV) have a mean free path of about 1.5 cm in soft tissue, whereas its low-energy Auger electrons deposit their energy in the immediate vicinity of the atom (Section 1.3).

In Compton scattering the photon transfers part of its energy to an atomic electron; at kilovoltage energies the scattered photon retains most of the incident energy [@podgorsak2016; @attix1986]. Electron binding reduces the free-electron (Klein–Nishina) cross-section at low energies and small angles, as described by the incoherent scattering function $S(x,Z)$, where $x$ is the momentum-transfer variable; Rayleigh scattering only deflects the photon and is described by the atomic form factor $F(x,Z)$ [@hubbell1975]. In soft tissue incoherent scattering overtakes photoabsorption in the upper part of the interval below 50 keV, while in silver photoabsorption dominates throughout [@podgorsak2016; @nist_xcom]. Since not all the energy of an interacting photon is absorbed locally, the mass energy-absorption coefficient $\mu_{en}/\rho$ includes only the energy transferred to charged particles and not re-emitted by them as bremsstrahlung [@attix1986; @seltzer1993]:

$$
\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho} = \left[\frac{\tau}{\rho}\left(1-\frac{X}{E}\right) + \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{inc}}}{\rho}\,\frac{\bar{T}_{\mathrm{inc}}}{E}\right]\left(1-\bar{g}\right)
$$ {#eq:muen_def}

where $X$ is the mean energy emitted as fluorescence radiation per photoabsorption event, $\bar{T}_{\mathrm{inc}}$ is the mean kinetic energy of the Compton recoil electron, $\bar{g}$ is the mean fraction of the kinetic energy of the secondary electrons converted into bremsstrahlung, which is negligible for kilovoltage photons in low-Z media, and the remaining symbols are as in {{REF:eq:mu_sum}} [@attix1986]. The NIST values of $\mu_{en}/\rho$ used in this work are based on the calculations of Seltzer [@seltzer1993; @nist_xraymass].

According to the International Commission on Radiation Units and Measurements (ICRU), kerma $K$ is the sum of the initial kinetic energies of all charged particles liberated by photons per unit mass, the collision kerma $K_{\mathrm{col}}$ is its part not converted into bremsstrahlung, and the absorbed dose $D$ is the mean energy imparted per unit mass, in grays (1 Gy = 1 J/kg) [@icru85; @attix1986]. Under charged-particle equilibrium (CPE), when the energy carried into a small volume by charged particles equals the energy carried out of it, the absorbed dose equals the collision kerma [@attix1986; @andreo2017]:

$$
D = K_{\mathrm{col}} = \Psi\,\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}
$$ {#eq:dose_cpe}

where $\Psi = \Phi E$ is the energy fluence of monoenergetic photons with energy $E$ and fluence $\Phi$; for a polyenergetic beam the right-hand side is integrated over the photon spectrum (Section 2.5). Secondary electrons released by photons below 50 keV have ranges in tissue much shorter than a millimetre, so that CPE is established close to surfaces and boundaries, and by the Fano theorem it is not disturbed by density variations in a medium of uniform atomic composition [@fano1954]. Two media exposed to the same photon spectrum therefore receive absorbed doses in the ratio of their spectrum-averaged mass energy-absorption coefficients. This relation underlies the macroscopic, kerma-based DEF (Section 2.6) and fails only within distances of the order of the electron ranges from a high-Z inclusion.

The coefficients of silver and of ICRU-44 soft tissue [@icru44] are compared in {{REF:fig02_coeffs}}, plotted from the NIST tables [@nist_xraymass]. Above about 4 keV, that is, above the L-edges, both coefficients of silver exceed those of tissue by more than an order of magnitude, and the ratio of the mass energy-absorption coefficients increases from 21.0 at 8 keV to 31.8 at 25 keV. At the K-edge $\mu/\rho$ of silver increases by a factor of 5.8, from 9.53 to 55.4 cm²/g, whereas $\mu_{en}/\rho$ increases only by a factor of 2.4, from 8.54 to 20.3 cm²/g. The reason is K-shell fluorescence: about 83 % of the K vacancies ($\omega_K = 0.831$) relax by emitting K x-rays, mainly Kα photons of about 22 keV, whose energy leaves the interaction site and is excluded from $\mu_{en}/\rho$.

{{FIG:fig02_coeffs|Mass attenuation coefficients (solid lines) and mass energy-absorption coefficients (dashed lines) of silver and ICRU-44 soft tissue in the energy range 1–50 keV (NIST data). The vertical dashed line marks the Ag K-edge at 25.51 keV; the arrow indicates the L-subshell edges of silver.}}

For soft tissue the two coefficients nearly coincide below about 10 keV and diverge at higher energies, where scattering, which deposits little energy, takes a growing share of the attenuation. Above the K-edge, $\mu_{en}/\rho$ of silver decreases with energy more slowly than its $\mu/\rho$, because the fixed fluorescence energy becomes a progressively smaller fraction of the photon energy, and also more slowly than $\mu_{en}/\rho$ of tissue. Consequently, after the jump at the edge from about 32 to about 76, the silver-to-tissue ratio of the mass energy-absorption coefficients continues to grow, reaching 102.7 at 30 keV, 120.0 at 35 keV and a maximum of about 139 at 40–45 keV. The fraction of the photon energy fluence between the Ag K-edge and the maximum photon energy, where silver absorbs most efficiently relative to tissue, is therefore the key characteristic of a beam intended for silver-enhanced irradiation, and its determination for a copper-anode tube is a central task of this work.

## 1.3. High-Z radiosensitizers: from iodine contrast to metal nanoparticles

The concept of dose enhancement near a high-Z medium in kilovoltage beams goes back to Spiers, who analysed how the increased energy absorption of bone and the ranges of the secondary electrons determine the dose to soft tissue in and near irradiated bone [@spiers1949]. This analysis already contained both physical ingredients of later dose-enhancement concepts: enhanced energy transfer to the high-Z material and energy transport by secondary electrons into adjacent tissue. Das and Chopra later quantified the backscatter dose perturbation at interfaces between tissue and high-Z materials in kilovoltage beams [@das1995]. Beyond distances of the order of the electron ranges, the dose is again governed by the local photon fluence and energy-absorption coefficient.

The first therapeutic applications used iodine from clinically approved contrast media. Matsudaira et al. found that an iodinated contrast medium sensitized cultured mammalian cells to x-rays but not to gamma rays [@matsudaira1980], which points to the photoelectric absorption of low-energy photons by iodine. Mello et al. calculated the dose enhancement in iodine-loaded tumours for kilovoltage x-rays [@mello1983], and Solberg et al. computed DEFs for the dose-enhancement therapy of brain tumours [@solberg1992]. The concept reached the clinic when a computed tomography scanner modified for therapy (CTRx) delivered the first radiotherapy of human metastatic brain tumours with iodinated contrast [@rose1999]. Monochromatic synchrotron beams were later used to treat rats bearing F98 gliomas loaded with an iodinated contrast agent [@adam2003] and to determine experimentally the optimal energy for enhancing the radiotoxicity of stable iodinated compounds in cells [@corde2004]. Kilovoltage contrast-enhanced radiotherapy was also analysed dosimetrically and microdosimetrically by MC simulation [@verhaegen2005], and modified megavoltage beams were combined with iodine and gadolinium contrast media [@robar2002].

Platinum-based drugs combine a chemotherapeutic agent with a high-Z atom. Biston et al. reported the cure of rats bearing radioresistant F98 gliomas treated with cisplatin and irradiated with monochromatic synchrotron x-rays, which exploited the photoactivation of platinum at its K-edge [@biston2004]. Pignol et al. analysed the clinical significance of inner-shell ionisation and Auger cascades for radiosensitization with iododeoxyuridine, bromodeoxyuridine, platinum salts and gadolinium porphyrin compounds [@pignol2003]; when the heavy atom lies in or near DNA, the short-range Auger electrons deposit their energy close to this critical target. These studies drew attention to the subcellular location of the high-Z atom, not only to its concentration, and transition-metal complexes have since been studied widely as radiosensitizers that combine this physical mechanism with chemical and biological activity [@gill2019].

The modern stage of the field began with the study of Hainfeld et al., in which gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) 1.9 nm in diameter markedly improved the one-year survival of tumour-bearing mice irradiated with 250 kVp (kilovolt peak) x-rays [@hainfeld2004]. The key parameters of nanoparticle efficacy, namely the material, size, coating and photon energy, have been analysed systematically [@retif2015]. Radiosensitization by AuNPs has been demonstrated in vitro with superficial kilovoltage x-rays [@rahman2009], with orthovoltage and superficial beams for folate-conjugated AuNPs in HeLa cells [@khoshgard2014], and with monoenergetic synchrotron beams used to find the optimal photon energy [@rahman2014]. Cellular uptake and radiosensitization depend on the particle size [@chithrani2010], and experiments with DNA in solution identified the photon energy, particle size and concentration as the governing parameters [@brun2009]. These results are summarised in several reviews [@mesbahi2010; @jain2012; @haume2016; @song2017; @penninckx2020] and in a roadmap to clinical use [@schuemann2016].

The physics of AuNP radiosensitization has been studied mainly by MC simulation. Following the first MC estimate of the macroscopic tumour dose enhancement by AuNPs in typical radiation treatments [@cho2005], Zhang et al. quantified the tumour-selective enhancement for various photon sources [@zhang2009], Kakade and Sharma estimated it for therapeutic photon beams [@kakade2015], and Koger and Kirkby found the kilovoltage range optimal for AuNP-enhanced arc radiotherapy [@koger2016]. Lechtman et al. showed how radiosensitization depends on the photon energy and on the size, concentration and location of the nanoparticles [@lechtman2011]. On the microscopic scale, the dose enhancement around AuNPs is highly localised [@jones2010], and the spatial distribution of the secondary electrons from irradiated AuNPs was characterised with the Geant4 toolkit [@leung2011]. These studies consistently show a strong energy dependence, so that a reliable description of the incident spectrum is a prerequisite of any dose-enhancement calculation.

The biological effect of high-Z nanoparticles, however, cannot be explained by the increase in the mean dose alone. Radio-enhancement is usually divided into a physical stage, in which the particle absorbs additional photon energy and emits photoelectrons, Auger electrons and fluorescence photons; a chemical stage, with additional production of reactive oxygen species (ROS); and a biological stage, involving oxidative stress, perturbation of the cell cycle and inhibition of DNA repair [@kuncic2018; @her2017; @butterworth2012; @rosa2017]. McMahon et al. showed with a local effect model that the highly inhomogeneous nanoscale energy deposition near irradiated heavy-atom nanoparticles can explain biological effects that exceed the macroscopic dose enhancement [@mcmahon2011]. The sensitizer enhancement ratio (SER) measured in cell experiments may therefore exceed the macroscopic DEF for the same irradiation. The macroscopic DEF nevertheless remains the baseline quantity that separates the physical contribution from the chemical and biological ones.

Although gold dominates the literature, the choice of the element is not obvious. Roeske et al. characterised the theoretical, kerma-based dose enhancement from nanoparticles of various elements as a function of photon energy [@roeske2007], and McMahon et al. used physical modelling to optimise the choice of element for nanoparticle radiosensitizers [@mcmahon2016]. In the kerma approximation the comparison reduces to the ratio $R(E)$ of the mass energy-absorption coefficient of the element to that of tissue: for tissue containing a mass fraction $w$ of the element and irradiated by monoenergetic photons of energy $E$, the DEF equals $1 + w\,[R(E) - 1]$ (Section 2.6). The ratio $R(E)$ for silver, iodine, gadolinium, platinum, gold and bismuth is shown in {{REF:fig03_ratio}} for 5–45 keV, which contains practically the whole spectrum of the 45 kV tube studied here.

{{FIG:fig03_ratio|Ratio of the mass energy-absorption coefficient of Ag, I, Gd, Pt, Au and Bi to that of ICRU-44 soft tissue in the energy range 5–45 keV (NIST data). The vertical dashed line marks the Ag K-edge at 25.51 keV.}}

Below about 12 keV the ratios for silver, platinum, gold and bismuth are similar, and that for iodine is somewhat higher. Above its L-edges (7.2–8.4 keV) and up to about 13 keV gadolinium has the highest ratio, so that it absorbs the Cu Kα lines at about 8.0 keV particularly efficiently. The L-edges of platinum, gold and bismuth at about 12–16 keV raise their ratios several-fold, and between about 12 and 25.5 keV silver has the lowest ratio of the six. Above its K-edge silver first equals and then exceeds gadolinium, whose K-edge (50.239 keV) lies above the maximum photon energy of the beam, and it exceeds iodine up to the iodine K-edge at 33.169 keV. At 40–45 keV the ratios for iodine, platinum and silver become comparable, while gold and bismuth retain the highest values.

Two conclusions follow. First, per unit mass, silver is expected to be a weaker physical sensitizer than platinum, gold and bismuth, whose ratios exceed its own over most of the interval; its advantage over iodine and gadolinium is limited to energies above its K-edge, and whether it yields a higher DEF depends on the spectrum. Second, the physical efficacy of silver is controlled mainly by the fraction of the photon energy fluence above its K-edge: between 8 keV and the edge, which includes the Cu K lines at 8.0–8.9 keV, the mass energy-absorption coefficient of silver exceeds that of tissue only 21–32 times, but above the edge 76–139 times. The design parameters of the tube, and especially its filtration, which redistribute the energy fluence relative to 25.514 keV, are therefore expected to be decisive for the DEF, as tested quantitatively in Chapter 3. The arguments that nevertheless favour silver are reviewed in Section 1.4.
