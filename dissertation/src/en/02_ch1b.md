## 1.4. Silver and silver nanoparticles as radiosensitizers

Silver ($Z = 47$) occupies an intermediate position between the light elements of soft tissue and the heavy elements, such as gold ($Z = 79$), that dominate the literature on high-Z radiosensitizers. Silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) are widely studied in biomedicine. Their synthesis, properties and therapeutic applications have been reviewed by Wei et al. [@wei2015], and their biomedical applications, including antimicrobial and anticancer uses, by Burdușel et al. [@burdusel2018]. Silver is less expensive than gold, and AgNPs can also be obtained by green synthesis, for example with egg white as the reaction medium [@lu2012]. For radiotherapy, two properties of silver are of particular interest: its biological activity and the position of its K absorption edge inside the spectra of x-ray tubes operated at a few tens of kilovolts.

The biological activity of AgNPs is closely related to their toxicity. AshaRani et al. showed that AgNPs are cytotoxic and genotoxic in human cells: they induce the formation of ROS, cause DNA damage and cell-cycle arrest, and involve the mitochondria in the toxic response [@asharani2009]. These effects correspond to the chemical and biological stages of radio-enhancement described in Section 1.3, in which additional ROS, oxidative stress, perturbation of the cell cycle and inhibition of DNA repair amplify the initial physical damage [@kuncic2018; @rosa2017]. AgNPs may therefore sensitize cells to radiation beyond what their physical dose enhancement alone would produce. At the same time, their cytotoxicity [@asharani2009] makes the selective accumulation of AgNPs in the tumour a prerequisite of therapeutic use, and the effect of dose enhancement in the tumour on the surrounding healthy tissues also has to be assessed [@caglar2024].

Experimental evidence of radiosensitization by silver is summarised in {{REF:tab:lit_ag}}. In an early report, Xu et al. showed that AgNPs sensitize cancer cells to killing by ionising radiation [@xu2009]. Lu et al. obtained biocompatible AgNPs by egg-white-mediated green synthesis and observed enhanced radiation effects on cancer cells [@lu2012]. Glioma is the tumour model studied most often in this group of works. Liu et al. examined AgNPs as a novel radiation sensitizer for glioma in vitro and in vivo and proposed them as an alternative to gold [@liu2013]. In a subsequent study, AgNPs outperformed AuNPs in radiosensitizing U251 glioma cells in vitro and in an intracranial mouse model of glioma [@liu2016]. AgNPs also enhanced the efficacy of radiotherapy in hypoxic glioma cells [@liu2018], which are less sensitive to x-rays because of the oxygen effect [@hall2018], and functionalisation of AgNPs with polyethylene glycol (PEG) and the aptamer AS1411 enhanced their radiosensitizing effect in glioma irradiation therapy [@zhao2019]. Outside glioma, Swanner et al. found that the cytotoxic and radiosensitizing effects of AgNPs differ between triple-negative breast cancer cells and non-triple-negative breast cells [@swanner2015], and Habiba et al. used silver nanoprisms decorated with graphene as radiosensitizers to enhance the efficacy of colorectal cancer radiotherapy [@habiba2019].

The studies in {{REF:tab:lit_ag}} differ in the type and coating of the particles, for example PEG- and aptamer-coated AgNPs [@zhao2019] or graphene-decorated nanoprisms [@habiba2019], in the biological models and in the irradiation conditions. Their results therefore cannot be compared quantitatively with one another. Moreover, the physical dose enhancement depends strongly on the photon spectrum (Section 1.3), so that a biological enhancement ratio reported without a specified spectrum cannot be divided into physical and non-physical contributions. The comparison with gold [@liu2016] is particularly instructive. Per unit mass, silver and gold absorb photon energy comparably below about 12 keV, whereas between about 12 and 45 keV gold absorbs more strongly ({{REF:fig03_ratio}}). In megavoltage beams, on the other hand, the macroscopic dose enhancement is much smaller than in kilovoltage beams even for gold [@cho2005], because Compton scattering, whose mass coefficient depends only weakly on the atomic number, dominates there [@podgorsak2016]. For kilovoltage spectra below 45 keV and for megavoltage beams, a larger macroscopic physical dose enhancement is therefore an unlikely explanation for the advantage of AgNPs. This suggests that the chemical and biological activity of silver [@asharani2009] and nanoscale effects of energy deposition [@mcmahon2011] make a substantial contribution; confirming this requires a quantitative estimate of the physical part for a well-defined beam.

In the literature reviewed here, dosimetric studies of silver are fewer than biological ones. Taha et al. used MC simulation with a digital phantom to estimate the dose enhancement due to AgNPs implanted in a brain tumour during brachytherapy [@taha2019]. Çağlar et al. studied by MC simulation how the dose enhancement produced by AgNPs in a tumour affects the surrounding healthy tissues [@caglar2024], and Eşitmez et al. compared silver and gold nanoparticles for dose enhancement in layered skin geometries in an MC sensitivity study [@esitmez2026]. Merkis et al. investigated experimentally the dose sensitivity and the dose enhancement effect in dose gels enriched with AgNPs [@merkis2023]. The depth dependence of nanoparticle dose enhancement in orthovoltage beams, which is also relevant to silver, was examined by Chow and Jubran in an MC phantom study [@chow2023]. A systematic analysis of how the design and filtration of a kilovoltage x-ray tube determine the DEF of silver-loaded tissue has not been found in the literature reviewed in this work.

The choice of a beam for silver follows from its atomic data ({{REF:t2_2}}). The K-shell binding energy of silver is 25.514 keV, its L3 edge lies at 3.351 keV and its Kα1 line has an energy of 22.163 keV. Because the K-shell fluorescence yield is $\omega_K = 0.831$, only about 17 % of the K vacancies relax by Auger transitions, which deposit their energy close to the atom [@krause1979; @bambynek1972]. The remaining 83 % emit K x-rays, mainly Kα photons of about 22 keV, which have a mean free path of about 1.5 cm in soft tissue and carry this energy away from the absorbing atom. As shown in Sections 1.2 and 1.3, the ratio of the mass energy-absorption coefficients of silver and ICRU-44 soft tissue is 21–32 between 8 keV and the K-edge but 76–139 between the edge and 45 keV. The K-edge of gold lies at 80.725 keV and can be exploited only with tube voltages above about 81 kV, whereas the K-edge of silver lies inside the spectrum of any tube operated above about 26 kV. Kilovoltage spectra with a large fraction of the energy fluence between the Ag K-edge and the end-point energy of the beam, which is 45 keV for the tube considered here, are therefore the most favourable for the physical dose enhancement by silver. The Cu K lines at 8.0–8.9 keV, which dominate the photon fluence of the unfiltered beam of a copper-anode tube, lie far below this window.

## 1.5. Methods of modelling x-ray tube spectra

The calculation of the DEF requires the spectrum of the x-ray source. Spectra can be measured with semiconductor detectors [@birch1979], but such measurements require corrections for the detector response and are impractical for every tube configuration, so that parametric studies rely on calculated spectra. The calculation methods fall into four groups: empirical models, which interpolate measured or precomputed spectra; semi-empirical models, which combine a simplified physical description with parameters adjusted to measurements; physics-based deterministic models, which compute the spectrum from cross-sections and electron-penetration data; and full MC simulation of electron and photon transport in the tube. Their main features are compared in {{REF:tab:methods}}.

A representative empirical model is TASMIP (tungsten anode spectral model using interpolating polynomials), which generates tungsten-anode spectra from 30 to 140 kV with interpolating polynomials derived from measured data [@boone1997]. The spektr 3.0 tool provides spectrum modelling and analysis on the basis of the TASMICS model [@punnoose2016]. Empirical models are fast and accurate within the range of the underlying data, but they are bound to the anode material, anode angle and inherent filtration of the tubes for which the data were obtained. Added filtration can be included as an attenuation factor, but the influence of the anode material and angle, studied in this work, cannot be described.

Semi-empirical models go back to Kramers' theory of the continuous x-ray spectrum, according to which the energy emitted from a thick target per unit photon-energy interval is proportional to $Z(T_0 - E)$, where $Z$ is the atomic number of the target, $T_0$ is the initial kinetic energy of the electrons and $E$ is the photon energy [@kramers1923; @podgorsak2016]. Birch and Marshall developed a semi-empirical model of bremsstrahlung spectra and compared it with spectra measured with a lithium-drifted germanium (Ge(Li)) detector [@birch1979]. Tucker, Barnes and Chakraborty proposed a semi-empirical model for tungsten-target spectra (the TBC model) [@tucker1991]. Models of this group combine a simplified bremsstrahlung cross-section with an approximate description of electron penetration and of photon self-absorption in the target and adjust some parameters to experimental data. They are computationally cheap and show explicitly how the spectrum depends on the tube voltage, anode angle and filtration, but their accuracy is limited by the simplified physics and their parameters are specific to the target material, usually tungsten.

Physics-based deterministic models replace most empirical parameters with data derived from theory or from MC calculations. Poludniowski and Evans described the penetration of electrons into x-ray targets with characteristics derived from MC simulations [@poludniowski2007a], and Poludniowski used them to calculate x-ray production and filtration within the target [@poludniowski2007b]. This theory was implemented in the SpekCalc program for tungsten anodes [@poludniowski2009]. The later SpekPy v2 toolkit offers several physics models, among them the casim model used in this work, and several anode materials, including copper [@poludniowski2021]; in version 2.5.4 [@spekpy_soft] the copper anode is available for 10–50 kV. SpekPy relies on a model of K and L x-ray emission [@omar2018], and the same group developed a model of the energy and angular distribution of bremsstrahlung [@omar2020a] and validated the resulting spectra from 20 to 300 kV [@omar2020b]. SpekPy has also been validated against measured spectra and HVLs [@bujila2020]. Deterministic models are fast and physically well founded but cover only the anode materials and voltages for which precomputed transport data exist.

The most complete description is given by MC simulation of the coupled transport of electrons and photons in the anode and the tube. Ay et al. simulated diagnostic and mammography spectra with the MCNP4C code [@ay2004], and Verhaegen et al. modelled radiotherapy kilovoltage x-ray units with the EGS4/BEAM system [@verhaegen1999]. General-purpose codes for such simulations include EGSnrc with its condensed-history electron transport [@kawrakow2000], the Geant4 toolkit [@agostinelli2003] and PENELOPE [@salvat2019]. MC simulation accounts for electron backscattering, the angular distribution of bremsstrahlung and the real geometry of the tube. It requires, however, detailed geometric data and long computation times and is therefore better suited to benchmark calculations than to extensive parametric studies.

Whatever the model, the calculated spectrum must be related to measurable quantities. In kilovoltage dosimetry the beam quality is specified by the HVL together with the tube voltage [@hill2014], as in the protocol of the American Association of Physicists in Medicine (AAPM TG-61) for 40–300 kV x-ray beams [@ma2001] and in the code of practice of the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA TRS-398) [@iaea_trs398]. The standard radiation qualities of the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC 61267), such as the RQR series for diagnostic beams, are also defined by the HVL [@iec61267]. The HVL is therefore the natural quantity for comparing models with each other and with measurements. It does not, however, fix the fraction of the energy fluence above the Ag K-edge, so that two beams with similar HVLs can give different DEF values for silver; Chapter 3 shows this for filters with absorption edges ({{REF:t3_4}}).

Most of the models discussed above were developed for tungsten anodes [@boone1997; @tucker1991; @poludniowski2009], the usual target material of medical x-ray tubes [@bushberg2012]. Copper-anode tubes are used mainly in x-ray diffraction (XRD), where the Cu Kα line is the working radiation and the Kβ line is suppressed by a nickel filter [@cullity2001]. In XRD the bremsstrahlung continuum is an unwanted background, whereas for dose enhancement by silver it is the useful part of the beam, because only bremsstrahlung photons can exceed the Ag K-edge. In the literature reviewed here, the full spectrum of copper-anode tubes and its dependence on the tube parameters have received much less attention than those of tungsten-anode tubes. For this reason, a semi-analytical physics-based model of the copper-anode tube, called the developed model (DM), was constructed in this work. It combines the Bethe collision stopping power in the continuous slowing-down approximation (CSDA) [@bethe1930; @icru37], the non-relativistic Bethe–Heitler bremsstrahlung cross-section with the Elwert factor [@bethe1934; @elwert1939; @koch1959] and the Gryzinski K-shell ionisation cross-section [@gryzinski1965] with a single adjustable parameter, the electron depth factor (Chapter 2). All dependences on the tube parameters thus remain explicit, and SpekPy v2 serves as the reference for the validation of the DM.

## 1.6. Conclusions to Chapter 1

The review of the literature leads to the following conclusions, which determine the tasks of this work. The physical dose enhancement by a high-Z element in kilovoltage beams is governed by the ratio of its mass energy-absorption coefficient to that of tissue, which for silver increases from 21–32 between 8 keV and the K-edge at 25.514 keV to 76–139 between the edge and 45 keV. The physical efficacy of silver is therefore determined mainly by the fraction of the photon energy fluence above 25.514 keV, and a DEF value is meaningful only together with the spectrum for which it was obtained. AgNPs act as radiosensitizers in models of glioma, breast cancer and colorectal cancer, in one study more effectively than AuNPs, and they are themselves cytotoxic and generate ROS. However, the macroscopic physical contribution of silver for a well-characterised kilovoltage beam, which is needed to separate physical from chemical and biological effects, has not been determined systematically in the literature reviewed.

Spectrum models are well developed for tungsten anodes, whereas the full spectrum of copper-anode tubes, used mainly in XRD, has received much less attention. Accordingly, this work develops a semi-analytical model of the spectrum of a 45 kV copper-anode tube and validates it against SpekPy v2. Both models are then used to calculate the spectrum as a function of the tube design and filtration and to determine the DEF of silver-loaded soft tissue and its dependence on concentration, depth and filtration. The analytical results are verified by MC simulation, silver is compared with other high-Z elements, and the uncertainty of the DEF is estimated as a basis for practical recommendations.
