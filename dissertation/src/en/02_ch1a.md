# CHAPTER 1. RADIOSENSITIZATION BY HIGH-Z ELEMENTS AND X-RAY SPECTRUM MODELING: LITERATURE REVIEW

This chapter reviews the physical and radiobiological background of the work. It first considers the clinical problem that motivates the use of radiosensitizers and then the interaction of kilovoltage photons with biological matter. It further traces the development of radiosensitization by elements with a high atomic number (high-Z elements), from iodinated contrast agents to metal nanoparticles. The final sections review silver as a radiosensitizer and the methods of modelling x-ray tube spectra, which together define the problem addressed in this dissertation.

## 1.1. Radiotherapy and the problem of normal-tissue tolerance

Cancer is a major cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, as documented by the GLOBOCAN estimates for 36 cancer types in 185 countries [@bray2024]. Radiotherapy (RT) is, together with surgery and chemotherapy, one of the principal modalities of cancer treatment [@baskar2012]. An analysis of evidence-based clinical guidelines estimated that about half of all cancer patients, approximately 52 %, have an indication for RT during the course of their disease [@delaney2005]. The therapeutic action of RT is based on damage to cellular DNA, produced directly by secondary electrons or indirectly by the free radicals formed in the radiolysis of water, and double-strand breaks are the most lethal of these lesions [@hall2018]. Because energy deposition by ionising radiation is not selective, the same damage occurs in the healthy tissues traversed by the beam, and their response ultimately limits the dose that can be delivered to the tumour [@hall2018; @baskar2012].

The quantitative expression of this limitation is the therapeutic ratio [@hall2018]. The probabilities of tumour control and of normal-tissue complications both increase with dose along sigmoid dose–response curves, and the therapeutic ratio characterises the separation between the two curves [@hall2018]. When the curves lie close together, a dose escalation that would improve local control also produces an unacceptable rate of severe complications, so that the tolerance of the normal tissues and organs near the target sets an upper limit on the prescribed dose [@hall2018]. Normal-tissue toxicity is regarded as the main factor limiting the effectiveness of RT [@baskar2012]. The problem is aggravated for radioresistant tumours, in particular for tumours containing hypoxic cells, which are considerably less sensitive to x-rays than well-oxygenated cells because of the oxygen effect [@hall2018].

Several strategies have been developed to widen the therapeutic window. Fractionation of the total dose exploits the differences in repair and recovery between normal and tumour tissues [@hall2018; @baskar2012]. Conformal techniques, such as three-dimensional conformal and intensity-modulated RT combined with image guidance, shape the high-dose region to the target volume and reduce the irradiated volume of normal tissue [@khan2014; @baskar2012]. Neither approach, however, changes the ratio of the energy absorbed per unit mass in tumour cells and in normal cells located in the same region of the beam, for example in normal tissue infiltrated by the tumour. A third strategy acts on this ratio directly: radiosensitizers are agents that increase the effect of radiation on tumour cells more than on normal cells [@hall2018; @wardman2007].

Chemical radiosensitizers include the oxygen mimetics, such as the nitroimidazoles, which imitate the action of oxygen in hypoxic cells [@wardman2007]. Compounds of platinum, gold, ruthenium and other transition metals combine chemical and biological activity with the presence of heavy atoms [@gill2019]. A distinct, essentially physical principle underlies the use of high-Z elements. An element of this kind concentrated in the tumour absorbs a larger fraction of the energy of the incident photons than soft tissue does, so that the tumour dose increases while the irradiation of the surrounding tissues remains unchanged [@hainfeld2004; @kuncic2018]. The magnitude of this increase is expressed by the dose enhancement factor (DEF), the ratio of the absorbed dose in tissue containing the high-Z agent to the absorbed dose in the same tissue without it (Section 2.6). In contrast to chemical sensitization, this physical component of the effect can be calculated from interaction data once the radiation field is known.

The physical gain from a high-Z element depends strongly on the photon energy. Monte Carlo (MC) calculations for gold nanoparticles showed that the macroscopic dose enhancement is much larger with kilovoltage (kV) than with megavoltage (MV) x-ray beams [@cho2005], because the strongly Z-dependent photoelectric effect dominates only at low photon energies (Section 1.2). Kilovoltage x-rays are used in superficial and orthovoltage therapy [@khan2014], and the dosimetry of 40–300 kV beams in radiotherapy and radiobiology follows dedicated protocols in which the beam quality is specified by the half-value layer (HVL) [@ma2001; @iaea_trs398]. Since the enhancement is determined by the photon energy distribution, the DEF of an agent cannot be stated without specifying the x-ray spectrum, which depends on the design of the x-ray tube and on its filtration. The present work addresses this problem for silver irradiated with the beam of a 45 kV x-ray tube with a copper anode.

## 1.2. Interaction of kilovoltage photons with biological matter

Between a few kiloelectronvolts and 50 keV, photons interact with matter through photoelectric absorption, incoherent (Compton) scattering and coherent (Rayleigh) scattering, whereas pair production is impossible because its threshold, $2m_ec^2 = 1.022$ MeV, lies far above this range [@podgorsak2016; @attix1986]. The attenuation of a narrow photon beam is described by the mass attenuation coefficient, which is the sum of the partial coefficients of the three processes [@attix1986; @andreo2017]:

$$
\frac{\mu}{\rho} = \frac{\tau}{\rho} + \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{coh}}}{\rho} + \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{inc}}}{\rho}
$$ {#eq:mu_sum}

where $\mu$ is the linear attenuation coefficient, $\rho$ is the density of the medium, and $\tau/\rho$, $\sigma_{\mathrm{coh}}/\rho$ and $\sigma_{\mathrm{inc}}/\rho$ are the mass coefficients of photoelectric absorption, coherent scattering and incoherent scattering, respectively, all in cm²/g. For a mixture such as soft tissue, the mass coefficients are the sums of the elemental values weighted by the mass fractions of the elements [@attix1986]. Evaluated data for all elements and for many materials of dosimetric interest are tabulated by the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) [@nist_xraymass; @nist_xcom].

In the photoelectric effect the photon is absorbed by a bound atomic electron, which is ejected with a kinetic energy equal to the photon energy $E$ minus its binding energy [@podgorsak2016]. The interaction occurs preferentially with the most tightly bound shell that is energetically accessible, and between the absorption edges the mass photoelectric coefficient varies approximately as

$$
\frac{\tau}{\rho} \propto \frac{Z^{3}}{E^{3}}
$$ {#eq:tau_scaling}

where $Z$ is the atomic number of the absorbing element and $E$ is the photon energy; the corresponding atomic cross-section varies approximately as $Z^{4}/E^{3}$, and the exponents change slowly with $Z$ and $E$ [@podgorsak2016; @attix1986]. Per unit mass, a heavy element therefore absorbs photons far more strongly than the light elements H, C, N and O, which make up almost the entire mass of soft tissue [@icru44]. This scaling, however, holds only within one interval between absorption edges. Below the K-edge of a heavy element its K-shell electrons cannot take part in photoabsorption, and the ratio of its coefficient to that of tissue is much smaller than the atomic numbers alone would suggest.

Absorption edges are abrupt increases of $\tau/\rho$ at the photon energies equal to the binding energies of the atomic shells [@podgorsak2016]; for silver, the K-edge lies at 25.514 keV and the L-edges near 3.4–3.8 keV (L3 at 3.351 keV). The vacancy produced by photoabsorption is filled by an electron from an outer shell, and the released energy is either emitted as a characteristic (fluorescence) x-ray or transferred to another bound electron, which is ejected as an Auger electron or, for transitions within the same shell, as a Coster–Kronig electron [@bambynek1972; @krause1979]. The K-shell fluorescence yield $\omega_K$, the probability of radiative relaxation of a K-shell vacancy, increases rapidly with $Z$: it equals 0.039 for aluminium, 0.454 for copper, 0.831 for silver and 0.960 for gold ({{REF:t2_2}}) [@krause1979; @schoonjans2011]. For the light elements of tissue, whose K-shell binding energies are below 1 keV, practically all the absorbed energy remains near the interaction site as kinetic energy of photo- and Auger electrons. A heavy element, in contrast, re-emits a large part of the absorbed energy as fluorescence photons; Ag Kα photons (about 22 keV) have a mean free path of about 1.5 cm in soft tissue. The low-energy Auger electrons of a heavy atom deposit their energy in its immediate vicinity, which is important for the nanoscale effects discussed in Section 1.3.

In Compton scattering the photon transfers part of its energy to an atomic electron and is deflected; for a free electron the cross-section is given by the Klein–Nishina formula [@podgorsak2016; @attix1986]. At kilovoltage energies the scattered photon retains most of the incident energy, so that only a small fraction of it is transferred to the recoil electron. The binding of the atomic electrons reduces the cross-section at low energies and small scattering angles, which is described by the incoherent scattering function $S(x,Z)$, where $x$ is the momentum-transfer variable [@hubbell1975]. Rayleigh scattering by the atom as a whole changes only the direction of the photon, transfers no energy to the medium and is described by the atomic form factor $F(x,Z)$ [@hubbell1975]. In soft tissue photoabsorption dominates at the lowest energies and Compton scattering near the upper end of the kilovoltage range, whereas in silver photoabsorption dominates throughout the range considered here [@podgorsak2016; @nist_xcom].

Not all the energy of an interacting photon is absorbed locally. The mass energy-absorption coefficient $\mu_{en}/\rho$ includes only the energy that is transferred to charged particles and is not subsequently re-emitted by them as bremsstrahlung [@attix1986; @seltzer1993]:

$$
\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho} = \left[\frac{\tau}{\rho}\left(1-\frac{X}{E}\right) + \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{inc}}}{\rho}\,\frac{\bar{T}_{\mathrm{inc}}}{E}\right]\left(1-\bar{g}\right)
$$ {#eq:muen_def}

where $X$ is the mean energy emitted as fluorescence radiation per photoabsorption event, $\bar{T}_{\mathrm{inc}}$ is the mean kinetic energy of the recoil electron in incoherent scattering, $\bar{g}$ is the mean fraction of the kinetic energy of the secondary electrons converted into bremsstrahlung, and the remaining symbols are as in {{REF:eq:mu_sum}}. Coherent scattering makes no contribution, and for kilovoltage photons in low-Z media $\bar{g}$ is negligibly small [@attix1986]. The calculations of Seltzer, which account for the fluorescence emitted after photoabsorption, form the basis of the NIST tables of $\mu/\rho$ and $\mu_{en}/\rho$ used in this work [@seltzer1993; @nist_xraymass].

These coefficients link the radiation field to the dosimetric quantities defined by the International Commission on Radiation Units and Measurements (ICRU) [@icru85]. Kerma $K$ is the sum of the initial kinetic energies of the charged particles liberated by photons per unit mass, the collision kerma $K_{\mathrm{col}}$ is its part not converted into bremsstrahlung, and the absorbed dose $D$ is the mean energy imparted to matter per unit mass, measured in grays (1 Gy = 1 J/kg) [@icru85; @attix1986]. Under charged-particle equilibrium (CPE), when the energy carried into a small volume by charged particles is balanced by the energy carried out of it, the absorbed dose equals the collision kerma [@attix1986; @andreo2017]:

$$
D = K_{\mathrm{col}} = \Psi\,\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}
$$ {#eq:dose_cpe}

where $\Psi = \Phi E$ is the energy fluence of monoenergetic photons with energy $E$ and fluence $\Phi$, and $\mu_{en}/\rho$ refers to the irradiated medium; for a polyenergetic beam the right-hand side is integrated over the photon spectrum (Section 2.5). The secondary electrons released by photons below 50 keV have ranges in tissue much smaller than a millimetre, so that CPE is established very close to surfaces and boundaries. The Fano theorem, according to which the secondary-particle fluence in a medium of uniform atomic composition does not depend on density variations [@fano1954], further supports this approach. Two media exposed to the same photon fluence therefore receive absorbed doses in the ratio of their mass energy-absorption coefficients. This relation is the basis of the macroscopic, kerma-based DEF (Section 2.6), and it fails only within distances of the order of the electron ranges from a high-Z inclusion.

The coefficients of silver and of soft tissue with the composition of ICRU Report 44 [@icru44] are compared in {{REF:fig02_coeffs}}, plotted from the NIST tables [@nist_xraymass]. Over the whole interval silver exceeds tissue by more than an order of magnitude: the ratio of their mass energy-absorption coefficients increases from 21.0 at 8 keV to 31.8 at 25 keV. At the K-edge the mass attenuation coefficient of silver increases by a factor of 5.8, from 9.53 to 55.4 cm²/g, whereas its mass energy-absorption coefficient increases only by a factor of 2.4, from 8.54 to 20.3 cm²/g. The difference is caused by K-shell fluorescence: about 83 % of the K vacancies ($\omega_K = 0.831$) relax by the emission of K x-rays of about 22 keV, which carry part of the absorbed energy away and are therefore excluded from $\mu_{en}/\rho$. Above the edge $\mu_{en}/\rho$ of silver decreases more slowly than $\mu/\rho$, because the fluorescence energy, fixed by the line energies, becomes a progressively smaller fraction of the photon energy.

{{FIG:fig02_coeffs|Mass attenuation coefficients (solid lines) and mass energy-absorption coefficients (dashed lines) of silver and ICRU-44 soft tissue in the energy range 1–50 keV (NIST data). The vertical dashed line marks the Ag K-edge at 25.51 keV; the arrow indicates the L-subshell edges of silver.}}

For soft tissue the two coefficients practically coincide below about 10 keV, where photoabsorption dominates, and diverge at higher energies, where scattering, which deposits little or no energy, accounts for a growing share of the attenuation. As a consequence, the ratio of the mass energy-absorption coefficients of silver and tissue jumps at the K-edge from about 32 to about 76 and keeps growing above it, reaching 102.7 at 30 keV, 120.0 at 35 keV and a maximum of about 139 at 40–45 keV. The interval between the Ag K-edge and the maximum photon energy of the beam is therefore the region in which silver absorbs energy most efficiently relative to tissue. The fraction of the beam spectrum lying in this interval is the key characteristic of an x-ray beam intended for silver-enhanced irradiation, and its determination for a copper-anode tube is a central task of this work.

## 1.3. High-Z radiosensitizers: from iodine contrast to metal nanoparticles

The concept of dose enhancement by a high-Z medium in kilovoltage beams goes back to Spiers, who analysed how the increased energy absorption of bone and the ranges of the secondary electrons determine the dose to soft tissue within and near irradiated bone [@spiers1949]. This work introduced the two physical ingredients of all later dose-enhancement concepts: the enhanced transfer of photon energy to a high-Z material and the transport of this energy by secondary electrons into the adjacent soft tissue. Later studies quantified the dose perturbation caused by backscatter at interfaces between tissue and high-Z materials in kilovoltage photon beams [@das1995]. Such perturbations extend only over distances of the order of the electron ranges, beyond which the dose is again governed by the local photon fluence and the local energy-absorption coefficient.

The first therapeutic applications used iodine, which was available in clinically approved contrast media. Matsudaira et al. found that an iodinated contrast medium sensitized cultured mammalian cells to x-rays but not to gamma rays [@matsudaira1980], a dependence on radiation quality that pointed to the photoelectric absorption of low-energy photons by iodine. Mello et al. calculated the dose enhancement in iodine-loaded tumours for kilovoltage x-rays [@mello1983], and Solberg et al. computed dose enhancement factors for the dose enhancement therapy of brain tumours [@solberg1992]. The concept reached the clinic when a computed tomography (CT) scanner modified for therapy (CTRx) was used for the first radiotherapy of human metastatic brain tumours with iodinated contrast [@rose1999]. Monochromatic synchrotron beams then allowed the energy dependence to be studied directly: rats bearing F98 gliomas loaded with an iodinated contrast agent were treated by synchrotron radiotherapy [@adam2003], and the optimal monochromatic energy for the enhancement of cell radiotoxicity by stable iodinated compounds was determined experimentally [@corde2004]. Contrast-enhanced radiotherapy with kilovoltage x-rays was also analysed dosimetrically and microdosimetrically by MC simulation [@verhaegen2005], and modified megavoltage photon beams were combined with iodine and gadolinium contrast media [@robar2002].

Platinum-based drugs made it possible to combine a chemotherapeutic agent with a high-Z atom. Biston et al. reported the cure of rats bearing radioresistant F98 gliomas treated with cisplatin and irradiated with monochromatic synchrotron x-rays, exploiting the photoactivation of platinum at its K-edge [@biston2004]. Pignol et al. analysed the clinical significance of inner-shell ionisation and Auger cascades for radiosensitization with iododeoxyuridine (IUdR), bromodeoxyuridine (BUdR), platinum salts and gadolinium porphyrin compounds [@pignol2003]; when the heavy atom lies in or near DNA, the Auger electrons deposit their energy close to this critical target. Transition-metal complexes have since been studied widely as radiosensitizers that combine this physical mechanism with chemical and biological activity [@gill2019]. These studies showed that the subcellular location of the high-Z element, and not only its concentration, influences the outcome.

The modern stage of the field began with the study of Hainfeld et al., in which gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) 1.9 nm in diameter markedly improved the one-year survival of tumour-bearing mice irradiated with 250 kVp x-rays [@hainfeld2004]. Nanoparticles deliver a high local concentration of heavy atoms, and the key parameters that determine their efficacy, namely the material, size, coating and photon energy, have been analysed systematically [@retif2015]. Radiosensitization by AuNPs has been demonstrated in vitro with superficial kilovoltage x-rays [@rahman2009], with orthovoltage and superficial beams for folate-conjugated AuNPs in HeLa cells [@khoshgard2014], and with monoenergetic synchrotron beams, which were used to determine the optimal photon energy [@rahman2014]. Both the cellular uptake of AuNPs and the resulting radiosensitization depend on the particle size [@chithrani2010], and experiments with DNA in solution identified the photon energy, particle size and concentration as the governing parameters [@brun2009]. These results are summarised in several reviews [@mesbahi2010; @jain2012; @haume2016; @song2017; @penninckx2020].

The physics of AuNP radiosensitization has been studied mainly by MC simulation. Cho made the first MC estimate of the macroscopic tumour dose enhancement due to AuNPs in typical radiation treatments and found it to be much larger for kilovoltage than for megavoltage beams [@cho2005]. Zhang et al. quantified the tumour-selective dose enhancement for various photon sources [@zhang2009], Kakade and Sharma estimated it for therapeutic photon beams [@kakade2015], and Koger and Kirkby found the kilovoltage range to be optimal for AuNP-enhanced arc radiotherapy [@koger2016]. Lechtman et al. showed how the radiosensitization depends on the photon energy and on the size, concentration and location of the nanoparticles [@lechtman2011]. On the microscopic scale, the dose enhancement around AuNPs was found to be highly localised [@jones2010], and the spatial distribution of the secondary electrons produced by irradiated AuNPs was characterised with the Geant4 toolkit [@leung2011]. All these studies show a strong dependence of the enhancement on photon energy, which makes a reliable description of the incident spectrum a prerequisite of any dose-enhancement calculation.

The biological effect of high-Z nanoparticles, however, cannot be explained by the increase in the average dose alone. Radio-enhancement is usually divided into a physical stage, in which the particle absorbs additional photon energy and emits photoelectrons, Auger electrons and fluorescence photons; a chemical stage, with additional production of reactive oxygen species (ROS); and a biological stage, involving oxidative stress, perturbation of the cell cycle and inhibition of DNA repair [@kuncic2018; @her2017; @butterworth2012; @rosa2017]. McMahon et al. showed with a local effect model that the highly inhomogeneous nanoscale energy deposition near irradiated heavy-atom nanoparticles can explain biological effects that exceed the macroscopic dose enhancement [@mcmahon2011]. The sensitizer enhancement ratio (SER) measured in cell experiments may therefore be larger than the macroscopic DEF calculated for the same irradiation. A roadmap to the clinical use of AuNPs has summarised the corresponding recommendations and open issues [@schuemann2016]. The macroscopic DEF nevertheless remains the baseline quantity, because it sets the additional energy available to all later stages and is needed to separate the physical contribution from the chemical and biological ones.

Although gold dominates the literature, the choice of the element is not obvious. Roeske et al. characterised the theoretical, kerma-based dose enhancement from nanoparticles of various elements as a function of photon energy [@roeske2007], and McMahon et al. used physical modelling to optimise the choice of element for nanoparticle radiosensitizers [@mcmahon2016]. In the kerma approximation the comparison reduces to the ratio $R(E)$ of the mass energy-absorption coefficient of the element to that of tissue: for tissue containing a mass fraction $w$ of the element and irradiated by monoenergetic photons of energy $E$, the DEF equals $1 + w\,[R(E) - 1]$ (Section 2.6). The ratio $R(E)$ for silver, iodine, gadolinium, platinum, gold and bismuth is shown in {{REF:fig03_ratio}} for the interval 5–45 keV, which covers the spectrum of the 45 kV tube studied in this work.

{{FIG:fig03_ratio|Ratio of the mass energy-absorption coefficient of Ag, I, Gd, Pt, Au and Bi to that of ICRU-44 soft tissue in the energy range 5–45 keV (NIST data). The vertical dashed line marks the Ag K-edge at 25.51 keV.}}

Below about 12 keV the ratios for silver, platinum, gold and bismuth are close to one another and the ratio for iodine is somewhat higher, while gadolinium, after the jumps at its L-edges between 7.2 and 8.4 keV, has the highest ratio of the six elements. Since the Cu Kα lines at about 8.0 keV fall in this region, gadolinium absorbs the characteristic radiation of a copper anode particularly efficiently. The L-edges of platinum, gold and bismuth, located between about 12 and 16 keV, raise their ratios several-fold, and from about 16 keV up to the Ag K-edge these three elements exceed silver several times. The ratio for silver remains below about 32 up to its K-edge and is the lowest of the six between about 12 keV and 25.5 keV. At 25.514 keV it jumps to about 76 and then rises to about 139 at 40–45 keV. Just above the edge it equals the ratio for gadolinium, whose K-edge (50.239 keV) lies beyond the maximum photon energy of the beam, and it exceeds that ratio at higher energies; it also exceeds the ratio for iodine up to the iodine K-edge at 33.169 keV, above which iodine, like platinum, becomes comparable with silver at 40–45 keV, whereas gold and bismuth retain the highest values.

Two conclusions follow from this comparison. First, per unit mass, silver cannot be a more efficient physical sensitizer than platinum, gold or bismuth for broad kilovoltage spectra, and its advantage over iodine and gadolinium is confined to the window above 25.5 keV. Second, the physical efficacy of silver is controlled almost entirely by the fraction of the photon energy fluence above its K-edge: photons below the edge, including the Cu K lines at 8.0–8.9 keV, are absorbed by silver only 21–32 times more strongly per unit mass than by tissue, whereas photons above the edge are absorbed 76–139 times more strongly. The design parameters of the tube, and especially its filtration, which redistribute the energy fluence relative to 25.514 keV, are therefore expected to be decisive for the DEF, and this expectation is tested quantitatively in Chapter 3. The reasons for nevertheless considering silver, namely its biological activity and the experimental evidence of its radiosensitizing action, are reviewed in Section 1.4.
