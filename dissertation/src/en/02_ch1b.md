## 1.4. Silver and silver nanoparticles as radiosensitizers

Silver ($Z = 47$) lies between the light elements of soft tissue and the heavy elements, such as gold ($Z = 79$), that dominate the literature on high-Z radiosensitizers. Silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) have been reviewed with respect to their synthesis, properties and therapeutic applications [@wei2015] and their biomedical, including antimicrobial and anticancer, uses [@burdusel2018]; they are less expensive than gold and can be produced by green synthesis, for example with egg white as the reaction medium [@lu2012]. For radiotherapy, silver is of interest because of its biological activity and because its K absorption edge lies inside the spectra of x-ray tubes operated at a few tens of kilovolts.

The biological activity of AgNPs is closely related to their toxicity: they are cytotoxic and genotoxic in human cells, inducing ROS formation, DNA damage and cell-cycle arrest with involvement of the mitochondria [@asharani2009]. These effects correspond to the chemical and biological stages of radio-enhancement (Section 1.3), in which ROS, oxidative stress, cell-cycle perturbation and inhibition of DNA repair amplify the initial physical damage [@kuncic2018; @rosa2017], so AgNPs may sensitize cells beyond what their physical dose enhancement alone would produce. Their cytotoxicity, however, makes selective accumulation in the tumour a prerequisite of therapeutic use, and the effect of the tumour dose enhancement on the surrounding healthy tissues also has to be assessed [@caglar2024].

Experimental evidence of radiosensitization by silver is summarised in {{REF:tab:lit_ag}}. Since the early report by Xu et al. [@xu2009], glioma has been the most frequently studied model [@liu2013; @liu2016; @liu2018; @zhao2019], including hypoxic glioma cells, which are less sensitive to x-rays because of the oxygen effect [@hall2018]; breast [@swanner2015] and colorectal [@habiba2019] cancer models have also been used. Because the studies differ in particle type and coating, biological model and irradiation conditions, their results cannot be compared quantitatively. Moreover, since the physical dose enhancement depends strongly on the photon spectrum (Section 1.3), a biological enhancement ratio reported without a specified spectrum cannot be divided into physical and non-physical contributions.

The advantage of AgNPs over AuNPs [@liu2016] is particularly instructive. Per unit mass, silver and gold absorb photon energy comparably below about 12 keV, but between about 12 and 45 keV gold absorbs more strongly ({{REF:fig03_ratio}}); in megavoltage beams the macroscopic dose enhancement is much smaller even for gold [@cho2005], because Compton scattering, whose mass coefficient depends only weakly on the atomic number, dominates there [@podgorsak2016]. A larger macroscopic physical dose enhancement is therefore an unlikely explanation for the advantage of silver, both for kilovoltage spectra below 45 keV and for megavoltage beams. This points to a substantial contribution from the chemical and biological activity of silver [@asharani2009] and from nanoscale effects of energy deposition [@mcmahon2011]; confirming it requires a quantitative estimate of the physical part for a well-defined beam.

Dosimetric studies of silver are fewer than biological ones. MC simulations have estimated the dose enhancement by AgNPs implanted in a brain tumour during brachytherapy [@taha2019], its effect on the surrounding healthy tissues [@caglar2024] and the performance of silver relative to gold in layered skin geometries [@esitmez2026]; dose enhancement has also been measured in AgNP-enriched dose gels [@merkis2023], and its depth dependence in orthovoltage beams has been examined for nanoparticles in a phantom [@chow2023]. A systematic analysis of how the design and filtration of a kilovoltage x-ray tube determine the DEF of silver-loaded tissue has not been found in the literature reviewed.

The choice of a beam for silver follows from its atomic data ({{REF:t2_2}}): the K-shell binding energy is 25.514 keV, the L3 edge lies at 3.351 keV and the Kα1 line at 22.163 keV. Because the K-shell fluorescence yield is $\omega_K = 0.831$, only about 17 % of the K vacancies relax by Auger transitions, which deposit their energy close to the atom [@krause1979; @bambynek1972]; the other 83 % emit mainly Kα photons of about 22 keV, whose mean free path of about 1.5 cm in soft tissue carries this energy away. The ratio of the mass energy-absorption coefficients of silver and ICRU-44 soft tissue is 21–32 between 8 keV and the K-edge but 76–139 between the edge and 45 keV (Sections 1.2 and 1.3). Whereas the K-edge of gold (80.725 keV) can be exploited only above about 81 kV, that of silver lies inside the spectrum of any tube operated above about 26 kV. The most favourable spectra for silver therefore carry a large fraction of their energy fluence between the Ag K-edge and the end-point energy, 45 keV for the tube considered here; the Cu K lines at 8.0–8.9 keV, which dominate the photon fluence of the unfiltered copper-anode beam, lie far below this window.

## 1.5. Methods of modelling x-ray tube spectra

The calculation of the DEF requires the spectrum of the source. Measurement with semiconductor detectors [@birch1979] needs corrections for the detector response and is impractical for every tube configuration, so parametric studies rely on calculated spectra. The four groups of methods (empirical, semi-empirical, physics-based deterministic and MC) are compared in {{REF:tab:methods}}. The empirical model TASMIP (tungsten anode spectral model using interpolating polynomials) generates tungsten-anode spectra from 30 to 140 kV with polynomials derived from measured data [@boone1997], and the spektr 3.0 tool is based on the TASMICS model [@punnoose2016]. Such models are fast and accurate within the range of their data but bound to the anode material, anode angle and inherent filtration of the measured tubes; added filtration can be included as an attenuation factor, but the influence of the anode material and angle, studied in this work, cannot.

Semi-empirical models go back to Kramers' theory, in which the energy emitted from a thick target per unit photon-energy interval is proportional to $Z(T_0 - E)$, where $Z$ is the atomic number of the target, $T_0$ is the initial kinetic energy of the electrons and $E$ is the photon energy [@kramers1923; @podgorsak2016]. Birch and Marshall compared their model with spectra measured with a Ge(Li) detector [@birch1979], and Tucker, Barnes and Chakraborty proposed the TBC model for tungsten targets [@tucker1991]. These models combine a simplified bremsstrahlung cross-section with an approximate description of electron penetration and target self-absorption and fit some parameters to experiment. They are cheap and show explicitly how the spectrum depends on the tube voltage, anode angle and filtration, but their accuracy is limited by the simplified physics and their parameters are specific to the target material, usually tungsten.

Physics-based deterministic models replace most empirical parameters with data derived from theory or MC calculations. Poludniowski and Evans described electron penetration into x-ray targets with MC-derived characteristics [@poludniowski2007a], which were then used to calculate x-ray production and filtration within the target [@poludniowski2007b] and implemented for tungsten anodes in SpekCalc [@poludniowski2009]. The later SpekPy v2 toolkit offers several physics models, among them the casim model used in this work, and several anodes, including copper [@poludniowski2021], which version 2.5.4 provides for 10–50 kV [@spekpy_soft]. It relies on models of K and L x-ray emission [@omar2018] and of the energy and angular distribution of bremsstrahlung [@omar2020a], validated from 20 to 300 kV [@omar2020b] and against measured spectra and HVLs [@bujila2020]. Deterministic models are fast and physically well founded but cover only the anodes and voltages for which precomputed transport data exist.

MC simulation of the coupled transport of electrons and photons in the anode and the tube gives the most complete description, for example with MCNP4C for diagnostic and mammography spectra [@ay2004] or EGS4/BEAM for kilovoltage radiotherapy units [@verhaegen1999]; other general-purpose codes are EGSnrc [@kawrakow2000], Geant4 [@agostinelli2003] and PENELOPE [@salvat2019]. It accounts for electron backscattering, the angular distribution of bremsstrahlung and the real tube geometry. However, it requires detailed geometric data and long computation times and is therefore better suited to benchmarks than to extensive parametric studies.

Whatever the model, the calculated spectrum must be related to measurable quantities. In kilovoltage dosimetry the beam quality is specified by the HVL together with the tube voltage [@hill2014], as in the protocol of the American Association of Physicists in Medicine (AAPM TG-61) for 40–300 kV beams [@ma2001] and the code of practice of the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA TRS-398) [@iaea_trs398]; the standard radiation qualities of the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC 61267) are also defined by the HVL [@iec61267]. The HVL is therefore the natural quantity for comparing models with each other and with measurements. It does not, however, fix the fraction of the energy fluence above the Ag K-edge, so beams with similar HVLs can give different DEF values for silver, as Chapter 3 shows for filters with absorption edges ({{REF:t3_4}}).

Most of these models were developed for tungsten, the usual target material of medical x-ray tubes [@bushberg2012]. Copper-anode tubes are used mainly in x-ray diffraction (XRD), where the Cu Kα line is the working radiation and the Kβ line is suppressed by a nickel filter [@cullity2001]. In XRD the bremsstrahlung continuum is an unwanted background, whereas for dose enhancement by silver it is the useful part of the beam, because only bremsstrahlung photons can exceed the Ag K-edge; yet the full spectrum of copper-anode tubes and its dependence on the tube parameters have received much less attention than those of tungsten-anode tubes. For this reason, a semi-analytical physics-based model of the copper-anode tube, the developed model (DM), was constructed in this work. It combines the Bethe collision stopping power in the continuous slowing-down approximation (CSDA) [@bethe1930; @icru37], the non-relativistic Bethe–Heitler bremsstrahlung cross-section with the Elwert factor [@bethe1934; @elwert1939; @koch1959] and the Gryzinski K-shell ionisation cross-section [@gryzinski1965], with a single adjustable parameter, the electron depth factor (Chapter 2). All dependences on the tube parameters thus remain explicit, and SpekPy v2 serves as the reference for the validation of the DM.

## 1.6. Conclusions to Chapter 1

The review of the literature leads to the following conclusions, which determine the tasks of this work. The physical dose enhancement by a high-Z element in kilovoltage beams is governed by the ratio of its mass energy-absorption coefficient to that of tissue, which for silver rises sharply above the K-edge at 25.514 keV; the physical efficacy of silver is therefore determined mainly by the fraction of the photon energy fluence above this edge, and a DEF value is meaningful only together with the spectrum for which it was obtained. AgNPs act as radiosensitizers in glioma, breast and colorectal cancer models, in one study more effectively than AuNPs, and are themselves cytotoxic and generate ROS. However, the macroscopic physical contribution of silver for a well-characterised kilovoltage beam, which is needed to separate physical from chemical and biological effects, has not been determined systematically.

Spectrum models are well developed for tungsten anodes, whereas the full spectrum of copper-anode tubes has received much less attention. This work therefore develops a semi-analytical model of the spectrum of a 45 kV copper-anode tube, validates it against SpekPy v2 and uses both models to calculate the spectrum as a function of the tube design and filtration and the DEF of silver-loaded soft tissue as a function of concentration, depth and filtration. The analytical results are verified by MC simulation, silver is compared with other high-Z elements, and the uncertainty of the DEF is estimated as a basis for practical recommendations.
