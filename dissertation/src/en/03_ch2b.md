## 2.6. Dose enhancement factor: definitions and calculation

The physical radio-enhancement is quantified by the DEF, the ratio of absorbed doses at a point with and without silver under identical irradiation:

$$
\mathrm{DEF} = \frac{D_{t+\mathrm{Ag}}}{D_t}
$$ {#eq:def}

where $D_{t+\mathrm{Ag}}$ and $D_t$ are the absorbed doses in silver-loaded and unloaded (reference) tissue. This macroscopic DEF assumes a homogeneous silver distribution and doses averaged over volumes much larger than cells and electron ranges.

Below 50 keV secondary electrons have sub-millimetre ranges in tissue, so charged-particle equilibrium (CPE) holds except near interfaces [@attix1986; @andreo2017] and the dose equals the collision kerma {{REF:eq:dose_cpe}}, practically the kerma in low-Z media [@icru85]. By analogy with {{REF:eq:kerma}},

$$
D = \int_{0}^{T_0} \Psi(E)\,\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}(E)\,\mathrm{d}E
$$ {#eq:dose_spec}

where $\Psi(E) = E\,\Phi(E)$ is the spectral energy fluence, $\Phi(E)$ the spectral photon fluence {{REF:eq:fluence}}, $\mu_{en}/\rho$ the mass energy-absorption coefficient of the medium and $T_0$ = 45 keV the maximum photon energy. Since $\mu_{en}/\rho$ excludes the energy of scattered and fluorescence photons [@seltzer1993], the Ag K x-rays ($\omega_K = 0.831$) are assumed to escape. For a silver mass fraction $w$ the mixture rule gives [@nist_xraymass]

$$
\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_{t+\mathrm{Ag}} = (1-w)\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_t + w\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_{\mathrm{Ag}}
$$ {#eq:mixture}

where $t$ and Ag denote tissue and silver; mass fractions of 0.1–2 % are considered. Substituting {{REF:eq:dose_spec}} and {{REF:eq:mixture}} into {{REF:eq:def}} gives the surface DEF:

$$
\mathrm{DEF} = \frac{\int \Psi(E)\left[(1-w)\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_t + w\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_{\mathrm{Ag}}\right]\mathrm{d}E}{\int \Psi(E)\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_t\mathrm{d}E}
$$ {#eq:def_spec}

The fluence normalisation cancels, so the DEF depends only on the spectral shape, and DM spectra are usable despite their less accurate absolute output (Section 3.1). As the numerator is linear in $w$, {{REF:eq:def_spec}} becomes exactly

$$
\mathrm{DEF} = 1 + w\left(\langle R\rangle - 1\right)
$$ {#eq:def_lin}

with

$$
\langle R\rangle = \frac{\int \Psi(E)\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_{\mathrm{Ag}}\mathrm{d}E}{\int \Psi(E)\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_t\mathrm{d}E} = \frac{\int R(E)\,d_t(E)\,\mathrm{d}E}{\int d_t(E)\,\mathrm{d}E}
$$ {#eq:ratio_avg}

where $R(E) = (\mu_{en}/\rho)_{\mathrm{Ag}}/(\mu_{en}/\rho)_t$ and $d_t(E) = \Psi(E)\,(\mu_{en}/\rho)_t$ is the dose spectrum of unloaded tissue, so $\langle R\rangle$, the dose-weighted mean of $R(E)$, depends on the spectrum alone. For ICRU-44 tissue, $R(E)$ jumps at the Ag K-edge (25.514 keV) from about 32 to about 76 and reaches about 139 at 40–45 keV. Shifting the dose spectrum above the K-edge therefore raises $\langle R\rangle$ and the slope, the basis of the filter optimisation in Chapter 3.

Where silver is present only locally at depth $z$, without perturbing the beam, the primary-beam DEF is

$$
\mathrm{DEF}(z) = \frac{\int \Psi(E)\,e^{-\mu_t(E)\,z}\left[(1-w)\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_t + w\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_{\mathrm{Ag}}\right]\mathrm{d}E}{\int \Psi(E)\,e^{-\mu_t(E)\,z}\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_t\mathrm{d}E}
$$ {#eq:def_depth}

where $\Psi(E)$ is the surface energy fluence and $\mu_t(E) = \rho_t\,(\mu/\rho)_t(E)$ the linear attenuation coefficient of tissue of density $\rho_t$ = 1.06 g/cm³; scatter and inverse-square fall-off are neglected, and the denominator normalised to $z = 0$ is the relative primary dose in unloaded tissue. Tissue removes first the low-energy photons, for which $R(E)$ is small, so the DEF increases with depth. Silver filling the region above the point also attenuates the beam, most strongly just above the K-edge, where its $\mu/\rho$ increases 5.8-fold. For a slab loaded from the surface to beyond $z$,

$$
\mathrm{DEF}_{\mathrm{slab}}(z) = \frac{\int \Psi(E)\,e^{-\mu_{t+\mathrm{Ag}}(E)\,z}\left[(1-w)\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_t + w\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_{\mathrm{Ag}}\right]\mathrm{d}E}{\int \Psi(E)\,e^{-\mu_t(E)\,z}\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_t\mathrm{d}E}
$$ {#eq:def_slab}

where $\mu_{t+\mathrm{Ag}}(E) = \rho_t\left[(1-w)(\mu/\rho)_t + w\,(\mu/\rho)_{\mathrm{Ag}}\right]$ is the linear attenuation coefficient of the loaded medium (density approximated by $\rho_t$). The added attenuation preferentially removes the most effective photons, so $\mathrm{DEF}_{\mathrm{slab}}$ decreases with depth (self-shielding) and is no longer linear in $w$. At $w$ = 1 % silver attenuates just above its K-edge about as much as the tissue itself, and behind a loaded region the dose falls below its silver-free value.

The reference medium is ICRU-44 soft tissue (H 10.2, C 14.3, N 3.4, O 70.8, Na 0.2, P 0.3, S 0.3, Cl 0.2 and K 0.3 % by mass; 1.06 g/cm³) [@icru44], with water and the ICRU four-component tissue as alternatives; the choice affects the DEF (Section 3.10) and must be reported. On the subcellular scale, short-range photo- and Auger electrons deposit much of their energy close to nanoparticles [@jones2010], which can produce biological effects beyond the macroscopic enhancement (local effect model) [@mcmahon2011] depending on photon energy and particle size, concentration and location [@lechtman2011]. The calculated DEF is thus the physical baseline for identifying chemical, biological and nanoscale contributions.

## 2.7. Monte Carlo method for verification

The escape of Ag K-fluorescence and the neglect of scatter in the analytical model are tested with an independent MC code (`mc_dose.py`), simpler than PENELOPE, EGSnrc or Geant4 [@salvat2019; @kawrakow2000; @agostinelli2003] but including all photon interactions relevant below 50 keV. A laterally infinite 30 mm slab of ICRU-44 tissue is irradiated by a broad parallel beam at normal incidence; a layer at 2–12 mm contains silver with $w$ = 1 %, added as a partial density $\rho_{\mathrm{Ag}} = \rho_t\,w/(1-w)$. Photons are tracked by energy $E$, depth $z$ and direction cosine $u$. The SpekPy spectra of the 45 kV tube (θ = 12°, 0.5 mm Be, 10 cm air), unfiltered and with 1 mm Al, are sampled by inverting the cumulative distribution. Free paths are sampled by Woodcock (delta) tracking [@woodcock1965; @kalos2008] with the majorant $\mu_{\max}(E) = \mu_t(E) + \mu_{\mathrm{Ag}}(E)$, where $\mu_{\mathrm{Ag}} = \rho_{\mathrm{Ag}}(\mu/\rho)_{\mathrm{Ag}}$:

$$
s = -\frac{\ln \xi_1}{\mu_{\max}(E)}
$$ {#eq:mc_path}

where $s$ is the path length and $\xi_1$ a random number uniform in (0, 1); at the new depth $z + u\,s$ the collision is real with probability

$$
P_{\mathrm{real}} = \frac{\mu(z,E)}{\mu_{\max}(E)}
$$ {#eq:mc_real}

where $\mu(z,E)$ is the local coefficient ($\mu_t$ outside the layer, $\mu_t + \mu_{\mathrm{Ag}}$ inside it); otherwise it is virtual, so boundary distances are never computed.

The interaction type (photoabsorption in tissue or silver, incoherent or coherent scattering) is sampled from partial coefficients tabulated from xraylib [@schoonjans2011] at 1–46 keV in 5 eV steps. After photoabsorption in silver, a K vacancy (probability $\tau_K/\tau$, the K-shell share of the photoelectric cross-section) emits with probability $\omega_K$ one of the six principal K lines, chosen by radiative rates and transported isotropically; all other absorbed energy is deposited locally. The scattering angle $\vartheta$ is sampled from tissue tables of the Klein–Nishina cross-section times the incoherent scattering function and of the Thomson cross-section times the squared atomic form factor [@hubbell1975]; silver contributes only a few per cent of scattering. After incoherent scattering

$$
E' = \frac{E}{1 + \frac{E}{m_e c^2}\left(1 - \cos\vartheta\right)}
$$ {#eq:compton}

where $E$ and $E'$ are the photon energies before and after scattering and $m_e c^2$ = 510.999 keV is the electron rest energy; the direction cosine becomes $u' = u\cos\vartheta + \sqrt{1-u^2}\,\sin\vartheta\cos\varphi$ with $\varphi$ uniform in $[0, 2\pi)$. Photons below 1 keV and secondary electrons (ranges of a few tens of micrometres) deposit locally, giving the dose in bin $j$ per unit incident fluence

$$
D_j = \frac{1}{N}\,\frac{\sum_{i \in j} \varepsilon_i}{\rho_j\,\Delta z}
$$ {#eq:mc_dose}

where $\varepsilon_i$ are the energy deposits in the bin, $N$ the number of incident photons, $\rho_j$ the density ($\rho_t$ or $\rho_t + \rho_{\mathrm{Ag}}$) and $\Delta z$ = 0.25 mm the bin width. The MC DEF is the dose ratio for $w$ = 1 % and $w = 0$, from $4\times10^{6}$ histories in five batches whose spread gives the standard error; the analytical reference is {{REF:eq:def_slab}} applied to the buried layer. Two checks preceded the runs: for a broad 30 keV beam the ratio of surface dose to primary kerma is 1.31 owing to backscatter, which depends on field size and source distance [@grosswendt1990], and the energy-transfer coefficient from xraylib data agrees with the NIST $\mu_{en}/\rho$ within 0.3 % for O, C and H at 20–40 keV, where the two coefficients practically coincide.

## 2.8. Data sources, software implementation and validation strategy

All $\mu_{en}/\rho$ values and the $\mu/\rho$ of tissue, water and air are obtained from the NIST elemental tables [@nist_xraymass; @seltzer1993], for compounds by the mixture rule. The module `nist.py` interpolates them log–log between absorption edges, where the coefficients follow approximate power laws. Elemental $\mu/\rho$, edge and line energies, fluorescence yields, radiative rates and the MC cross-sections come from xraylib 4.3.0 [@schoonjans2011], whose $\mu/\rho$ agree with NIST [@nist_xcom] to better than 0.1 %.

The xraylib function `CS_Energy`, however, returns too low $\mu_{en}/\rho$ for light elements at 15–45 keV: for oxygen at 30 keV 0.108 instead of 0.173 cm²/g (−37 %) and for carbon 0.0515 instead of 0.0661 cm²/g, whereas for silver it agrees with NIST (16.58 versus 16.60 cm²/g). For tissue, composed mainly of O, C and H, it would underestimate the denominator of {{REF:eq:def_spec}} and overestimate the DEF: 1.62 instead of 1.48 for 1 % Ag with 1 mm Al. The xraylib cross-sections are unaffected (Section 2.7), but `CS_Energy` must be checked against NIST before use for low-Z media.

The reference spectrum model is SpekPy v2.5.4 [@poludniowski2021; @spekpy_soft] ("casim" physics model, copper anodes at 10–50 kV), validated against measured spectra and HVLs [@bujila2020]; its spectra validate the DM and are the principal DEF input. The code uses Python 3.13 with NumPy [@harris2020], SciPy [@virtanen2020] (minimisation and root-finding) and Matplotlib [@hunter2007].

The single adjustable parameter, the electron depth factor $\eta$ in {{REF:eq:depth}}, minimises the root-mean-square deviation between the DM and SpekPy bremsstrahlung spectra at 45 kV, θ = 12° and 0.5 mm Be, normalised to unit area above 3 keV and taken relative to the reference maximum; $\eta$ is searched in 0.25–0.7 and rounded to two decimals, all other configurations being predictions. The validation comprises component checks, e.g. of the stopping power against NIST ESTAR [@nist_estar] (Section 2.2); comparison with SpekPy for 25–50 kV and anode angles of 6–30°, with and without 1 mm Al, plus a tungsten-anode check; DEF comparison for DM and SpekPy spectra; MC verification; and a DEF sensitivity analysis.

## 2.9. Radiation safety when working with a kilovoltage x-ray tube

A 45 kV copper-anode tube emits very intense low-energy x-rays: the unfiltered air kerma at 10 cm is 383 mGy per mAs, i.e. 15.3 Gy/s at 40 mA, and the tissue surface receives 2 Gy in 0.12 s, so accidental hand exposure causes severe local injuries within seconds. The primary beam, scatter from the sample and holder, and housing leakage must be controlled. Protection follows the principles of justification, optimisation (as low as reasonably achievable, ALARA) and dose limitation of the International Commission on Radiological Protection [@icrp103], implemented in the Basic Safety Standards of the International Atomic Energy Agency [@iaea_gsr3] and, in Uzbekistan, in the Law "On Radiation Safety" [@uz_law_radsafety]. Occupational effective and equivalent doses (skin, extremities, eye lens) must stay below the limits.

The main measures are technical: a shielded housing, an enclosure whose door interlocks switch off the high voltage or close the shutter, warning lights, and fixed holders and collimators that keep hands out of the beam. Staff training, local rules, personal dosimetry and dose-rate surveys, particularly after maintenance, complement them. The high-voltage generator is a further hazard, and the beryllium window must not be touched (beryllium compounds are toxic).

Filtration also serves safety: the filters that markedly raise the DEF (1 mm Al to 0.2 mm Cu) lower the air kerma rate 120–1300-fold; with 1 mm Al the surface tissue kerma rate at 40 mA falls from 16.5 to 0.137 Gy/s and the time for 2 Gy rises from 0.12 to 14.6 s, controllable by a timer or shutter. The removed soft component would be absorbed superficially: the relative primary dose at 10 mm is 0.009 of the surface value without a filter and 0.44 with 1 mm Al. The DEF-maximising filter, whose beam can be specified by its HVL [@ma2001], thus also reduces unnecessary skin dose, in line with ALARA.

## 2.10. Conclusions to Chapter 2

1. A semi-analytical thick-target model (DM) of a reflection copper-anode x-ray tube has been formulated: Bethe electron slowing-down, non-relativistic Bethe–Heitler bremsstrahlung with the Elwert factor, direct and bremsstrahlung-induced K radiation, anode self-absorption, attenuation in window, air and filters, and one adjustable parameter, the electron depth factor $\eta$.
2. Air kerma and beam-quality parameters (HVLs, homogeneity coefficient, mean and effective energies) are calculated from the fluence spectra, the latter identically for DM and SpekPy.
3. Under CPE the DEF is a spectrum-weighted ratio of $\mu_{en}/\rho$; at the surface $\mathrm{DEF} - 1$ is exactly proportional to $w$, with a slope set by the dose spectrum above the Ag K-edge; the depth and loaded-slab forms describe beam hardening and self-shielding.
4. An independent MC code with Woodcock tracking, Compton and Rayleigh scattering and Ag K-fluorescence transport tests the assumptions of the analytical model.
5. NIST $\mu_{en}/\rho$ are used, because xraylib 4.3.0 `CS_Energy` underestimates them for light elements by up to 56 % (oxygen by 37 %), which would overestimate the DEF (1.62 instead of 1.48).
6. The unfiltered output (15.3 Gy/s at 10 cm and 40 mA) requires shielding, interlocks and dosimetric control; filtration reduces dose rate and superficial dose.
