# CHAPTER 2. PHYSICAL MODEL OF THE X-RAY SPECTRUM AND METHODS OF DOSE-ENHANCEMENT CALCULATION

This chapter presents the semi-analytical x-ray spectrum model developed in this work, referred to as the developed model (DM), and the methods used to characterise the beam and to calculate the dose enhancement factor (DEF). Sections 2.1–2.5 follow the radiation from the tube to the sample. Sections 2.6–2.9 describe the calculation of the DEF, the Monte Carlo (MC) verification, the data and software, and radiation safety. All formulas are given as implemented in the code of the DM (Appendix A), with energies in keV and lengths in cm unless stated otherwise.

## 2.1. Design and technical parameters of an x-ray tube with a copper anode

An x-ray tube is an evacuated envelope with two electrodes, the cathode and the anode, between which a high voltage is applied [@bushberg2012]. Electrons emitted thermionically by the heated cathode filament are shaped by a focusing cup into a beam that strikes a small area of the anode, the focal spot [@bushberg2012]. Accelerated by the tube voltage $U$, the electrons reach the anode with the kinetic energy $T_0 = eU$, where $e$ is the elementary charge; in the baseline configuration $T_0$ = 45 keV. Most of their energy is converted into heat, and only a small fraction is emitted as bremsstrahlung and characteristic x-rays [@bushberg2012; @podgorsak2016]. The tube current determines the number of electrons reaching the anode per unit time, so that the number of emitted photons is proportional to the product of the current and the exposure time, expressed in milliampere-seconds (mAs), whereas the shape of the spectrum does not depend on the current. All spectra in this work are therefore normalised to 1 mAs.

In the reflection-target tube considered here, the useful beam leaves the anode through the same face on which the electrons are incident ({{REF:fig01_tube}}), and the anode is thick enough to stop every electron. The anode angle $\theta$ is the angle between the anode surface and the central ray of the beam. Because the electron beam is perpendicular to the central ray, the electrons strike the anode at the angle $\theta$ to the surface normal, and the photons on the central ray leave the surface at the take-off angle $\theta$. A small anode angle reduces the effective size of the focal spot (the line-focus principle), but the photons emitted towards the anode side of the field travel longer paths in the anode, so that the beam on that side is weaker and harder (the heel effect) [@bushberg2012]. The DM calculates the spectrum on the central ray, where the anode angle enters through the geometry of electron penetration and of photon self-absorption (Sections 2.2 and 2.3).

The photons leave the vacuum envelope through a thin beryllium window, whose low atomic number and density give a weak attenuation of soft x-rays, while the window remains mechanically strong and vacuum-tight [@bushberg2012]. The window and the self-absorption in the anode form the inherent filtration of the tube. Added filters are metal foils placed in the beam outside the window; they remove the low-energy part of the spectrum and harden the beam [@bushberg2012; @khan2014]. The photons then cross an air gap to the sample at the focus–sample distance $r$ (labelled d in {{REF:fig01_tube}}; the symbol $d$ is reserved for the electron depth of Section 2.2).

{{FIG:fig01_tube|Schematic diagram of the modelled reflection-target x-ray tube with a copper anode and of the irradiation geometry. Electrons emitted by the cathode strike the anode at the focal spot; the photons emitted along the central ray at the anode angle θ pass through the beryllium window, the added filter and the air gap to the sample of tissue containing Ag. The focus–sample distance, marked d in the figure, is denoted r in the text.}}

Copper-anode tubes are standard sources for x-ray diffraction (XRD), which uses the intense Cu Kα line (8.05 keV, wavelength 0.154 nm) and suppresses the Kβ line (8.91 keV) with a thin nickel filter whose K absorption edge (8.33 keV) lies between the two lines [@cullity2001]. For irradiations with silver, however, the most useful photons lie above the Ag K-edge at 25.514 keV, in the bremsstrahlung continuum (Section 1.4), which in diffraction is merely a background. The whole spectrum must therefore be known and, as shown in Chapter 3, shaped by filtration. Because most published spectrum models were developed for tungsten anodes (Section 1.5), a dedicated model of the copper-anode tube is required.

The design and technical parameters of the modelled tube are listed in {{REF:t2_1}}. The baseline configuration is a copper anode with an anode angle of 12°, a 0.5 mm beryllium window, a constant potential of 45 kV, a current of 40 mA and a focus–sample distance of 10 cm in air, without added filtration; the table also gives the ranges studied in Chapter 3. The tube voltage, the anode material, the anode angle, the window and the added filters change the shape of the spectrum, and the distance acts both through the inverse-square law and through the attenuation in the air path. Under the assumption of a constant potential all electrons reach the anode with the same energy $T_0$; a voltage ripple would produce a weighted superposition of such spectra.

## 2.2. Electron slowing-down in the anode

Electrons with energies of a few tens of keV lose energy in the anode mainly by ionisation and excitation of the atoms, while elastic scattering by the nuclei changes their direction of motion. Radiative losses are small and are neglected in the slowing-down calculation. The DM uses the continuous-slowing-down approximation (CSDA), in which the mass collision stopping power is given by the Bethe formula [@bethe1930] in the form of ICRU Report 37 [@icru37]:

$$
\frac{S(T)}{\rho} = \frac{\kappa}{2}\,\frac{Z}{A}\,\frac{1}{\beta^{2}}\left[\ln\frac{\tau^{2}(\tau + 2)}{2\,(I/m_ec^{2})^{2}} + F^{-}(\tau)\right]
$$ {#eq:bethe}

where $S(T)$ is the linear collision stopping power for an electron with kinetic energy $T$, $\rho$ is the density of the anode, $\kappa = 4\pi N_A r_e^{2} m_ec^{2} = 0.307075$ MeV cm²/mol, $N_A$ is the Avogadro constant, $r_e$ is the classical electron radius, $m_ec^{2}$ = 510.999 keV is the electron rest energy, $Z$ and $A$ are the atomic number and the molar mass (g/mol) of the anode material, $\tau = T/m_ec^{2}$, $\beta$ is the electron velocity in units of the speed of light, $\beta^{2} = 1 - (1 + \tau)^{-2}$, $I$ is the mean excitation energy, and $F^{-}(\tau)$ is the electron term derived from the Møller cross-section:

$$
F^{-}(\tau) = 1 - \beta^{2} + \frac{\tau^{2}/8 - (2\tau + 1)\ln 2}{(\tau + 1)^{2}}
$$ {#eq:fminus}

For copper ($Z$ = 29, $\rho$ = 8.96 g/cm³) the value $I$ = 322 eV is used [@icru37], and the density-effect correction, which is negligible below 100 keV, is omitted. The formula gives 20.99, 13.18, 6.04 and 4.55 MeV cm²/g at 5, 10, 30 and 45 keV, respectively. As a check, the same code gives 6.603 MeV cm²/g for water at 50 keV, which is identical to the NIST ESTAR value [@nist_estar]. Shell corrections are not included, which affects only the lowest electron energies.

The path length travelled by an electron while slowing down from $T_0$ to $T$ is

$$
s(T) = \int_{T}^{T_0} \frac{\mathrm{d}T'}{S(T')}
$$ {#eq:csda}

where $T'$ is the variable of integration. The integral is evaluated by the trapezoidal rule with a step of 0.02 keV from $T_{\min}$ = 1 keV to $T_0$. The path to the lower limit, $s_0 = s(T_{\min})$, is practically the CSDA range; for 45 keV electrons in copper it equals 6.48 µm. If the logarithm in {{REF:eq:bethe}} is regarded as constant and $\beta^{2} \approx 2T/m_ec^{2}$, then $S \propto 1/T$ and {{REF:eq:csda}} gives $T_0^{2} - T^{2} \propto s$, which is the form of the empirical Thomson–Whiddington law [@whiddington1912].

Multiple elastic scattering makes the electron trajectories strongly curved, so the CSDA path is not the depth at which the photons are produced: the electrons diffuse into the target, and a considerable fraction of them is backscattered. Poludniowski and Evans derived the penetration characteristics of electrons in x-ray targets from MC simulations for the SpekCalc model [@poludniowski2007a]. The DM replaces such distributions by one mean depth for each electron energy, the projected depth

$$
d(T) = \eta\, s(T)\cos\theta
$$ {#eq:depth}

where $d(T)$ is the depth below the anode surface, measured along the normal, at which the electron has slowed down to the energy $T$, $\eta$ is the electron depth factor, and $\theta$ is the anode angle, which equals the angle of electron incidence measured from the surface normal (Section 2.1). The value $\eta = 1$ corresponds to straight-line motion, and $\eta < 1$ accounts for the diffusion of the electrons. This single adjustable parameter of the DM was fitted to the bremsstrahlung shape calculated with the SpekPy toolkit [@poludniowski2021] (Section 2.8), which gave $\eta$ = 0.40 and a maximum projected depth of 2.54 µm for 45 keV electrons at $\theta$ = 12°. Electron backscattering, which concerns about 30 % of the electrons in copper, is not modelled, and this contributes to the overestimate of the absolute output discussed in Section 3.1.

Panel (a) of {{REF:fig04_electron}} shows the stopping power and the projected depth for the baseline conditions. Because the stopping power is lowest at high energies and rises steeply as the electron slows down, the depth grows rapidly as the energy falls from 45 to about 20 keV and then approaches its maximum slowly. Photons with energies close to $T_0$ are therefore emitted only near the surface, whereas soft photons are produced along the whole path, including its deepest part. Since soft photons are also attenuated more strongly, they are the most affected by self-absorption in the anode. Panel (b) is discussed in Section 2.4.

## 2.3. Bremsstrahlung production in a thick target

Bremsstrahlung is emitted when an electron is decelerated in the Coulomb field of a nucleus, and the photon energy $E$ can take any value up to the electron kinetic energy $T$ [@podgorsak2016]. The classical theory of the continuous spectrum proposed by Kramers [@kramers1923] leads to the cross-section that is implemented in the DM in the form

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\mathrm{Kr}}}{\mathrm{d}E} = \frac{16\pi}{3\sqrt{3}}\,\frac{\alpha Z^{2} r_e^{2}}{\beta_0^{2}\,E}
$$ {#eq:kramers}

where $\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\mathrm{Kr}}/\mathrm{d}E$ is the cross-section per atom per unit photon energy (cm²/keV) for $E < T$ and zero otherwise, $\alpha$ is the fine-structure constant, and $\beta_0$ is the velocity of the electron with energy $T$ in units of the speed of light. With $\beta_0^{2} \approx 2T/m_ec^{2}$ and $S \propto 1/T$, integration over the slowing-down gives a number spectrum proportional to $Z(T_0 - E)/E$, i.e. an energy spectrum $E\,N(E)$ proportional to $Z(T_0 - E)$ that falls linearly to zero at $T_0$. This is Kramers' law, the starting point of the semi-empirical models (Section 1.5). The DM retains this cross-section only as an option.

Bethe and Heitler derived the bremsstrahlung cross-section in the Born approximation [@bethe1934]. Its non-relativistic, unscreened form, as compiled by Koch and Motz [@koch1959], is implemented as

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\mathrm{B}}}{\mathrm{d}E} = \frac{16}{3}\,\frac{\alpha Z^{2} r_e^{2}}{\beta_0^{2}\,E}\,\ln\frac{p_0 + p}{p_0 - p}
$$ {#eq:bhnr}

where $p_0$ and $p$ are the electron momenta before and after the emission, $p_0c = \sqrt{T(T + 2m_ec^{2})}$ and $pc = \sqrt{(T - E)(T - E + 2m_ec^{2})}$. In the non-relativistic limit the factor $1/\beta_0^{2}$ equals the factor $1/p_0^{2}$ of the original formula with momenta in units of $m_ec$. The Born approximation requires $2\pi Z\alpha/\beta_0 \ll 1$ and $2\pi Z\alpha/\beta \ll 1$, where $\beta$ is the electron velocity after the emission (kinetic energy $T - E$) in units of the speed of light [@koch1959]. This condition is violated for copper: with $Z\alpha \approx 0.21$ and $\beta_0 \le 0.4$, the parameter $2\pi Z\alpha/\beta_0$ exceeds 3 even for a 45 keV electron, and $2\pi Z\alpha/\beta$ is larger still. The resulting error is partly corrected by the Elwert factor, which approximately accounts for the Coulomb distortion of the electron wave functions [@elwert1939; @koch1959]:

$$
f_E = \frac{\beta_0}{\beta}\,\frac{1 - \exp(-2\pi Z\alpha/\beta_0)}{1 - \exp(-2\pi Z\alpha/\beta)}
$$ {#eq:elwert}

with $\beta_0$ and $\beta$ as defined above. The DM uses the product $\mathrm{d}\sigma/\mathrm{d}E = f_E\,\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\mathrm{B}}/\mathrm{d}E$, designated BH-E below. As $E$ approaches $T$, the momentum $p$ tends to zero, the logarithm in {{REF:eq:bhnr}} tends to $2p/p_0$ and the Born cross-section vanishes, while $f_E$ grows as $1/\beta$. The BH-E cross-section therefore remains finite at the high-energy end of the spectrum. The tabulated cross-sections of Seltzer and Berger for 1 keV–10 GeV electrons and $Z$ = 1–100 [@seltzer1986] include atomic screening and electron–electron bremsstrahlung, which the BH-E cross-section neglects. These simplifications contribute to the overestimate of the absolute yield of hard bremsstrahlung by the DM relative to SpekPy (Section 3.1). The DEF, however, depends only on the spectral shape, and after the fit of $\eta$ the DM reproduces the shape of the 45 kV SpekPy bremsstrahlung spectrum with a root-mean-square deviation of 2.46 % (Section 3.1).

In a thick target the emitted spectrum is the sum of the thin-target spectra produced along the whole slowing-down path. In the CSDA the electron passes $n_a\,\mathrm{d}T/S(T)$ atoms per unit area while its energy decreases by $\mathrm{d}T$, and a photon produced at the depth $d(T)$ is attenuated along the exit path $d(T)/\sin\theta$. Assuming isotropic emission, the number of bremsstrahlung photons per unit energy interval, per steradian and per incident electron emitted along the central ray is

$$
N_b(E) = \frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{E}^{T_0} \frac{n_a}{S(T)}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma}{\mathrm{d}E}(T, E)\,\exp\left[-\frac{\mu(E)\,d(T)}{\sin\theta}\right]\mathrm{d}T
$$ {#eq:thick}

where $n_a = \rho N_A/A$ is the number of atoms per unit volume of the anode, $\mathrm{d}\sigma/\mathrm{d}E$ is the BH-E cross-section, and $\mu(E)$ is the linear attenuation coefficient of the anode material, taken from the xraylib library [@schoonjans2011]. The same integral without the exponential factor and without the factor $1/4\pi$ gives the generated spectrum $N_g(E)$, i.e. the photons produced in all directions, which is used in Section 2.4. The integrals are evaluated by the trapezoidal rule, and the photon spectrum is calculated in bins of 0.05 keV from 1 keV to $T_0$. A similar thick-target formulation, with MC-derived electron penetration data, is the basis of SpekCalc [@poludniowski2007a; @poludniowski2007b].

The exponential factor in {{REF:eq:thick}} describes the self-absorption in the anode. The exit path grows as the anode angle decreases, and the attenuation is strongest for soft photons and for photons just above the copper K-edge at 8.98 keV, where $\mu$ increases abruptly. The factor $1/4\pi$ expresses the assumption of isotropic emission. Although the bremsstrahlung from a single interaction is anisotropic, multiple scattering in a thick target randomises the electron directions and smooths the angular distribution. The isotropic approximation, which more detailed models of x-ray tube bremsstrahlung avoid [@omar2020a], is one of the limitations of the DM (Section 3.11).

## 2.4. Characteristic radiation

When the electron energy exceeds the K-shell binding energy $E_K$ (8.979 keV for copper), the electron can ionise the K shell. The vacancy is filled from an outer shell, and the transition energy is emitted either as a characteristic photon, with the probability given by the fluorescence yield $\omega_K$ (0.454 for copper), or as an Auger electron [@bambynek1972; @krause1979]. The DM includes the four strongest lines, K$\alpha_1$ (K–L$_3$ transition, 8.048 keV), K$\alpha_2$ (K–L$_2$, 8.028 keV), K$\beta_1$ (K–M$_3$) and K$\beta_3$ (K–M$_2$), the last two at 8.905 keV, whose relative intensities $p_j$, the radiative rates normalised over the four lines, are 0.583, 0.299, 0.078 and 0.040. K vacancies are produced both directly by electron impact and indirectly by the absorption of bremsstrahlung photons above $E_K$. The copper L lines (below 1 keV) are absorbed in the beryllium window and are not modelled.

The K-shell ionisation cross-section is calculated with the binary-encounter formula of Gryziński [@gryzinski1965]:

$$
\sigma_K(T) = \frac{\pi e^{4} n_K}{E_K^{2}}\,\frac{1}{U_K}\left(\frac{U_K - 1}{U_K + 1}\right)^{3/2}\left[1 + \frac{2}{3}\left(1 - \frac{1}{2U_K}\right)\ln\left(2.7 + \sqrt{U_K - 1}\right)\right]
$$ {#eq:gryz}

where $U_K = T/E_K$ is the overvoltage ratio ($\sigma_K = 0$ for $U_K \le 1$), $n_K = 2$ is the number of K-shell electrons, and $\pi e^{4} = 6.5141\times10^{-20}$ cm² keV² in Gaussian units. As shown in panel (b) of {{REF:fig04_electron}}, the cross-section of copper rises from 25.5 b at 10 keV to 288 b at 20 keV, has a broad maximum of about 351 b near 35 keV and decreases to 339 b at 45 keV. Integration of $n_a\sigma_K/S$ over the slowing-down gives 0.0159 K ionisations per incident 45 keV electron. Experimental and theoretical cross-sections have been compiled by Llovet et al. [@llovet2014], and the distorted-wave Born calculations of Bote and Salvat are used in the PENELOPE code [@bote2008]. Because the Gryziński formula rises too slowly near the threshold, the DM underestimates the Cu K-line intensity by about 25–43 % relative to SpekPy, whose model of K and L emission [@omar2018] relies on cross-sections of this distorted-wave type; the deficit is largest at low tube voltages (Section 3.1).

The edge and line energies, fluorescence yields and radiative rates are taken from xraylib [@schoonjans2011]. Fluorescence yields were compiled by Krause [@krause1979] and evaluated transition energies by Deslattes et al. [@deslattes2003]. The data for the anode, the window, the main filter materials and several high-Z radiosensitizers are collected in {{REF:t2_2}}, which includes the nickel K-edge (8.333 keV) discussed in Section 2.1. The K-edges of molybdenum (20.000 keV), silver (25.514 keV) and tin (29.200 keV) lie close to the Ag K-edge, so that filters of these elements strongly absorb part of the photons that are most effective for dose enhancement by silver (Section 3.4).

{{TABLE:t2_2}}

The direct yield of line $j$, emitted along the central ray per steradian and per incident electron, is

$$
N_j^{\mathrm{dir}} = \frac{\omega_K\,p_j}{4\pi}\int_{E_K}^{T_0} \frac{n_a\,\sigma_K(T)}{S(T)}\,\exp\left[-\frac{\mu_j\,d(T)}{\sin\theta}\right]\mathrm{d}T
$$ {#eq:charK}

where $\mu_j = \mu(E_j)$ is the linear attenuation coefficient of the anode at the line energy $E_j$, and the other symbols are as in {{REF:eq:thick}}. Because the K lines lie below the K-edge of copper, the anode is relatively transparent to its own lines, and the line photons cannot themselves ionise the K shell.

The indirect fluorescence is excited by bremsstrahlung photons with $E > E_K$ that are absorbed in the anode by the K-shell photoelectric effect. The electrons penetrate only a few micrometres (Section 2.2), whereas the attenuation lengths of photons above the K-edge are comparable to or larger than this depth. The bremsstrahlung is therefore represented by a point source on the anode surface (the surface-source approximation), and half of the isotropically emitted photons enter the anode. Let $u = \cos\vartheta$, where $\vartheta$ is the angle between the photon direction and the inward normal; for isotropic emission $u$ has the probability density 1/2 on the interval $-1 \le u \le 1$. A photon with $u > 0$ reaches the depth $z$ after the path $z/u$ and interacts between $z$ and $z + \mathrm{d}z$ with the probability $(\mu/u)\exp(-\mu z/u)\,\mathrm{d}z$, where $\mu = \mu(E)$. Averaging over the directions gives the probability density of the interaction depth per emitted photon:

$$
P(z) = \frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1} \frac{\mu}{u}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mu z/u}\,\mathrm{d}u = \frac{\mu}{2}\,\mathrm{E}_1(\mu z)
$$ {#eq:absdepth}

where the substitution $t = 1/u$ has been made and $\mathrm{E}_1(x) = \int_{1}^{\infty} t^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{-xt}\,\mathrm{d}t$ is the exponential integral. Since $\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{E}_1(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = 1$, the integral of $P(z)$ over all depths equals 1/2: every photon entering the semi-infinite anode interacts in it.

A fraction $\tau_K(E)/\mu(E)$ of these interactions are K-shell photoabsorptions, where $\tau_K(E)$ is the linear K-shell photoelectric coefficient of copper, (the density multiplied by the partial K-shell photoionisation mass coefficient from xraylib), and $\mu(E)$ is the total linear attenuation coefficient. Each vacancy produces a photon of line $j$ with the probability $\omega_K p_j$, and this photon, emitted from the depth $z$ along the central ray, is attenuated by the factor $\exp(-b_j z)$, where $b_j = \mu_j/\sin\theta$. The yield of line $j$ per steradian along the central ray per generated bremsstrahlung photon of energy $E$ is therefore

$$
Y_j(E) = \frac{\omega_K\,p_j}{4\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty} \frac{\tau_K(E)}{\mu(E)}\,P(z)\,\mathrm{e}^{-b_j z}\,\mathrm{d}z = \frac{\omega_K\,p_j}{4\pi}\,\frac{\tau_K(E)}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty} \mathrm{E}_1(\mu z)\,\mathrm{e}^{-b_j z}\,\mathrm{d}z
$$ {#eq:indyield}

The remaining integral is a Laplace transform of the exponential integral. Interchanging the order of integration gives $\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{E}_1(x)\,\mathrm{e}^{-sx}\,\mathrm{d}x = \int_{1}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t/[t(t + s)] = \ln(1 + s)/s$, and the substitution $x = \mu z$ with $s = b_j/\mu$ yields

$$
\int_{0}^{\infty} \mathrm{E}_1(\mu z)\,\mathrm{e}^{-b_j z}\,\mathrm{d}z = \frac{1}{b_j}\ln\left(1 + \frac{b_j}{\mu}\right)
$$ {#eq:laplace}

Weighting $Y_j(E)$ with the generated spectrum and integrating over the photon energies above the K-edge gives the indirect yield per steradian and per incident electron:

$$
N_j^{\mathrm{ind}} = \frac{\omega_K\,p_j}{4\pi}\int_{E_K}^{T_0} N_g(E)\,\frac{\tau_K(E)}{2\,b_j}\,\ln\left[1 + \frac{b_j}{\mu(E)}\right]\mathrm{d}E
$$ {#eq:indirect}

where $N_g(E)$ is the generated bremsstrahlung spectrum defined in Section 2.3. The total yield of line $j$ is $N_j = N_j^{\mathrm{dir}} + N_j^{\mathrm{ind}}$. For the baseline tube the indirect contribution is 4.5 % of the Kα intensity and 4.8 % of the Kβ intensity. The share is slightly larger for Kβ because the indirect vacancies lie on average deeper than the direct ones and Kβ photons are attenuated less in the anode than Kα photons. Since the indirect contribution is small, the surface-source approximation and the neglect of scattered photons have only a minor effect on the line intensities.

## 2.5. Filtration, beam-quality parameters and air kerma

Between the anode and the sample the photons cross the beryllium window, the added filters and the air. Their attenuation is described by the Beer–Lambert law for narrow-beam geometry, in which scattered photons are assumed not to reach the point of interest:

$$
\mathcal{T}(E) = \exp\left[-\mu_{\mathrm{Be}}(E)\,t_{\mathrm{Be}} - \sum_{i}\mu_i(E)\,t_i - \mu_{\mathrm{air}}(E)\,r\right]
$$ {#eq:filter}

where $\mathcal{T}(E)$ is the transmission at the photon energy $E$, $\mu_{\mathrm{Be}}$, $\mu_i$ and $\mu_{\mathrm{air}}$ are the linear attenuation coefficients of beryllium, of the $i$-th added filter and of air, $t_{\mathrm{Be}}$ and $t_i$ are the thicknesses of the window and of the filters, and $r$ is the focus–sample distance. For the elements, the linear coefficients are the total mass attenuation coefficients from xraylib, which include coherent scattering, multiplied by the densities given in {{REF:t2_2}}. For dry air, the coefficients are obtained by the mixture rule from the NIST data for its constituent elements (C, N, O and Ar) [@nist_xraymass], with the density $1.205\times10^{-3}$ g/cm³. The whole distance is treated as an air path, which slightly overestimates the air attenuation.

The spectral photon fluence at the sample combines the yields of Sections 2.3 and 2.4 with the transmission and the inverse-square law:

$$
\Phi(E) = \frac{n_e\,Q}{r^{2}}\,\mathcal{T}(E)\left[N_b(E) + \sum_{j} N_j\,\delta(E - E_j)\right]
$$ {#eq:fluence}

where $\Phi(E)$ is the number of photons per unit area and per unit energy interval (cm$^{-2}$ keV$^{-1}$), $Q$ is the exposure in mAs, $n_e = 6.242\times10^{15}$ is the number of electrons per mAs, $N_j$ is the total yield of line $j$, and $\delta$ is the Dirac delta function. The factor $1/r^{2}$ converts the yield per steradian of a point source into the fluence at the distance $r$. In the numerical spectrum each delta function is replaced by the value $N_j/\Delta E$ in the bin of width $\Delta E$ = 0.05 keV that contains $E_j$. The spectral energy fluence is $\Psi(E) = E\,\Phi(E)$.

The output of the tube is characterised by the air kerma [@attix1986; @icru85]:

$$
K_{\mathrm{air}} = \int_{0}^{T_0} \Phi(E)\,E\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_{\mathrm{air}}\mathrm{d}E
$$ {#eq:kerma}

where $(\mu_{en}/\rho)_{\mathrm{air}}$ is the mass energy-absorption coefficient of dry air, obtained in the same way [@nist_xraymass]; the result in keV/g is converted to grays with 1 keV/g = $1.602\times10^{-13}$ Gy. Strictly, {{REF:eq:kerma}} gives the collision kerma, which for kilovoltage photons in air practically equals the total kerma, because the radiative losses of the secondary electrons are negligible [@attix1986]. With the coefficient of ICRU-44 soft tissue [@icru44] instead of air, the same expression gives the tissue kerma at the sample surface without backscatter, which is used in Section 3.6 to estimate exposure times. For the SpekPy spectra, which are filtered with the attenuation data of SpekPy itself, the air kerma calculated by SpekPy is used, whereas the HVLs and mean energies of all copper-anode spectra are evaluated with the same routines for both models.

The quality of kilovoltage beams is specified primarily by the first half-value layer (HVL), the thickness of a reference absorber, usually aluminium or copper, that reduces the air kerma of a narrow beam to one half [@ma2001; @iaea_trs398; @khan2014]. Standard radiation qualities of diagnostic beams are defined in the same way [@iec61267]. The first HVL in aluminium, HVL1 = $x_1$, is found from the condition

$$
\int_{0}^{T_0} \Phi(E)\,E\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_{\mathrm{air}}\exp\left[-\mu_{\mathrm{Al}}(E)\,x_1\right]\mathrm{d}E = \frac{1}{2}\,K_{\mathrm{air}}
$$ {#eq:hvl}

where $\mu_{\mathrm{Al}}(E)$ is the linear attenuation coefficient of aluminium. The thickness $x_2$ that reduces the air kerma to one quarter is found in the same way, the second HVL is HVL2 = $x_2 - x_1$, and both equations are solved by bisection. The homogeneity coefficient $h$ = HVL1/HVL2 equals unity for a monoenergetic beam and is normally smaller for a polyenergetic beam, because the first layers of the absorber remove the soft photons and harden the beam. The effective energy $E_{\mathrm{eff}}$ is the energy of the monoenergetic beam with the same first HVL; it is found from $\mu_{\mathrm{Al}}(E_{\mathrm{eff}}) = \ln 2/\mathrm{HVL1}$ by Brent's method.

The mean energy of the photon fluence spectrum is

$$
\bar{E} = \frac{\int_{0}^{T_0} E\,\Phi(E)\,\mathrm{d}E}{\int_{0}^{T_0} \Phi(E)\,\mathrm{d}E}
$$ {#eq:emean}

where both integrals include the characteristic lines. For the study of dose enhancement by silver, the fraction of photons above the Ag K-edge, $f_{\mathrm{Ag}} = \int_{E_{K,\mathrm{Ag}}}^{T_0}\Phi(E)\,\mathrm{d}E \big/ \int_{0}^{T_0}\Phi(E)\,\mathrm{d}E$ with $E_{K,\mathrm{Ag}}$ = 25.514 keV, and the corresponding fraction of the energy fluence are also evaluated. These quantities characterise the part of the spectrum in which the ratio of the mass energy-absorption coefficients of silver and tissue is largest (Sections 1.2 and 1.3). The HVLs, the mean energy and the DEF depend only on the shape of the spectrum and are therefore insensitive to errors in the absolute output.
