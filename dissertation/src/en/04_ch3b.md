## 3.7. Depth dependence and self-shielding

As the Ag-loaded target in samples, phantoms or animals lies beneath tissue, two cases were calculated with SpekPy spectra in the primary-beam kerma approximation (scatter is assessed in Section 3.9): a small loaded region at depth $z$ in Ag-free tissue (equation {{REF:eq:def_depth}}) and a region loaded uniformly from the surface to $z$ (equation {{REF:eq:def_slab}}). In the first case the DEF increases monotonically with depth ({{REF:fig18_def_depth}}; {{REF:t3_8}} also gives the dose $D/D_0$ relative to the surface dose $D_0$), because tissue preferentially absorbs low-energy photons, shifting the fluence to where the Ag/tissue $\mu_{en}/\rho$ ratio is larger. Without a filter the DEF for 1 % Ag rises from 1.20 to 1.56 at 20 mm, but the dose falls to 0.0090 at 10 mm as the Cu K lines and soft bremsstrahlung are absorbed: tissue acts as an added filter (Section 3.4). With Al 1 mm, which has already hardened the beam, the DEF rises only to 1.69 at 20 mm and the dose falls only to 0.439 at 10 mm.

{{FIG:fig18_def_depth|Dose enhancement factor for 1 % Ag versus depth of the Ag-loaded region in Ag-free soft tissue for the unfiltered 45 kV beam and four added filters (SpekPy, primary beam, kerma approximation).}}

{{TABLE:t3_8}}

Cu and Sn are the extremes. The 0.1 mm Cu beam comprises the Cu Kα line and a hard tail (Section 3.4); once tissue absorbs the Kα line, the DEF rises steeply from 1.29 to 1.77 at 5 mm, near the surface value with 0.2 mm Cu (1.80), and beyond about 2 mm exceeds all others. With Sn 0.1 mm it changes least (1.44 to 1.57 at 20 mm), since photons above the Sn K-edge (29.2 keV) are already strongly attenuated and tissue barely hardens the remaining narrow band. Surface rankings of filters thus fail for deep-seated targets.

Inside a region loaded from the surface the trend reverses ({{REF:fig19_def_slab}}): Ag strongly attenuates the photons above its K-edge that produce the enhancement (its mass attenuation coefficient rises 5.8-fold at 25.514 keV, from 9.53 to 55.4 cm²/g). With Al 1 mm the DEF falls from 1.48 to 1.21 at 10 mm for 1 % Ag and from 1.96 to 1.27 for 2 % Ag; self-shielding grows with concentration, and the linear relation of equation {{REF:eq:def_lin}} holds only for thin layers. The Sn-filtered beam, concentrated just above the Ag K-edge, loses more (1.44 to 1.15 at 10 mm, 1 % Ag). Enhancement in a thick loaded region is thus non-uniform and on average much lower than at the surface.

{{FIG:fig19_def_slab|Dose enhancement factor versus depth inside a uniformly Ag-loaded region starting at the surface: 0.5, 1.0 and 2.0 % Ag, Al 1.0 mm (solid) and Sn 0.1 mm (dashed) filters, self-shielding included.}}

## 3.8. Influence of tube voltage and comparison with other high-Z elements

The DEF versus tube voltage $U$ ({{REF:fig20_def_kvp}}, {{REF:t3_10}}) was calculated with SpekPy for 20–50 kV, where it supports the Cu anode, and with the DM for 60–80 kV. Photons above the Ag K-edge exist only above 25.5 kV, as the maximum photon energy is $eU$, and below this voltage the DEF changes little. The slope increases from 26–27 kV; between 30 and 50 kV the DEF with Al 1 mm rises from 1.31 to 1.52 ever more slowly (±0.009 per ±1 kV at 45 kV, Section 3.10), and above 50 kV the growth slows further as the spectrum extends beyond the maximum of the Ag/tissue ratio near 40–45 keV. As the DM gives slightly lower DEFs than SpekPy at 45 kV (1.464 versus 1.482), the 60–80 kV values are, if anything, underestimated, and part of the flattening between 50 and 60 kV reflects the change of model.

For tissue with 1 % by mass of I, Gd, Pt, Au or Bi ({{REF:fig21_elements}}, {{REF:t3_9}}) the unfiltered beam gives 1.20–1.33, as the dose comes from the Cu K lines and photons below 15 keV, where Pt, Au and Bi (L-edges at about 12–16 keV) absorb about as much energy per unit mass as Ag ({{REF:fig03_ratio}}); only Gd (1.33), whose L-edges at 7.2–8.4 keV absorb the Cu K lines, stands out. With Al 1 mm the DEF becomes 2.13–2.23 for Pt, Au and Bi and 1.71 for Gd, against 1.48 for Ag and 1.46 for I. Above their L-edges Pt, Au and Bi absorb more than Ag over the whole filtered spectrum, including 15–25 keV, where Ag absorbs only through its L and outer shells; Gd (K-edge 50.2 keV, beyond the spectrum) exceeds Ag only below the Ag K-edge, which still contributes much of the dose, and iodine resembles Ag with a higher K-edge (33.2 keV).

Sn 0.1 mm lowers the DEF for Ag and I (1.44 for both) by attenuating photons above 29.2 keV, where the Ag/tissue ratio reaches 100–139 and the iodine K-edge lies, but slightly raises it for the heavier elements, whose ratio varies less, through stronger suppression of soft photons (HVL 0.90 versus 0.83 mm Al). Per unit mass, Ag is thus a weaker physical sensitizer at 45 kV than Gd, Pt, Au and Bi and comparable to iodine. Its advantages lie elsewhere: a K-edge inside the spectrum, allowing tuning by filtration; biologically active silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) that generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) [@asharani2009]; and a lower cost than gold or platinum.

## 3.9. Monte Carlo verification

The analytical DEFs neglect scatter and assume that the energy of Ag K-fluorescence, excluded from $\mu_{en}/\rho$ of Ag, escapes. Both assumptions were tested with the Monte Carlo (MC) code and slab geometry of Section 2.7 (1 % Ag in a 10 mm layer at 2–12 mm depth; unfiltered and Al 1 mm-filtered SpekPy spectra; $4\times10^{6}$ histories in five batches) against the primary-beam kerma model for the same geometry, including attenuation by Ag. The two methods give the same profile shape ({{REF:fig22_mc}}; regional averages in {{REF:t3_11}}). With Al 1 mm the DEF rises sharply at the layer entrance and falls towards the exit through self-shielding. For the unfiltered beam an entrance peak is followed by a minimum at about 3–4 mm and a rise: near the entrance Ag attenuates mainly the soft components that still dominate the dose, whereas deeper, after their absorption, beam hardening prevails. The agreement is within 1.2 % (unfiltered) and 3.0 % (Al 1 mm) at the entrance and 6.5 % and 10.1 % at the exit, MC being higher everywhere.

{{FIG:fig22_mc|Depth profiles of the dose enhancement factor with a 10 mm layer of 1 % Ag at 2–12 mm (grey band) in soft tissue, unfiltered (left) and Al 1.0 mm (right): Monte Carlo (solid, ±2 standard errors shaded) and the analytical kerma model (dashed).}}

{{TABLE:t3_11}}

Two effects explain the excess: part of the Ag K-fluorescence (about 22 keV) is reabsorbed, its mean free path in tissue (about 1.5 cm) being comparable to the layer thickness and oblique photons travelling long paths in the laterally infinite layer, and scattered photons add dose. Both build up with depth, hence the growing difference; backward fluorescence also lifts the MC DEF slightly above unity in front of the layer for the filtered beam. Behind the layer the dose drops to 0.80 of the Ag-free value (0.76 analytically), which protects normal tissue behind a loaded tumour but also lowers the dose in tumour regions behind a heavily loaded region. The analytical model thus conservatively underestimates the DEF in a thick loaded region, by up to 10 %.

## 3.10. Uncertainty and sensitivity analysis

The central result, DEF = 1.482 for 1 % Ag with Al 1 mm, was tested by varying each parameter in turn within its plausible uncertainty ({{REF:fig23_sensitivity}}, {{REF:t3_12}}). The tissue coefficient was varied in the loaded and reference media together, acting only through $\langle R\rangle$ in equation {{REF:eq:def_lin}}. The spectrum model is represented by the DM–SpekPy difference, and $\eta$ (±25 %) by changes relative to the DM value of 1.464.

{{FIG:fig23_sensitivity|Sensitivity of the dose enhancement factor (1 % Ag, Al 1.0 mm, DEF = 1.482) to the input parameters at the two limits of each variation.}}

{{TABLE:t3_12}}

The largest change, 0.057, comes from the reference medium (1.539 for ICRU four-component tissue, 1.496 for water): both lack the trace elements Na, P, S, Cl and K that raise the energy absorption of ICRU-44 tissue, so $\langle R\rangle$ and the DEF increase. This choice, a matter of definition, is excluded from the uncertainty but must always be stated. Otherwise the spectrum model dominates (0.018), followed by the Ag coefficient, anode angle and tube voltage (0.009–0.010 each). A smaller anode angle raises the DEF through stronger self-filtration, whereas the Be window is irrelevant (0.0002), as the Al filter removes the photons it would attenuate. Treating each maximum change as the half-width of a rectangular distribution, with uncorrelated parameters, the combined standard uncertainty is

$$
u_c(\mathrm{DEF}) = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{\Delta_i^{2}}{3}}
$$ {#eq:uc_def}

where $\Delta_i$ is the maximum absolute change of the DEF due to the $i$-th parameter and $n = 8$ (all parameters except the reference medium). Thus $u_c = 0.016$: DEF = 1.48 ± 0.02 (coverage factor $k = 1$) or ± 0.03 ($k = 2$), more than an order of magnitude below the enhancement ($\mathrm{DEF}-1 = 0.48$) and about an order of magnitude below the differences between the main filters (0.14–0.32 between 1 mm Al, 2 mm Al and 0.2 mm Cu), so the filtration conclusions are robust. Incorrect interaction data would matter far more: the xraylib 4.3.0 function CS_Energy would give 1.62 instead of 1.48 (Section 2.8).

## 3.11. Discussion

The results agree with earlier work. Early studies calculated the enhancement in iodine-loaded tumours under kilovoltage x-rays [@mello1983; @solberg1992]; MC showed a much larger macroscopic enhancement by gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) for kilovoltage than megavoltage beams [@cho2005]; and nanoparticle enhancement for various elements was characterised versus photon energy with a kerma-based approach like the present one [@roeske2007]. The depth dependence in orthovoltage beams was studied by MC in phantoms [@chow2023]; for Ag, a single surface value cannot characterise a thick target (Section 3.7). This work adds a low-voltage Cu-anode tube, whose spectrum is dominated by lines below the Ag K-edge and whose attainable DEF is set by filtration rather than tube design. The MC studies of silver (Section 1.4) used brain-tumour brachytherapy [@taha2019], tumour–healthy-tissue [@caglar2024] and layered-skin [@esitmez2026] geometries, allowing only a qualitative comparison: the dose reduction to about 0.80 behind the loaded layer bears on the healthy-tissue doses of [@caglar2024], and the per-mass DEFs of Au and Ag (2.16 versus 1.48 with Al 1 mm) on the comparison in [@esitmez2026]. Measurements in AgNP-enriched dose gels [@merkis2023] could test the calculated DEF if the beam spectrum is specified.

A larger discrepancy arises with radiobiology. AgNPs reportedly sensitize cancer cells to ionising radiation [@xu2009], act as radiosensitizers for glioma [@liu2013] and outperformed AuNPs in U251 glioma cells in vitro and in an intracranial mouse model [@liu2016]. Such effects are quantified by the sensitizer enhancement ratio (SER), the ratio of doses giving equal effect without and with the sensitizer, which, unlike the DEF, reflects all mechanisms [@hall2018]. Macroscopic physics predicts the opposite Ag–Au ranking at equal mass fraction and little enhancement at low loading (DEF 1.048 for 0.1 % Ag with Al 1 mm). Although biological effects also depend on radiation quality, concentration and cellular localisation, the reported radiosensitization by AgNPs, especially their advantage over AuNPs, is unlikely to result from macroscopic dose enhancement alone. Two mechanisms may explain it: AgNPs cause ROS formation, DNA damage, cell-cycle arrest and impaired mitochondrial function in human cells [@asharani2009], and the dose enhancement around gold nanoparticles is highly localised [@jones2010], nanoscale energy deposition explaining effects beyond the macroscopic enhancement [@mcmahon2011]. The macroscopic DEF is thus only the physical baseline of a radio-enhancement with chemical and biological stages [@kuncic2018].

The work has several limitations. The kerma approximation ignores nanoscale dose heterogeneity, and Ag was assumed homogeneous, whereas radiosensitization by nanoparticles depends on their size, concentration and location [@lechtman2011]. The DM assumes isotropic bremsstrahlung and neglects electron backscattering from the anode, which with the approximate Born cross-section overestimates the output (DM/SpekPy air-kerma ratio 1.08–1.42) but not the DEF; its 26–43 % Cu K-line deficit is why the main results use SpekPy spectra. The heel effect was not modelled, the analytical depth calculations neglect scatter and fluorescence reabsorption (Section 3.9), and the MC geometry is a simple slab. No actual tube spectrum was measured, although SpekPy has been validated against measured spectra and HVLs [@bujila2020], and no biological response was modelled.

For an experiment with a 45 kV Cu-anode tube, a 1–2 mm Al (or 0.1–0.3 mm Ti) filter is recommended; with Al it gives a DEF of 1.48–1.62 for 1 % Ag and 2 Gy in 14.6–32.5 s at 10 cm and 40 mA ({{REF:t3_7}}). Thin Cu filters (0.05–0.1 mm) and K-edge filters at 20–30 keV (Mo, Ag, Sn) should be avoided, and 0.2 mm Cu, despite the largest DEF (1.80), needs 158 s for 2 Gy. The beam quality should be verified by an HVL measurement [@ma2001] against the calculated first HVL (0.83 mm Al with 1 mm Al, 1.27 mm Al with 2 mm Al). The Ag mass fraction should be at least 0.5 % (DEF 1.24 with 1 mm Al), since at 0.1 % the enhancement is below 5 %. For samples thicker than a few millimetres the DEF should be calculated for the actual depth and loading with NIST (not xraylib CS_Energy) coefficients and reported with the spectrum (or tube voltage, filtration and HVL), reference medium and depth.

## 3.12. Conclusions to Chapter 3

1. The DM (one fitted parameter, $\eta = 0.40$) agrees with SpekPy within −3.5 to −4.6 % in HVL for 1 mm Al and within 2 % in DEF for Al filters but underestimates the Cu K lines by 26–43 %.
2. The unfiltered spectrum is dominated by the Cu K lines (68.5 % of photons), giving a DEF of only 1.20 for 1 % Ag; tube design parameters change mainly the output, not the DEF (1.19–1.21).
3. Filtration is decisive: for 1 % Ag the DEF is 1.48 (Al 1 mm), 1.62 (Al 2 mm) and 1.80 (Cu 0.2 mm), at a 120–1300-fold lower dose rate; at equal transmission Al and Ti are optimal, K-edge filters at 20–30 keV unfavourable.
4. With Al 1 mm the DEF rises with the depth of a loaded region in Ag-free tissue (1.69 at 20 mm) but falls inside a uniformly loaded region by self-shielding (1.21 at 10 mm, 1 % Ag).
5. MC confirms the analytical model within 1–10 % (MC higher owing to fluorescence reabsorption and scatter) and shows a dose reduction to about 0.80 behind the loaded layer.
6. The DEF rises with tube voltage, most steeply once photons above the Ag K-edge appear (above about 26–27 kV); per unit mass, Ag is a weaker physical sensitizer than Gd, Pt, Au and Bi and comparable to iodine.
7. The DEF (1 % Ag, Al 1 mm) is 1.48 ± 0.02 ($k = 1$); the reference medium changes it by up to 0.057 and must be stated.
