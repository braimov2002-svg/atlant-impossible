# CHAPTER 1. RADIOSENSITIZATION BY HIGH-Z ELEMENTS AND X-RAY SPECTRUM MODELING: LITERATURE REVIEW

## 1.1. Radiotherapy and the problem of normal-tissue tolerance

Cancer is a major cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide [@bray2024], and radiotherapy (RT) is, together with surgery and chemotherapy, one of the principal modalities of its treatment [@baskar2012]; the optimal rate of RT utilisation estimated from evidence-based guidelines is approximately 52 % [@delaney2005]. RT acts by damaging cellular DNA, directly through secondary electrons or indirectly through the free radicals of water radiolysis, double-strand breaks being the most lethal lesions [@hall2018]. Because energy deposition is not selective, the healthy tissues traversed by the beam are damaged as well, and their response limits the dose that can be delivered to the tumour [@hall2018; @baskar2012]. The probabilities of tumour control and of normal-tissue complications both rise along sigmoid dose–response curves, and when the curves lie close together, that is, when the therapeutic ratio is small, normal-tissue toxicity becomes the main factor limiting the effectiveness of RT [@hall2018; @baskar2012]. The problem is aggravated for tumours containing hypoxic cells, which are considerably less sensitive to x-rays because of the oxygen effect [@hall2018].

Fractionation exploits the differences in repair between normal and tumour tissues [@hall2018; @baskar2012], and conformal, image-guided techniques such as intensity-modulated RT shape the high-dose region to the target [@khan2014]. Neither approach, however, changes the ratio of the energy absorbed per unit mass in tumour and normal cells located in the same region of the beam. Radiosensitizers act on this ratio, increasing the effect of radiation on tumour cells more than on normal cells [@hall2018; @wardman2007]. Chemical radiosensitizers include oxygen mimetics such as the nitroimidazoles [@wardman2007], and compounds of platinum, gold, ruthenium and other transition metals combine chemical and biological activity with the presence of heavy atoms [@gill2019]. High-Z elements act by an essentially physical principle: concentrated in the tumour, such an element absorbs a larger fraction of the photon energy than soft tissue, raising the tumour dose while the dose to the surrounding tissue without the element remains, to a first approximation, unchanged [@hainfeld2004; @kuncic2018]. This gain is expressed by the dose enhancement factor (DEF), the ratio of the absorbed dose in tissue containing the agent to that in the same tissue without it (Section 2.6); unlike chemical sensitization, it can be calculated from interaction data once the radiation field is known.

Monte Carlo (MC) calculations for gold nanoparticles showed a much larger macroscopic dose enhancement with kilovoltage than with megavoltage beams [@cho2005], because the strongly Z-dependent photoelectric effect dominates only at low photon energies (Section 1.2). Kilovoltage x-rays are used in superficial and orthovoltage therapy [@khan2014], and the dosimetry of 40–300 kV beams follows protocols in which the beam quality is specified by the half-value layer (HVL) [@ma2001; @iaea_trs398]. The DEF of an agent therefore cannot be stated without the x-ray spectrum, which depends on the design of the tube and on its filtration. The present work addresses this problem for silver irradiated with the beam of a 45 kV x-ray tube with a copper anode.

## 1.2. Interaction of kilovoltage photons with biological matter

At energies up to 50 keV, photons interact through photoelectric absorption, incoherent (Compton) scattering and coherent (Rayleigh) scattering; pair production, with its threshold $2m_ec^2 = 1.022$ MeV, is impossible [@podgorsak2016; @attix1986]. The mass attenuation coefficient is the sum of the partial coefficients [@attix1986; @andreo2017]:

$$
\frac{\mu}{\rho} = \frac{\tau}{\rho} + \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{coh}}}{\rho} + \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{inc}}}{\rho}
$$ {#eq:mu_sum}

where $\mu$ is the linear attenuation coefficient, $\rho$ is the density, and $\tau/\rho$, $\sigma_{\mathrm{coh}}/\rho$ and $\sigma_{\mathrm{inc}}/\rho$ are the mass coefficients (cm²/g) of photoelectric absorption, coherent scattering and incoherent scattering. For a mixture such as soft tissue the elemental coefficients are weighted by mass fractions [@attix1986]; evaluated data are tabulated by the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) [@nist_xraymass; @nist_xcom]. In photoabsorption a bound electron, preferentially of the most tightly bound accessible shell, is ejected with the photon energy $E$ minus its binding energy, and between absorption edges [@podgorsak2016]

$$
\frac{\tau}{\rho} \propto \frac{Z^{3}}{E^{3}}
$$ {#eq:tau_scaling}

where $Z$ is the atomic number of the absorbing element; the atomic cross-section scales as $Z^{4}/E^{3}$ [@podgorsak2016; @attix1986]. Per unit mass, a heavy element therefore absorbs far more strongly than H, C, N and O, which make up almost the entire mass of soft tissue [@icru44]. Below its K-edge, however, the K-shell electrons cannot take part in photoabsorption, and the ratio to tissue is much smaller than the atomic numbers suggest.

Absorption edges are abrupt increases of $\tau/\rho$ at the binding energies of the atomic shells [@podgorsak2016]; for silver the K-edge lies at 25.514 keV and the L-subshell edges between 3.35 and 3.81 keV. The vacancy is filled from an outer shell, and the released energy is emitted as a characteristic (fluorescence) x-ray or an Auger electron [@bambynek1972; @krause1979]. The K-shell fluorescence yield $\omega_K$ rises steeply with $Z$, from 0.039 for aluminium to 0.960 for gold, and equals 0.831 for silver ({{REF:t2_2}}) [@krause1979; @schoonjans2011]. In H, C, N and O, whose K-shell binding energies are below 1 keV, practically all the absorbed energy remains near the interaction site as electron kinetic energy. Silver instead re-emits a large part of it as Kα photons of about 22 keV with a mean free path of about 1.5 cm in soft tissue, whereas its low-energy Auger electrons deposit their energy close to the atom (Section 1.3).

In Compton scattering at kilovoltage energies the photon retains most of its energy [@podgorsak2016; @attix1986]. Electron binding reduces the Klein–Nishina cross-section at low energies and small angles, as described by the incoherent scattering function $S(x,Z)$, where $x$ is the momentum-transfer variable; Rayleigh scattering only deflects the photon and is described by the atomic form factor $F(x,Z)$ [@hubbell1975]. In soft tissue incoherent scattering overtakes photoabsorption in the upper part of the interval below 50 keV, while in silver photoabsorption dominates throughout [@podgorsak2016; @nist_xcom]. The mass energy-absorption coefficient includes only the energy transferred to charged particles and not re-emitted as bremsstrahlung [@attix1986; @seltzer1993]:

$$
\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho} = \left[\frac{\tau}{\rho}\left(1-\frac{X}{E}\right) + \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{inc}}}{\rho}\,\frac{\bar{T}_{\mathrm{inc}}}{E}\right]\left(1-\bar{g}\right)
$$ {#eq:muen_def}

where $X$ is the mean energy emitted as fluorescence per photoabsorption event, $\bar{T}_{\mathrm{inc}}$ is the mean kinetic energy of the Compton recoil electron, $\bar{g}$ is the mean fraction of the kinetic energy of the secondary electrons converted into bremsstrahlung, negligible for kilovoltage photons in low-Z media, and the other symbols are as in {{REF:eq:mu_sum}} [@attix1986]. The NIST values of $\mu_{en}/\rho$ used in this work are based on the calculations of Seltzer [@seltzer1993; @nist_xraymass].

According to the International Commission on Radiation Units and Measurements (ICRU), kerma $K$ is the sum of the initial kinetic energies of all charged particles liberated by photons per unit mass, the collision kerma $K_{\mathrm{col}}$ is its part not converted into bremsstrahlung, and the absorbed dose $D$ is the mean energy imparted per unit mass (1 Gy = 1 J/kg) [@icru85; @attix1986]. Under charged-particle equilibrium (CPE), when the energy carried into a small volume by charged particles equals that carried out, the absorbed dose equals the collision kerma [@attix1986; @andreo2017]:

$$
D = K_{\mathrm{col}} = \Psi\,\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}
$$ {#eq:dose_cpe}

where $\Psi = \Phi E$ is the energy fluence of monoenergetic photons with energy $E$ and fluence $\Phi$; for a polyenergetic beam the right-hand side is integrated over the spectrum (Section 2.5). Secondary electrons released by photons below 50 keV have ranges in tissue much shorter than a millimetre, so CPE holds except close to boundaries, and by the Fano theorem it is not disturbed by density variations in a medium of uniform atomic composition [@fano1954]. Two media exposed to the same spectrum therefore receive doses in the ratio of their spectrum-averaged mass energy-absorption coefficients. This relation underlies the macroscopic, kerma-based DEF (Section 2.6) and fails only within the electron ranges from a high-Z inclusion.

{{REF:fig02_coeffs}} compares the coefficients of silver and of ICRU-44 soft tissue [@icru44], taken from the NIST tables [@nist_xraymass]. Above the L-edges, both coefficients of silver exceed those of tissue by more than an order of magnitude, and the ratio of the mass energy-absorption coefficients increases from 21.0 at 8 keV to 31.8 at 25 keV. At the K-edge $\mu/\rho$ of silver increases by a factor of 5.8, from 9.53 to 55.4 cm²/g, but $\mu_{en}/\rho$ only by a factor of 2.4, from 8.54 to 20.3 cm²/g. The reason is K-shell fluorescence: about 83 % of the K vacancies relax by emitting K x-rays, mainly Kα photons of about 22 keV, whose energy leaves the interaction site and is excluded from $\mu_{en}/\rho$.

{{FIG:fig02_coeffs|Mass attenuation (solid lines) and mass energy-absorption (dashed lines) coefficients of silver and ICRU-44 soft tissue at 1–50 keV (NIST data). The vertical dashed line marks the Ag K-edge at 25.51 keV; the arrow indicates the Ag L-subshell edges.}}

For soft tissue the two coefficients nearly coincide below about 10 keV and diverge at higher energies, where scattering, which deposits little energy, takes a growing share of the attenuation. Above the K-edge, $\mu_{en}/\rho$ of silver decreases with energy more slowly than its $\mu/\rho$, because the fixed fluorescence energy becomes a progressively smaller fraction of the photon energy, and also more slowly than $\mu_{en}/\rho$ of tissue. Consequently, after the jump at the edge from about 32 to about 76, the silver-to-tissue ratio continues to grow, reaching 102.7 at 30 keV, 120.0 at 35 keV and a maximum of about 139 at 40–45 keV. The fraction of the photon energy fluence above the Ag K-edge is therefore the key characteristic of a beam intended for silver-enhanced irradiation, and its determination for a copper-anode tube is a central task of this work.

## 1.3. High-Z radiosensitizers: from iodine contrast to metal nanoparticles

Dose enhancement near a high-Z medium in kilovoltage beams was first analysed by Spiers, who showed how the increased energy absorption of bone and the ranges of the secondary electrons determine the dose to soft tissue in and near bone [@spiers1949]; this already combined enhanced energy transfer to the high-Z material with electron transport into adjacent tissue. Das and Chopra later quantified the backscatter dose perturbation at tissue–high-Z interfaces in kilovoltage beams [@das1995]. The first systematic studies used iodinated contrast media. Matsudaira et al. found that such a medium sensitized cultured mammalian cells to x-rays but not to gamma rays [@matsudaira1980], which points to the photoelectric absorption of low-energy photons by iodine. The dose enhancement in iodine-loaded tumours was calculated for kilovoltage x-rays [@mello1983] and for the dose-enhancement therapy of brain tumours [@solberg1992], and a computed tomography scanner modified for therapy (CTRx) delivered the first such radiotherapy of human brain metastases [@rose1999]. Monochromatic synchrotron beams were used to treat rats bearing iodine-loaded F98 gliomas [@adam2003] and to determine the optimal energy for the radiotoxicity of iodinated compounds in cells [@corde2004]. Kilovoltage contrast-enhanced RT was analysed by MC simulation [@verhaegen2005], and modified megavoltage beams were combined with iodine and gadolinium contrast media [@robar2002].

Platinum-based drugs combine a chemotherapeutic agent with a high-Z atom: Biston et al. cured rats bearing radioresistant F98 gliomas with cisplatin and monochromatic synchrotron x-rays that exploited the photoactivation of platinum at its K-edge [@biston2004]. Pignol et al. analysed the role of inner-shell ionisation and Auger cascades in radiosensitization by iododeoxyuridine, bromodeoxyuridine, platinum salts and gadolinium porphyrins [@pignol2003]; when the heavy atom lies in or near DNA, the short-range Auger electrons deposit their energy close to this critical target. The subcellular location of the high-Z atom therefore matters, not only its concentration.

The modern stage began with the study of Hainfeld et al., in which gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) 1.9 nm in diameter markedly improved the one-year survival of tumour-bearing mice irradiated with 250 kVp (kilovolt peak) x-rays [@hainfeld2004]. The material, size, coating and photon energy that determine nanoparticle efficacy have been analysed systematically [@retif2015]. AuNP radiosensitization was demonstrated in vitro with superficial kilovoltage x-rays [@rahman2009], with orthovoltage and superficial beams for folate-conjugated AuNPs in HeLa cells [@khoshgard2014], and with monoenergetic synchrotron beams [@rahman2014]. Uptake and radiosensitization depend on particle size [@chithrani2010], and experiments with DNA in solution identified photon energy, particle size and concentration as the governing parameters [@brun2009]. These results are summarised in reviews [@mesbahi2010; @jain2012; @haume2016; @song2017; @penninckx2020] and in a roadmap to clinical use [@schuemann2016].

The physics of AuNP radiosensitization has been studied mainly by MC simulation. After the first estimate of the macroscopic tumour dose enhancement [@cho2005], the tumour-selective enhancement was quantified for various photon sources [@zhang2009] and therapeutic beams [@kakade2015], and the kilovoltage range was found optimal for AuNP-enhanced arc radiotherapy [@koger2016]. Lechtman et al. showed how radiosensitization depends on the photon energy and on the size, concentration and location of the nanoparticles [@lechtman2011]. On the microscopic scale the enhancement around AuNPs is highly localised [@jones2010], and the spatial distribution of their secondary electrons was characterised with the Geant4 toolkit [@leung2011]. The consistently strong energy dependence makes a reliable description of the incident spectrum a prerequisite of any dose-enhancement calculation.

The biological effect of high-Z nanoparticles, however, cannot be explained by the increase in the mean dose alone. Radio-enhancement is usually divided into a physical stage (additional photon absorption and emission of photoelectrons, Auger electrons and fluorescence photons), a chemical stage (additional production of reactive oxygen species, ROS) and a biological stage (oxidative stress, perturbation of the cell cycle, inhibition of DNA repair) [@kuncic2018; @her2017; @butterworth2012; @rosa2017]. McMahon et al. showed with a local effect model that the highly inhomogeneous nanoscale energy deposition near heavy-atom nanoparticles can explain biological effects exceeding the macroscopic dose enhancement [@mcmahon2011], so that the sensitizer enhancement ratio (SER) measured in cells may exceed the macroscopic DEF. The macroscopic DEF nevertheless remains the baseline quantity needed to separate the physical contribution from the chemical and biological ones.

Although gold dominates the literature, the choice of the element is not obvious: the kerma-based dose enhancement from nanoparticles of various elements has been characterised as a function of photon energy [@roeske2007], and the choice of element has been optimised by physical modelling [@mcmahon2016]. In the kerma approximation the comparison reduces to the ratio $R(E)$ of the mass energy-absorption coefficient of the element to that of tissue: for tissue containing a mass fraction $w$ of the element and irradiated by monoenergetic photons of energy $E$, the DEF equals $1 + w\,[R(E) - 1]$ (Section 2.6). {{REF:fig03_ratio}} shows $R(E)$ for silver, iodine, gadolinium, platinum, gold and bismuth at 5–45 keV, an interval covering practically the whole spectrum of the 45 kV tube.

{{FIG:fig03_ratio|Ratio of the mass energy-absorption coefficient of Ag, I, Gd, Pt, Au and Bi to that of ICRU-44 soft tissue at 5–45 keV (NIST data). The vertical dashed line marks the Ag K-edge at 25.51 keV.}}

Below about 12 keV the ratios for silver, platinum, gold and bismuth are similar, and that for iodine is somewhat higher. Above its L-edges (7.2–8.4 keV) and up to about 13 keV gadolinium has the highest ratio and absorbs the Cu Kα lines at about 8.0 keV particularly efficiently. The L-edges of platinum, gold and bismuth at about 12–16 keV raise their ratios several-fold, leaving silver with the lowest ratio of the six between about 12 and 25.5 keV. Above its K-edge silver first equals and then exceeds gadolinium, whose K-edge (50.239 keV) lies above the maximum photon energy of the beam, and it exceeds iodine up to the iodine K-edge at 33.169 keV. At 40–45 keV the ratios for iodine, platinum and silver become comparable, while gold and bismuth retain the highest values.

Two conclusions follow. First, per unit mass silver is expected to be a weaker physical sensitizer than platinum, gold and bismuth, and its advantage over iodine and gadolinium is limited to energies above its K-edge, so whether it yields a higher DEF depends on the spectrum. Second, the physical efficacy of silver is controlled mainly by the fraction of the energy fluence above its K-edge, where the silver-to-tissue ratio is 76–139 instead of 21–32 between 8 keV and the edge, an interval that includes the Cu K lines at 8.0–8.9 keV. The design parameters of the tube, and especially its filtration, which redistribute the energy fluence relative to 25.514 keV, are therefore expected to be decisive for the DEF, as tested quantitatively in Chapter 3. The arguments that nevertheless favour silver are reviewed in Section 1.4.
