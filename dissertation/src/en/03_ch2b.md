## 2.6. Dose enhancement factor: definitions and calculation

The physical radio-enhancement by silver is quantified by the DEF, the ratio of the absorbed doses at the same point of tissue with and without silver under identical irradiation:

$$
\mathrm{DEF} = \frac{D_{t+\mathrm{Ag}}}{D_t}
$$ {#eq:def}

where $D_{t+\mathrm{Ag}}$ is the absorbed dose in the silver-loaded tissue (energy imparted per unit mass of the mixture) and $D_t$ is that in the unloaded (reference) tissue; values below unity indicate a dose reduction, e.g. by shielding. This macroscopic DEF assumes a homogeneous silver distribution and doses averaged over volumes much larger than a cell and than the electron ranges, so it describes only the physical stage of radio-enhancement.

Below 50 keV the secondary electrons have ranges in soft tissue far below a millimetre (Section 1.2), so charged-particle equilibrium (CPE) holds except in thin layers near interfaces [@attix1986; @andreo2017]. The absorbed dose then equals the collision kerma {{REF:eq:dose_cpe}}, which practically coincides with the kerma because bremsstrahlung losses in low-Z media are negligible [@icru85]. By analogy with the air kerma {{REF:eq:kerma}},

$$
D = \int_{0}^{T_0} \Psi(E)\,\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}(E)\,\mathrm{d}E
$$ {#eq:dose_spec}

where $\Psi(E) = E\,\Phi(E)$ is the spectral energy fluence, $\Phi(E)$ is the spectral photon fluence {{REF:eq:fluence}}, $\mu_{en}/\rho$ is the mass energy-absorption coefficient of the medium and $T_0$ = 45 keV is the maximum photon energy. Since $\mu_{en}/\rho$ excludes the energy carried away by scattered and fluorescence photons [@seltzer1993], the Ag K x-rays, emitted after about 83 % of K-shell vacancies ($\omega_K = 0.831$), are assumed to escape; Section 2.7 tests this assumption. For a homogeneous mixture with silver mass fraction $w$ the mixture rule gives [@nist_xraymass]

$$
\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_{t+\mathrm{Ag}} = (1-w)\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_t + w\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_{\mathrm{Ag}}
$$ {#eq:mixture}

where the subscripts $t$ and Ag denote tissue and silver; the same rule over elemental mass fractions yields the coefficients of tissue, water and air (Section 2.8). Mass fractions from 0.1 to 2 % (1–20 mg of silver per gram of loaded tissue) are considered. Substituting {{REF:eq:dose_spec}} and {{REF:eq:mixture}} into {{REF:eq:def}} gives the DEF at the surface:

$$
\mathrm{DEF} = \frac{\int \Psi(E)\left[(1-w)\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_t + w\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_{\mathrm{Ag}}\right]\mathrm{d}E}{\int \Psi(E)\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_t\mathrm{d}E}
$$ {#eq:def_spec}

where all coefficients are taken at the photon energy $E$. The fluence normalisation cancels, so the DEF depends only on the spectral shape, and DM spectra can be used on an equal footing with SpekPy despite their less accurate absolute output (Section 3.1). In the function `def_factor` of `xspec.py` the integrals are sums over the 0.05 keV spectral bins. Since the numerator is linear in $w$, {{REF:eq:def_spec}} can be rearranged exactly into

$$
\mathrm{DEF} = 1 + w\left(\langle R\rangle - 1\right)
$$ {#eq:def_lin}

with

$$
\langle R\rangle = \frac{\int \Psi(E)\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_{\mathrm{Ag}}\mathrm{d}E}{\int \Psi(E)\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_t\mathrm{d}E} = \frac{\int R(E)\,d_t(E)\,\mathrm{d}E}{\int d_t(E)\,\mathrm{d}E}
$$ {#eq:ratio_avg}

where $R(E) = (\mu_{en}/\rho)_{\mathrm{Ag}}/(\mu_{en}/\rho)_t$ and $d_t(E) = \Psi(E)\,(\mu_{en}/\rho)_t$ is the dose spectrum of unloaded tissue. Thus $\langle R\rangle$ is the dose-weighted mean of $R(E)$, and the slope $\langle R\rangle - 1$ is a property of the spectrum alone. For ICRU-44 tissue, $R(E)$ grows from 21.0 at 8 keV to 31.8 at 25 keV, jumps at the Ag K-edge (25.514 keV) to about 76 and reaches about 139 at 40–45 keV (Section 1.2). Any shift of the dose spectrum above the K-edge therefore raises the slope, which is the basis of the filter optimisation in Chapter 3.

Inside the medium the spectrum hardens. At a depth $z$ where silver is present only locally (a thin layer or small region that does not perturb the beam), the DEF for the primary beam is

$$
\mathrm{DEF}(z) = \frac{\int \Psi(E)\,e^{-\mu_t(E)\,z}\left[(1-w)\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_t + w\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_{\mathrm{Ag}}\right]\mathrm{d}E}{\int \Psi(E)\,e^{-\mu_t(E)\,z}\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_t\mathrm{d}E}
$$ {#eq:def_depth}

where $\Psi(E)$ is the energy fluence at the surface and $\mu_t(E) = \rho_t\,(\mu/\rho)_t(E)$ is the linear attenuation coefficient of tissue of density $\rho_t$ = 1.06 g/cm³; scattered photons and the inverse-square fall-off are neglected. The denominator, normalised to its value at $z = 0$, is the relative primary dose in unloaded tissue. Tissue removes first the low-energy photons, for which $R(E)$ is small, so the DEF increases with depth. If silver fills the whole region above the point of interest, it also attenuates the beam, most strongly just above the K-edge, where its mass attenuation coefficient increases 5.8-fold. For a slab loaded uniformly from the surface to beyond $z$,

$$
\mathrm{DEF}_{\mathrm{slab}}(z) = \frac{\int \Psi(E)\,e^{-\mu_{t+\mathrm{Ag}}(E)\,z}\left[(1-w)\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_t + w\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_{\mathrm{Ag}}\right]\mathrm{d}E}{\int \Psi(E)\,e^{-\mu_t(E)\,z}\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_t\mathrm{d}E}
$$ {#eq:def_slab}

where $\mu_{t+\mathrm{Ag}}(E) = \rho_t\left[(1-w)(\mu/\rho)_t + w\,(\mu/\rho)_{\mathrm{Ag}}\right]$ is the linear attenuation coefficient of the loaded medium (density approximated by $\rho_t$), and the denominator refers to silver-free tissue at the same depth. The added attenuation preferentially removes the most effective photons above the K-edge, so $\mathrm{DEF}_{\mathrm{slab}}$ decreases with depth (self-shielding) and is no longer linear in $w$. At $w$ = 1 % silver attenuates just above its K-edge about as much as the tissue itself, and behind a loaded region the dose falls below its silver-free value.

The reference medium is ICRU-44 soft tissue (H 10.2, C 14.3, N 3.4, O 70.8, Na 0.2, P 0.3, S 0.3, Cl 0.2 and K 0.3 % by mass; 1.06 g/cm³) [@icru44]; water and the ICRU four-component tissue serve as alternatives, and since this choice affects the DEF (Section 3.10), it must be reported with it. The macroscopic DEF does not resolve the subcellular scale, where short-range photoelectrons and Auger electrons deposit much of their energy close to the nanoparticles [@jones2010]. Such localised deposition can produce biological effects beyond the macroscopic enhancement (local effect model) [@mcmahon2011], depending on the photon energy and the size, concentration and location of the particles [@lechtman2011]. The DEF calculated here is thus the physical baseline against which chemical, biological and nanoscale contributions can be identified.

## 2.7. Monte Carlo method for verification

The two main assumptions of the analytical model, the escape of the Ag K-fluorescence and the neglect of scattered photons, are tested with an independent MC code (`mc_dose.py`). It is much simpler than PENELOPE, EGSnrc or Geant4 [@salvat2019; @kawrakow2000; @agostinelli2003] but includes all photon interactions relevant below 50 keV and the transport of silver fluorescence. A laterally infinite 30 mm slab of ICRU-44 tissue is irradiated by a broad parallel beam at normal incidence; a layer at depths of 2–12 mm contains silver with $w$ = 1 %, added as a partial density $\rho_{\mathrm{Ag}} = \rho_t\,w/(1-w)$. Photons are tracked by their energy $E$, depth $z$ and direction cosine $u$ until absorption or escape, and the deposited energy is scored in 0.25 mm bins. The incident SpekPy spectra of the 45 kV tube (θ = 12°, 0.5 mm Be, 10 cm air), unfiltered and with 1 mm Al, are sampled by inversion of the cumulative distribution within the 0.05 keV bins.

Free paths are sampled by Woodcock (delta) tracking [@woodcock1965; @kalos2008] with the majorant $\mu_{\max}(E) = \mu_t(E) + \mu_{\mathrm{Ag}}(E)$, where $\mu_{\mathrm{Ag}} = \rho_{\mathrm{Ag}}(\mu/\rho)_{\mathrm{Ag}}$ and both terms come from xraylib:

$$
s = -\frac{\ln \xi_1}{\mu_{\max}(E)}
$$ {#eq:mc_path}

where $s$ is the path length and $\xi_1$ is a random number uniform in (0, 1). At the new depth $z + u\,s$ the collision is real with the probability

$$
P_{\mathrm{real}} = \frac{\mu(z,E)}{\mu_{\max}(E)}
$$ {#eq:mc_real}

where $\mu(z,E)$ is the local coefficient ($\mu_t$ outside the layer, $\mu_t + \mu_{\mathrm{Ag}}$ inside it); a virtual collision leaves the photon unchanged, so distances to the layer boundaries are never computed.

The interaction type (photoabsorption in tissue or silver, incoherent or coherent scattering) is sampled from partial coefficients tabulated from xraylib [@schoonjans2011] at 1–46 keV in 5 eV steps. After photoabsorption in silver above the K-edge, the K shell is selected with the probability $\tau_K/\tau$ (K-shell to total photoelectric cross-section), and with the probability $\omega_K$ the vacancy emits one of the six principal K lines, chosen by their radiative rates. This photon is emitted isotropically and transported, whereas the remaining energy, like the whole energy of all other photoabsorption events, is deposited locally. The scattering angle $\vartheta$ is sampled from cumulative tables (91 energies, 1441 angles) of the Klein–Nishina cross-section times the incoherent scattering function and of the Thomson cross-section times the squared atomic form factor [@hubbell1975], computed for tissue, since silver contributes only a few per cent of the scattering in the layer. After incoherent scattering

$$
E' = \frac{E}{1 + \frac{E}{m_e c^2}\left(1 - \cos\vartheta\right)}
$$ {#eq:compton}

where $E$ and $E'$ are the photon energies before and after scattering and $m_e c^2$ = 510.999 keV is the electron rest energy. The energy $E - E'$ is deposited locally, the direction cosine becomes $u' = u\cos\vartheta + \sqrt{1-u^2}\,\sin\vartheta\cos\varphi$ with the azimuth $\varphi$ uniform in $[0, 2\pi)$, and photons below 1 keV are absorbed. Secondary electrons, with ranges of a few tens of micrometres, also deposit locally, and the dose in bin $j$ per unit incident fluence is

$$
D_j = \frac{1}{N}\,\frac{\sum_{i \in j} \varepsilon_i}{\rho_j\,\Delta z}
$$ {#eq:mc_dose}

where $\varepsilon_i$ are the energy deposits in the bin, $N$ is the number of incident photons, $\rho_j$ is the density ($\rho_t$ or $\rho_t + \rho_{\mathrm{Ag}}$) and $\Delta z$ = 0.25 mm. The MC DEF is the dose ratio for $w$ = 1 % and $w = 0$, obtained from $4\times10^{6}$ histories in five independent batches whose spread gives the standard error; the analytical reference is {{REF:eq:def_slab}} applied to the buried layer only. Two checks preceded the runs. For a broad 30 keV beam the ratio of the surface dose to the primary kerma is 1.31 owing to backscatter, which in kilovoltage beams depends on the field size and source distance [@grosswendt1990]. The energy-transfer coefficient reconstructed from the xraylib data agrees with the NIST $\mu_{en}/\rho$ within 0.3 % for O, C and H at 20–40 keV, where the two coefficients practically coincide, so the MC interaction data are consistent with NIST.

## 2.8. Data sources, software implementation and validation strategy

All $\mu_{en}/\rho$ values and the $\mu/\rho$ of tissue, water and air are taken from the NIST tables of Hubbell and Seltzer [@nist_xraymass], based on the calculations of Seltzer [@seltzer1993]. The module `nist.py` interpolates them log–log separately within each segment between absorption edges (recognised as repeated table energies), where the coefficients follow approximate power laws of energy. The mixture rule uses the ICRU-44 tissue, water and NIST dry-air compositions. The elemental $\mu/\rho$ of the anode, window, filter materials and silver, the edge and line energies, fluorescence yields, radiative rates and the MC cross-sections come from xraylib 4.3.0 [@schoonjans2011], whose $\mu/\rho$ agree with NIST [@nist_xcom] to better than 0.1 %.

The xraylib function `CS_Energy`, however, returns too low $\mu_{en}/\rho$ for light elements at 15–45 keV: for oxygen at 30 keV 0.108 instead of 0.173 cm²/g (−37 %) and for carbon 0.0515 instead of 0.0661 cm²/g, whereas for silver it agrees with NIST (16.58 versus 16.60 cm²/g). Since tissue consists mainly of O, C and H, this function would underestimate the denominator of {{REF:eq:def_spec}} and overestimate the DEF: 1.72 instead of 1.48 for 1 % Ag with 1 mm Al. The xraylib photoabsorption and scattering cross-sections are not affected (Section 2.7). NIST values of $\mu_{en}/\rho$ are therefore used throughout, and `CS_Energy` should be checked against NIST before use for low-Z media.

The reference spectrum model is SpekPy v2.5.4 [@poludniowski2021; @spekpy_soft] with its "casim" physics model, which supports copper anodes at 10–50 kV and has been validated against measured spectra and HVLs [@bujila2020]. Its spectra validate the DM and are the principal input of the DEF calculations, which are repeated with DM spectra. The code is written in Python 3.13 with NumPy [@harris2020], SciPy [@virtanen2020] (bounded minimisation; Brent's root-finding for the effective energy and equal-transmission thicknesses) and Matplotlib [@hunter2007]. The scripts `run_all.py`, `run_sweeps.py` and `mc_dose.py` produce the main results, the filter sweeps and the MC verification.

The single adjustable parameter, the electron depth factor $\eta$ in {{REF:eq:depth}}, minimises the root-mean-square deviation between the DM and SpekPy bremsstrahlung spectra (lines excluded) at 45 kV, θ = 12° and 0.5 mm Be, both normalised to unit area above 3 keV, with the deviation taken relative to the reference maximum; $\eta$ is searched in 0.25–0.7 and rounded to two decimals, and all other configurations are predictions without refitting. The validation comprises (i) component checks, e.g. the Bethe stopping power of water at 50 keV, 6.603 MeV cm²/g, identical to NIST ESTAR [@nist_estar]; (ii) comparison of spectra and beam-quality parameters with SpekPy for 25–50 kV and anode angles of 6–30°, with and without 1 mm Al, plus a tungsten-anode check at 50, 80 and 100 kV; (iii) comparison of the DEF from DM and SpekPy spectra; (iv) the MC verification; and (v) a sensitivity analysis of the DEF.

## 2.9. Radiation safety when working with a kilovoltage x-ray tube

A 45 kV copper-anode tube emits very intense low-energy x-rays: the unfiltered air kerma at 10 cm from the focal spot is 383 mGy per mAs (Chapter 3), i.e. 15.3 Gy/s at 40 mA, and the tissue surface receives 2 Gy in 0.12 s. An accidental exposure of the hands can thus cause severe local radiation injuries within seconds, and the primary beam, the radiation scattered by the sample and holder, and the housing leakage must all be controlled. Protection rests on the principles of justification, optimisation (as low as reasonably achievable, ALARA) and dose limitation of the International Commission on Radiological Protection [@icrp103], implemented in the Basic Safety Standards of the International Atomic Energy Agency [@iaea_gsr3] and, in the Republic of Uzbekistan, in the Law "On Radiation Safety" [@uz_law_radsafety]. In this planned exposure situation occupational exposure must remain below the limits on the effective dose and on the equivalent doses to the skin, extremities and lens of the eye.

The main measures are technical. The shielded housing lets radiation out only through the window and shutter, the sample is irradiated in a closed enclosure whose door interlocks switch off the high voltage or close the shutter, warning lights signal the operating state, and fixed holders and collimators keep the hands out of the beam. The high-voltage generator is an additional hazard, and the fragile beryllium window must not be touched, since beryllium compounds are toxic. Staff training, local rules, personal dosimetry and dose-rate surveys around the enclosure, particularly after maintenance, complete these measures.

Added filtration also serves safety. The filters that markedly raise the DEF (from 1 mm Al to 0.2 mm Cu) lower the air kerma rate 120–1300-fold; with 1 mm Al the surface tissue kerma rate at 40 mA falls from 16.5 to 0.137 Gy/s, and the time for 2 Gy rises from 0.12 to 14.6 s, which a timer or shutter controls with a small relative error. The filter removes the soft component absorbed in the superficial layers: the relative primary dose at 10 mm is 0.009 of the surface value without a filter and 0.44 with 1 mm Al. The filter that maximises the DEF, whose beam can be specified by its HVL as in the kilovoltage dosimetry protocol [@ma2001], thus also reduces the unnecessary skin dose and is an optimisation measure in the sense of ALARA.

## 2.10. Conclusions to Chapter 2

1. A semi-analytical thick-target model (DM) of the spectrum of a reflection x-ray tube with a copper anode has been formulated, including Bethe electron slowing-down, non-relativistic Bethe–Heitler bremsstrahlung with the Elwert factor, direct and bremsstrahlung-induced K radiation, anode self-absorption and attenuation in the window, air and filters, with a single adjustable parameter, the electron depth factor $\eta$.
2. The beam-quality parameters (first and second HVL, homogeneity coefficient, mean and effective energies) and the air kerma are calculated with identical routines for the DM and SpekPy spectra.
3. Under CPE below 50 keV, the DEF is a spectrum-weighted ratio of mass energy-absorption coefficients; at the surface $\mathrm{DEF} - 1$ is exactly proportional to the silver mass fraction, with a slope set by the dose spectrum above the Ag K-edge, and the depth and loaded-slab forms describe beam hardening and self-shielding.
4. An independent MC code with Woodcock tracking, Compton and Rayleigh scattering and Ag K-fluorescence transport tests the assumptions of the analytical model.
5. All $\mu_{en}/\rho$ values are taken from NIST, because the xraylib 4.3.0 function `CS_Energy` underestimates them for light elements by up to 37 % and would overestimate the DEF (1.72 instead of 1.48).
6. The unfiltered output (15.3 Gy/s at 10 cm and 40 mA) requires shielding, interlocks and dosimetric control; filtration reduces both the dose rate and the superficial dose.
