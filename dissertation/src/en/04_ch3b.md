## 3.7. Depth dependence and self-shielding

The DEF values discussed so far refer to the surface of the medium, whereas in experiments with tissue samples, phantoms or small animals the Ag-loaded target lies beneath tissue that modifies the beam. Two situations were therefore calculated with the SpekPy spectra in the primary-beam kerma approximation (Section 2.6): a small Ag-loaded region at depth $z$ in Ag-free soft tissue, attenuated only by the overlying tissue (equation {{REF:eq:def_depth}}), and a region uniformly loaded with Ag from the surface to the depth $z$, in which Ag itself contributes to the attenuation (equation {{REF:eq:def_slab}}). Scattered radiation is not included in either case; its role is assessed in Section 3.9.

For a loaded region at depth $z$ the DEF increases monotonically with depth for all filters ({{REF:fig18_def_depth}}; {{REF:t3_8}} also gives the relative tissue dose of the primary beam $D/D_0$, where $D_0$ is the dose at the surface). The overlying tissue preferentially absorbs low-energy photons and shifts the energy fluence towards higher energies, where the Ag/tissue ratio of the mass energy-absorption coefficients is larger. For the unfiltered beam the DEF for 1 % Ag rises from 1.20 at the surface to 1.34 at 5 mm and 1.56 at 20 mm, but as the Cu K lines and the soft bremsstrahlung are absorbed, the relative dose falls to 0.328 at 1 mm and 0.0090 at 10 mm: a few millimetres of tissue act as an added filter, with the trade-off described in Section 3.6. With the 1 mm Al filter, which has already hardened the beam, the DEF increases more moderately, to 1.69 at 20 mm, while the relative dose decreases only to 0.439 at 10 mm.

{{FIG:fig18_def_depth|Dose enhancement factor for 1 % Ag versus the depth of the Ag-loaded region in Ag-free ICRU-44 soft tissue for the 45 kV Cu-anode beam without added filter and with Al 0.5 mm, Al 1.0 mm, Cu 0.1 mm and Sn 0.1 mm filters (SpekPy spectra, primary beam, kerma approximation).}}

{{TABLE:t3_8}}

The Cu and Sn filters represent the two extremes. The beam filtered by 0.1 mm Cu consists of the transmitted Cu Kα line and a hard bremsstrahlung tail (Section 3.4); as the Kα line is absorbed in the first millimetres of tissue, the DEF rises steeply from 1.29 at the surface to 1.77 at 5 mm, approaching the surface value obtained with 0.2 mm Cu (1.80) because both beams then consist essentially of the hard component, and from about 2 mm onwards this curve lies above all others. The Sn 0.1 mm filter produces the weakest depth dependence (from 1.44 to 1.57 at 20 mm), because the photons above the Sn K-edge (29.2 keV) have been strongly attenuated and tissue hardens the remaining narrow band only slightly. The ranking of filters by their surface DEF therefore does not hold for deep-seated targets.

The opposite trend appears when the loaded region extends from the surface ({{REF:fig19_def_slab}}): the DEF decreases with depth, because Ag most strongly attenuates the photons above its K-edge that produce the enhancement (at 25.514 keV the mass attenuation coefficient of Ag increases 5.8-fold, from 9.53 to 55.4 cm²/g). With the Al 1.0 mm filter the DEF falls from 1.48 at the surface to 1.34 at 5 mm and 1.21 at 10 mm for 1 % Ag, and from 1.96 to 1.57 and 1.27 for 2 % Ag. Self-shielding thus increases with the concentration, and the linear relation between $\mathrm{DEF}-1$ and $w$ of equation {{REF:eq:def_lin}} is valid only for thin loaded layers. The Sn-filtered beam, whose spectrum is concentrated just above the Ag K-edge where the attenuation by Ag is largest, loses a larger part of its enhancement (for 1 % Ag, from 1.44 to 1.15 at 10 mm). The enhancement in a uniformly loaded region several millimetres thick is therefore non-uniform and, on average, considerably lower than at the surface.

## 3.8. Influence of tube voltage and comparison with other high-Z elements

The dependence of the DEF on the tube voltage $U$ is shown in {{REF:fig20_def_kvp}} and {{REF:t3_10}} for the Al 0.5 mm and Al 1.0 mm filters; SpekPy spectra were used for 20–50 kV, the range in which SpekPy supports the Cu anode, and the DM for 60–80 kV. Since the maximum photon energy equals $eU$, where $e$ is the elementary charge, photons above the Ag K-edge (25.514 keV) are present only above 25.5 kV. Below this voltage the DEF depends only weakly on $U$ (with Al 1.0 mm, 1.26 at 20 kV and 1.27 at 25 kV), owing to the gradual hardening of the bremsstrahlung. The slope increases visibly from 26–27 kV, and between 30 and 50 kV the DEF for Al 1.0 mm rises from 1.31 to 1.52 with a gradually decreasing slope, consistent near 45 kV with the change of ±0.009 for ±1 kV found in Section 3.10. Above 50 kV the growth slows down further (1.58 at 60 kV and 1.68 at 80 kV), because the spectrum extends beyond the maximum of the Ag/tissue ratio of the mass energy-absorption coefficients near 40–45 keV. As the DM gives slightly lower DEFs than SpekPy at 45 kV (1.464 versus 1.482 for Al 1.0 mm), the values at 60–80 kV are, if anything, underestimated, and part of the flattening between 50 and 60 kV reflects the change of the spectrum model.

{{TABLE:t3_10}}

To place silver among other candidate radiosensitizers, the DEF was calculated under the same conditions for soft tissue containing 1 % by mass of I, Gd, Pt, Au or Bi ({{REF:fig21_elements}}, {{REF:t3_9}}). Without added filtration all elements give similar DEFs of 1.20–1.33, because the dose is dominated by the Cu K lines and by photons below 15 keV. Pt and Au hardly differ from Ag: their L-edges, like those of Bi, lie above the Cu lines, at about 12–16 keV, and below these edges they absorb about as much energy per unit mass as Ag ({{REF:fig03_ratio}}). Gd gives the largest value (1.33), since its L-absorption edges at 7.2–8.4 keV make it a strong absorber of the Cu K lines.

{{FIG:fig21_elements|Dose enhancement factor of soft tissue containing 1 % by mass of Ag, I, Gd, Pt, Au or Bi for the 45 kV Cu-anode beam without added filter and with Al 1.0 mm and Sn 0.1 mm filters (SpekPy spectra, kerma approximation).}}

{{TABLE:t3_9}}

With the Al 1.0 mm filter the picture changes completely: the DEF reaches 2.13 for Pt, 2.16 for Au, 2.23 for Bi and 1.71 for Gd, whereas Ag (1.48) and I (1.46) remain close to each other. Above their L-edges Pt, Au and Bi absorb more energy per unit mass than Ag over the whole filtered spectrum, including the 15–25 keV region where Ag absorbs only through its L and outer shells. Gd, whose K-edge (50.2 keV) lies above the maximum photon energy, exceeds Ag only below the Ag K-edge, but this region still contributes much of the tissue dose of the filtered beam; iodine behaves like Ag, with its K-edge higher, at 33.2 keV. The Sn 0.1 mm filter slightly increases the DEF for Gd, Pt, Au and Bi but decreases it for Ag and I (1.44 for both), because it strongly attenuates the photons above 29.2 keV, where the Ag/tissue ratio reaches 100–139 and where the iodine K-edge lies. For the heavier elements, whose ratio varies much less across the filtered spectrum, the stronger suppression of low-energy photons by Sn (HVL 0.90 versus 0.83 mm Al) outweighs this loss.

Per unit mass, therefore, Ag is a weaker physical sensitizer at 45 kV than Gd, Pt, Au and Bi and is comparable to iodine: with Al 1 mm its value of $\mathrm{DEF}-1$ (0.48) is less than half that of Au. The arguments in favour of Ag lie elsewhere. Its K-edge lies inside the 45 kV spectrum, so that its enhancement can be tuned by filtration; silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) are biologically active and generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) [@asharani2009]; and silver is considerably cheaper than gold or platinum. The choice of Ag must thus be justified by its combined physical and biological action (Section 3.11).

## 3.9. Monte Carlo verification

The analytical DEFs rest on two simplifications: only the primary beam is considered, and the energy of the Ag K-fluorescence photons, which is excluded from the mass energy-absorption coefficient of Ag, is assumed to escape. Both were tested with the Monte Carlo (MC) code described in Section 2.7. A laterally infinite 30 mm slab of ICRU-44 soft tissue was irradiated by a broad parallel beam, with Ag added to a mass fraction of 1 % in a 10 mm layer at depths of 2–12 mm. For each configuration, $4\times10^{6}$ photon histories in 5 independent batches were tracked with the Woodcock method, including Ag K-fluorescence transport and Compton and Rayleigh scattering, with local deposition of the electron energy; the dose was scored in 0.25 mm bins for the unfiltered and the Al 1.0 mm-filtered SpekPy spectra. The analytical reference is the primary-beam kerma model for the same geometry, in which the beam is attenuated by tissue and, inside and behind the layer, also by Ag.

The MC and analytical depth profiles of the DEF are compared in {{REF:fig22_mc}}, and {{REF:t3_11}} gives their averages over characteristic regions. Both methods reproduce the same shape inside the layer. With Al 1 mm the DEF rises sharply at the entrance and then decreases towards the exit because of self-shielding. For the unfiltered beam a narrow entrance peak is followed by a minimum at about 3–4 mm and a subsequent rise, which in the analytical profile forms a broad maximum near 7–8 mm: near the entrance the attenuation by Ag acts mainly on the soft components that still dominate the dose and lowers the DEF, whereas deeper, after these components have been absorbed, beam hardening raises it again. At the layer entrance the two methods agree within 1.2 % for the unfiltered beam and within 3.0 % for Al 1 mm; the difference grows to 6.5 % and 10.1 % at the layer exit, the MC values being higher in all regions.

{{FIG:fig22_mc|Depth profiles of the dose enhancement factor in ICRU-44 soft tissue with a 10 mm layer containing 1 % Ag at 2–12 mm (grey band) for the unfiltered beam (left) and the Al 1.0 mm filter (right): Monte Carlo (solid line; shaded band ±2 standard errors from 5 batches) and the analytical primary-beam kerma model (dashed line).}}

{{TABLE:t3_11}}

Two effects explain this excess. First, part of the Ag K-fluorescence (about 22 keV) is reabsorbed within the layer: the mean free path of these photons in tissue, about 1.5 cm, is comparable to the layer thickness, and photons emitted at oblique angles travel long paths inside the laterally infinite layer, so part of the energy that the kerma approximation treats as escaping is deposited there. Second, scattered photons, which the primary-beam model ignores, contribute to the dose. Both contributions build up with depth, which explains why the difference grows towards the exit; fluorescence emitted backwards also raises the MC DEF slightly above unity in front of the layer for the filtered beam. Behind the layer the dose is reduced to 0.80 of the Ag-free value according to MC and to 0.76 according to the analytical model, because the layer shields the downstream tissue; this protects normal tissue behind a loaded tumour but also lowers the dose in tumour regions behind a heavily loaded region. The analytical model thus underestimates the macroscopic DEF in a thick loaded region by up to 10 % and is in this respect conservative, whereas near the layer entrance the agreement is within 1–3 %.

## 3.10. Uncertainty and sensitivity analysis

The central result, DEF = 1.482 for 1 % Ag with the Al 1.0 mm filter, was tested by a one-at-a-time sensitivity analysis ({{REF:fig23_sensitivity}}, {{REF:t3_12}}): each parameter was varied within its plausible uncertainty, as listed in {{REF:t3_12}}, while the others were kept fixed. The coefficient of tissue was varied consistently in the loaded and in the reference medium, so that it acts only through $\langle R\rangle$ in equation {{REF:eq:def_lin}}. The spectrum model was represented by the DM–SpekPy difference, and the changes for the depth factor $\eta$ of the DM (±25 %) were evaluated relative to the DM value of 1.464.

Spectrum model: DM relative to SpekPy; reference medium: ICRU four-component tissue (larger change) and water; η: changes relative to the DM value of 1.464.}}

{{TABLE:t3_12}}

The largest change, 0.057, results from the choice of the reference medium: the ICRU four-component tissue gives 1.539 and water 1.496. Both lack the trace elements Na, P, S, Cl and K, whose strong photoabsorption raises the energy absorption of ICRU-44 tissue, and a less absorbing reference medium increases $\langle R\rangle$ and hence the DEF. This choice is a matter of definition, not of uncertainty, and was excluded from the combined uncertainty, but the reference medium must always be stated. Among the other parameters the spectrum model dominates (0.018), followed by the coefficient of Ag, the anode angle and the tube voltage (0.009–0.010 each). A smaller anode angle increases the DEF (1.492 at 9° and 1.476 at 15°) through stronger self-filtration in the anode, whereas the Be window is irrelevant (0.0002), because the Al filter removes the soft photons that the window would attenuate. Treating each maximum change as the half-width of a rectangular distribution and the parameters as uncorrelated, the combined standard uncertainty is

$$
u_c(\mathrm{DEF}) = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{\Delta_i^{2}}{3}}
$$ {#eq:uc_def}

where $\Delta_i$ is the maximum absolute change of the DEF caused by varying the $i$-th parameter and $n = 8$ is the number of parameters included (all except the reference medium). This gives $u_c = 0.016$, i.e. DEF = 1.48 ± 0.02 with the coverage factor $k = 1$, or ± 0.03 with $k = 2$. The uncertainty is more than an order of magnitude smaller than the enhancement itself ($\mathrm{DEF}-1 = 0.48$) and than the differences between the main filter options, so the conclusions about filtration are robust. Incorrect interaction data would matter far more: the xraylib 4.3.0 function CS_Energy would give 1.72 instead of 1.48 (Section 2.8).

## 3.11. Discussion

The results are consistent with earlier work on dose enhancement by high-Z agents. Dose enhancement in iodine-loaded tumours irradiated with kilovoltage x-rays was calculated already in early studies [@mello1983; @solberg1992], MC estimates for gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) showed that the macroscopic enhancement is much larger for kilovoltage than for megavoltage beams [@cho2005], and the theoretical enhancement from nanoparticles of various elements has been characterised versus photon energy with a kerma-based approach like the present one [@roeske2007]. The depth dependence of the enhancement in orthovoltage beams has been studied by MC calculations in phantoms [@chow2023], and the present results (Section 3.7) show for Ag that a single surface value cannot characterise a thick target. The present work adds the case of a low-voltage Cu-anode tube, whose spectrum is dominated by characteristic lines below the Ag K-edge and for which the attainable DEF is determined by filtration rather than by the design of the tube.

MC studies of silver include the dose enhancement from AgNPs implanted in a brain tumour during brachytherapy [@taha2019], the effect of tumour dose enhancement by AgNPs on the surrounding healthy tissues [@caglar2024] and a comparison of Ag and Au nanoparticles in layered skin geometries [@esitmez2026]. Since the present calculations do not reproduce their conditions, only qualitative points of contact are noted: the dose reduction to about 0.80 behind a 10 mm layer with 1 % Ag is relevant to the healthy-tissue doses addressed in [@caglar2024], and the DEFs of Au and Ag per unit mass at 45 kV (2.16 versus 1.48 with Al 1 mm) to the Ag–Au comparison in [@esitmez2026]. Dose enhancement in AgNP-enriched dose gels has also been investigated experimentally [@merkis2023]; such measurements could test the calculated DEF if the beam spectrum is specified.

A more significant discrepancy appears in comparison with radiobiological studies. AgNPs have been reported to sensitize cancer cells to killing by ionising radiation [@xu2009] and to act as radiation sensitizers for glioma [@liu2013], and they outperformed AuNPs in radiosensitizing U251 glioma cells in vitro and in an intracranial mouse model [@liu2016]. This biological effect is quantified by the sensitizer enhancement ratio (SER), the ratio of doses giving the same effect without and with the sensitizer, which, unlike the DEF, reflects all mechanisms of sensitization [@hall2018]. For the beams considered here, macroscopic physics predicts the opposite ranking of Ag and Au at equal mass fraction, and at low mass fractions the physical enhancement is small (DEF 1.048 for 0.1 % Ag with Al 1 mm). A direct numerical comparison is not possible, because the biological effect also depends on the radiation quality, the concentration and the cellular localisation of the nanoparticles; nevertheless, the reported radiosensitization by AgNPs, in particular their advantage over AuNPs, is unlikely to be explained by macroscopic dose enhancement alone. Two mechanisms may account for the difference: AgNPs cause ROS formation, DNA damage, cell-cycle arrest and impaired mitochondrial function in human cells [@asharani2009], and the dose enhancement around gold nanoparticles is highly localised [@jones2010], with nanoscale energy deposition able to explain biological effects beyond the macroscopic enhancement [@mcmahon2011]. The macroscopic DEF calculated here is thus only the physical baseline of a radio-enhancement that also has chemical and biological stages [@kuncic2018].

The results are subject to several limitations. The kerma approximation is appropriate at the macroscopic scale but ignores the nanoscale dose heterogeneity around nanoparticles, and Ag was assumed to be distributed homogeneously, whereas the radiosensitization by nanoparticles depends on their size, concentration and location [@lechtman2011]. The DM assumes isotropic bremsstrahlung emission and neglects electron backscattering from the anode; together with the approximate Born cross-section, this overestimates the absolute output (DM/SpekPy air-kerma ratio 1.08–1.42) but does not affect the DEF. The DM also underestimates the Cu K lines by 25–43 %, which is why the main DEF results were obtained with SpekPy spectra. The off-axis variation of the spectrum caused by the heel effect was not modelled, the analytical depth calculations neglect scattered radiation and fluorescence reabsorption (underestimating the DEF in a thick loaded layer by up to 10 %), and the MC model uses a simple slab geometry. Finally, no spectrum of an actual tube was measured, although SpekPy has been validated against measured spectra and HVLs [@bujila2020], and no biological response was modelled.

For an experiment with a 45 kV Cu-anode tube the following is recommended. The beam should be filtered with 1–2 mm of Al (or 0.1–0.3 mm of Ti); with Al this gives DEF 1.48–1.62 for 1 % Ag and exposure times for 2 Gy of 14.6–32.5 s at 10 cm and 40 mA ({{REF:t3_7}}). Thin Cu filters (0.05–0.1 mm) and K-edge filters with edges between 20 and 30 keV (Mo, Ag, Sn) should be avoided, and 0.2 mm Cu, although it gives the largest DEF (1.80), requires 158 s for 2 Gy. The beam quality should be verified by an HVL measurement according to the kilovoltage dosimetry protocol [@ma2001]; agreement with the calculated first HVL (0.83 mm Al with 1 mm Al, 1.27 mm Al with 2 mm Al) indicates that the spectrum and the filter thickness are as assumed. The Ag mass fraction should be at least 0.5 %, for which the DEF with 1 mm Al is 1.24, since at 0.1 % the enhancement is below 5 %. For samples thicker than a few millimetres the DEF should be calculated for the actual depth and loading with NIST mass energy-absorption coefficients rather than the xraylib function CS_Energy, and it should always be reported with the spectrum (or at least the tube voltage, filtration and HVL), the reference medium and the depth.

## 3.12. Conclusions to Chapter 3

1. The DM (one fitted parameter, $\eta = 0.40$) agrees with SpekPy for Al-filtered beams within −4.6 to −3.5 % in HVL and 1–2 % in DEF but underestimates the Cu K lines by 25–43 %.
2. The unfiltered 45 kV spectrum is dominated by the Cu K lines (68.5 % of the photons), and the DEF for 1 % Ag is only 1.20; the tube design parameters change mainly the output, not the DEF (1.19–1.21).
3. Filtration is decisive: the DEF for 1 % Ag is 1.48 with Al 1 mm, 1.62 with Al 2 mm and 1.80 with Cu 0.2 mm, at a 120–1300-fold lower dose rate; at equal transmission Al and Ti are optimal and K-edge filters at 20–30 keV unfavourable.
4. With Al 1 mm, the DEF rises with the depth of a loaded region in Ag-free tissue (to 1.69 at 20 mm) but falls inside a uniformly loaded region through self-shielding (to 1.21 at 10 mm for 1 % Ag).
5. The DEF increases with tube voltage, most steeply once photons above the Ag K-edge appear (above about 27 kV); per unit mass, Ag is a weaker physical sensitizer than Gd, Pt, Au and Bi and comparable to iodine.
6. MC confirms the analytical model within 1–10 % (MC higher owing to fluorescence reabsorption and scatter) and shows a dose reduction to about 0.80 behind the loaded layer.
7. The DEF for 1 % Ag with Al 1 mm is 1.48 ± 0.02 ($k = 1$); the reference medium changes it by up to 0.057 and must be stated.
