## 1.4. Silver and silver nanoparticles as radiosensitizers

Silver ($Z = 47$) lies between the light elements of soft tissue and the heavy elements, such as gold ($Z = 79$), that dominate the literature on high-Z radiosensitizers. Reviews cover the synthesis, properties and therapeutic applications of silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) [@wei2015] and their antimicrobial and anticancer uses [@burdusel2018]; AgNPs are cheaper than gold and can be made by green synthesis, for example in egg white [@lu2012]. For radiotherapy, silver is of interest for its biological activity and because its K-edge lies inside the spectra of x-ray tubes operated at a few tens of kilovolts.

The biological activity of AgNPs is linked to their toxicity: they are cytotoxic and genotoxic in human cells, inducing ROS, DNA damage and cell-cycle arrest with mitochondrial involvement [@asharani2009]. Such effects belong to the chemical and biological stages of radio-enhancement (Section 1.3), in which ROS, oxidative stress and inhibited DNA repair amplify the physical damage [@kuncic2018; @rosa2017], so AgNPs may sensitize cells beyond their physical dose enhancement. Their cytotoxicity, however, makes selective tumour accumulation a prerequisite of therapy, and the effect of the tumour dose enhancement on healthy tissues must be assessed [@caglar2024].

Experimental evidence is summarised in {{REF:tab:lit_ag}}. Since the early report by Xu et al. [@xu2009], glioma has been the most studied model [@liu2013; @liu2016; @liu2018; @zhao2019], including hypoxic cells, which are less radiosensitive because of the oxygen effect [@hall2018]; breast [@swanner2015] and colorectal [@habiba2019] cancers have also been studied. The studies differ in particles, coatings, biological models and irradiation conditions and cannot be compared quantitatively. Moreover, because the physical dose enhancement depends strongly on the spectrum (Section 1.3), an enhancement ratio reported without the spectrum cannot be divided into physical and non-physical contributions.

The advantage of AgNPs over AuNPs [@liu2016] is instructive. Per unit mass, silver and gold absorb photon energy comparably below about 12 keV, but gold absorbs more strongly between about 12 and 45 keV ({{REF:fig03_ratio}}), and in megavoltage beams the macroscopic enhancement is much smaller even for gold [@cho2005] because Compton scattering, which depends only weakly on the atomic number, dominates [@podgorsak2016]. A larger macroscopic physical dose enhancement is thus an unlikely explanation for the advantage of silver, both below 45 keV and at megavoltage energies. This points to substantial contributions from the chemical and biological activity of silver [@asharani2009] and from nanoscale energy deposition [@mcmahon2011]; confirming it requires a quantitative estimate of the physical part for a well-defined beam.

Dosimetric studies of silver are fewer. MC simulations have addressed AgNPs in a brain tumour during brachytherapy [@taha2019], the effect on surrounding healthy tissues [@caglar2024] and silver versus gold in layered skin [@esitmez2026]; dose enhancement has also been measured in AgNP-enriched dose gels [@merkis2023], and its depth dependence in orthovoltage beams has been studied for nanoparticles in a phantom [@chow2023]. A systematic analysis of how kilovoltage tube design and filtration determine the DEF of silver-loaded tissue has not been found in the literature reviewed.

The choice of beam follows from the atomic data of silver ({{REF:t2_2}}): K-edge 25.514 keV, L3 edge 3.351 keV and Kα1 line 22.163 keV. With $\omega_K = 0.831$, only about 17 % of K vacancies relax by Auger transitions that deposit their energy locally [@krause1979; @bambynek1972]; the other 83 % emit mainly Kα photons of about 22 keV, whose mean free path of about 1.5 cm in soft tissue carries this energy away. The ratio of the mass energy-absorption coefficients of silver and ICRU-44 soft tissue is 21–32 between 8 keV and the K-edge but 76–139 between the edge and 45 keV (Sections 1.2 and 1.3). Unlike the gold K-edge (80.725 keV), usable only above about 81 kV, the silver K-edge lies inside the spectrum of any tube operated above about 26 kV. The most favourable spectra therefore carry much of their energy fluence between the Ag K-edge and the end-point energy (45 keV here), far above the Cu K lines at 8.0–8.9 keV that dominate the photon fluence of the unfiltered copper-anode beam.

## 1.5. Methods of modelling x-ray tube spectra

The DEF calculation requires the source spectrum. Measurement with semiconductor detectors [@birch1979] needs detector-response corrections and is impractical for every configuration, so parametric studies use calculated spectra, obtained by the four groups of methods compared in {{REF:tab:methods}}. The empirical model TASMIP (tungsten anode spectral model using interpolating polynomials) interpolates measured tungsten-anode spectra from 30 to 140 kV [@boone1997], and spektr 3.0 is based on the TASMICS model [@punnoose2016]. Such models are fast and accurate within their data range but tied to the anode material, angle and inherent filtration of the measured tubes; added filtration can be included as attenuation, but the influence of the anode material and angle, studied in this work, cannot.

Semi-empirical models go back to Kramers' theory, in which the energy emitted from a thick target per unit photon-energy interval is proportional to $Z(T_0 - E)$, where $Z$ is the target atomic number, $T_0$ the initial electron kinetic energy and $E$ the photon energy [@kramers1923; @podgorsak2016]. Birch and Marshall compared their model with Ge(Li) detector measurements [@birch1979], and Tucker, Barnes and Chakraborty proposed the TBC model for tungsten [@tucker1991]. These models combine a simplified bremsstrahlung cross-section with approximate electron penetration and target self-absorption and fit some parameters to experiment. They are cheap and show the dependence on voltage, anode angle and filtration explicitly, but their accuracy is limited by the simplified physics and their parameters are target-specific, usually for tungsten.

Physics-based deterministic models replace most empirical parameters with theoretical or MC-derived data. Electron-penetration characteristics derived from MC [@poludniowski2007a] were used to calculate x-ray production and filtration in the target [@poludniowski2007b] and implemented for tungsten in SpekCalc [@poludniowski2009]. The later SpekPy v2 toolkit offers several physics models, including the casim model used here, and several anodes, including copper [@poludniowski2021], available for 10–50 kV in version 2.5.4 [@spekpy_soft]. It builds on models of K and L emission [@omar2018] and of bremsstrahlung energy and angular distributions [@omar2020a], validated from 20 to 300 kV [@omar2020b] and against measured spectra and HVLs [@bujila2020]. Such models are fast and physically founded but limited to the anodes and voltages with precomputed transport data.

MC simulation of coupled electron–photon transport in the tube gives the most complete description, for example MCNP4C for diagnostic and mammography spectra [@ay2004] or EGS4/BEAM for kilovoltage radiotherapy units [@verhaegen1999]; other general-purpose codes are EGSnrc [@kawrakow2000], Geant4 [@agostinelli2003] and PENELOPE [@salvat2019]. It includes electron backscattering, the bremsstrahlung angular distribution and the real tube geometry. However, it needs detailed geometric data and long computation times, so it suits benchmarks rather than extensive parametric studies.

| Approach | Examples | Advantages | Limitations |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Empirical | TASMIP [@boone1997]; spektr 3.0 [@punnoose2016] | Fast; accurate within the data range | Fixed anode material, angle and filtration |
| Semi-empirical | Kramers [@kramers1923]; Birch–Marshall [@birch1979]; TBC [@tucker1991] | Simple; explicit parameter dependence | Simplified physics; fitted parameters, mostly tungsten |
| Physics-based deterministic | SpekCalc [@poludniowski2009]; SpekPy v2 [@poludniowski2021]; developed model (this work) | Fast; physically founded; few parameters | Approximate electron transport; limited anodes and voltages |

Whatever the model, the spectrum must be related to measurable quantities. In kilovoltage dosimetry, beam quality is specified by the HVL together with the tube voltage [@hill2014], as in the protocol of the American Association of Physicists in Medicine for 40–300 kV beams (AAPM TG-61) [@ma2001], the code of practice of the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA TRS-398) [@iaea_trs398] and the standard radiation qualities of the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC 61267) [@iec61267]. The HVL is thus the natural quantity for comparing models with each other and with measurements. It does not, however, fix the energy-fluence fraction above the Ag K-edge, so beams with similar HVLs can give different DEFs for silver, as Chapter 3 shows for filters with absorption edges ({{REF:t3_4}}).

Most of these models were developed for tungsten, the usual target of medical tubes [@bushberg2012]. Copper-anode tubes serve mainly x-ray diffraction (XRD), where Cu Kα is the working line, Kβ is suppressed by a nickel filter [@cullity2001] and the bremsstrahlung continuum is unwanted background. For dose enhancement by silver, by contrast, the continuum is the useful part of the beam, because only bremsstrahlung photons can exceed the Ag K-edge; yet the full spectrum of copper-anode tubes and its dependence on tube parameters have received much less attention. This work therefore constructed a semi-analytical physics-based model of the copper-anode tube, the developed model (DM). It combines the Bethe collision stopping power in the continuous slowing-down approximation (CSDA) [@bethe1930; @icru37], the non-relativistic Bethe–Heitler bremsstrahlung cross-section with the Elwert factor [@bethe1934; @elwert1939; @koch1959] and the Gryzinski K-shell ionisation cross-section [@gryzinski1965], with one adjustable parameter, the electron depth factor (Chapter 2). All dependences on tube parameters remain explicit, and SpekPy v2 serves as the validation reference.

## 1.6. Conclusions to Chapter 1

The review leads to the following conclusions, which determine the tasks of this work. The physical dose enhancement by a high-Z element in kilovoltage beams is governed by its mass energy-absorption coefficient relative to that of tissue, which for silver rises sharply above the K-edge at 25.514 keV; the physical efficacy of silver is thus set mainly by the fraction of the energy fluence above this edge, and a DEF is meaningful only together with its spectrum. AgNPs radiosensitize glioma, breast and colorectal cancer models, in one study more effectively than AuNPs, and are themselves cytotoxic and generate ROS. However, the macroscopic physical contribution of silver for a well-characterised kilovoltage beam, needed to separate physical from chemical and biological effects, has not been determined systematically.

Spectrum models are well developed for tungsten anodes but much less so for the full spectrum of copper-anode tubes. This work therefore develops a semi-analytical model of the 45 kV copper-anode tube spectrum, validates it against SpekPy v2, and uses both models to calculate the spectrum as a function of tube design and filtration and the DEF of silver-loaded soft tissue as a function of concentration, depth and filtration. The results are verified by MC simulation, silver is compared with other high-Z elements, and the DEF uncertainty is estimated as a basis for practical recommendations.
