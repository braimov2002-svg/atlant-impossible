## 3.7. Depth dependence and self-shielding

In tissue samples, phantoms or small animals the Ag-loaded target lies beneath tissue that modifies the beam. Two cases were therefore calculated with the SpekPy spectra in the primary-beam kerma approximation (Section 2.6), without scattered radiation (assessed in Section 3.9): a small loaded region at depth $z$ in Ag-free soft tissue (equation {{REF:eq:def_depth}}) and a region loaded uniformly from the surface to $z$, in which Ag also attenuates the beam (equation {{REF:eq:def_slab}}).

In the first case the DEF increases monotonically with depth for all filters ({{REF:fig18_def_depth}}; {{REF:t3_8}} also gives the dose $D/D_0$ relative to the surface dose $D_0$), because tissue preferentially absorbs low-energy photons and shifts the energy fluence to where the Ag/tissue ratio of $\mu_{en}/\rho$ is larger. Without a filter the DEF for 1 % Ag rises from 1.20 at the surface to 1.56 at 20 mm, but the dose falls to 0.0090 at 10 mm as the Cu K lines and the soft bremsstrahlung are absorbed: the tissue acts as an added filter, with the trade-off of Section 3.6. With Al 1 mm, which has already hardened the beam, the DEF rises only to 1.69 at 20 mm, while the dose falls only to 0.439 at 10 mm.

{{FIG:fig18_def_depth|Dose enhancement factor for 1 % Ag versus the depth of the Ag-loaded region in Ag-free ICRU-44 soft tissue for the unfiltered 45 kV beam and four added filters (SpekPy spectra, primary beam, kerma approximation).}}

{{TABLE:t3_8}}

The Cu and Sn filters are the extremes. The 0.1 mm Cu beam consists of the Cu Kα line and a hard bremsstrahlung tail (Section 3.4); once tissue absorbs the Kα line, the DEF rises steeply from 1.29 to 1.77 at 5 mm, close to the surface value with 0.2 mm Cu (1.80), and beyond about 2 mm it exceeds all others. With Sn 0.1 mm it changes least (from 1.44 to 1.57 at 20 mm), because the photons above the Sn K-edge (29.2 keV) are already strongly attenuated and tissue hardens the remaining narrow band only slightly. The ranking of filters by surface DEF thus does not hold for deep-seated targets.

Inside a region loaded from the surface the trend reverses ({{REF:fig19_def_slab}}), because Ag strongly attenuates the photons above its K-edge that produce the enhancement (its mass attenuation coefficient increases 5.8-fold at 25.514 keV, from 9.53 to 55.4 cm²/g). With Al 1 mm the DEF falls from 1.48 at the surface to 1.21 at 10 mm for 1 % Ag and from 1.96 to 1.27 for 2 % Ag, so self-shielding grows with the concentration and the linear relation of equation {{REF:eq:def_lin}} holds only for thin layers. The Sn-filtered beam, concentrated just above the Ag K-edge, loses more of its enhancement (from 1.44 to 1.15 at 10 mm for 1 % Ag). The enhancement in a loaded region several millimetres thick is thus non-uniform and on average much lower than at the surface.

## 3.8. Influence of tube voltage and comparison with other high-Z elements

The DEF versus tube voltage $U$ ({{REF:fig20_def_kvp}}, {{REF:t3_10}}) was calculated with SpekPy for 20–50 kV, where it supports the Cu anode, and with the DM for 60–80 kV. As the maximum photon energy is $eU$, where $e$ is the elementary charge, photons above the Ag K-edge exist only above 25.5 kV, and below this voltage the DEF changes little. From 26–27 kV the slope increases; between 30 and 50 kV the DEF with Al 1 mm rises from 1.31 to 1.52 with a decreasing slope, consistent with the ±0.009 per ±1 kV found at 45 kV in Section 3.10. Above 50 kV the growth slows further (1.68 at 80 kV), because the spectrum extends beyond the maximum of the Ag/tissue ratio near 40–45 keV. Since the DM gives slightly lower DEFs than SpekPy at 45 kV (1.464 versus 1.482), the values at 60–80 kV are, if anything, underestimated, and part of the flattening between 50 and 60 kV reflects the change of the spectrum model.

{{FIG:fig20_def_kvp|Dose enhancement factor for 1 % Ag versus tube voltage with Al 0.5 mm and Al 1.0 mm filters: SpekPy v2 for 20–50 kV (solid lines) and the developed model for 60–80 kV (dashed lines); the vertical line marks the Ag K-edge.}}

{{TABLE:t3_10}}

For soft tissue containing 1 % by mass of I, Gd, Pt, Au or Bi ({{REF:fig21_elements}}, {{REF:t3_9}}), all elements give DEFs of 1.20–1.33 without added filtration, because the dose is dominated by the Cu K lines and by photons below 15 keV, where Pt, Au and Bi (L-edges at about 12–16 keV, above the Cu lines) absorb about as much energy per unit mass as Ag ({{REF:fig03_ratio}}). Only Gd (1.33) stands out, since its L-edges at 7.2–8.4 keV make it a strong absorber of the Cu K lines.

{{FIG:fig21_elements|Dose enhancement factor of soft tissue with 1 % by mass of Ag, I, Gd, Pt, Au or Bi at 45 kV without added filter and with Al 1.0 mm or Sn 0.1 mm filters (SpekPy spectra, kerma approximation).}}

{{TABLE:t3_9}}

With Al 1 mm the picture changes completely: the DEF reaches 2.13 for Pt, 2.16 for Au, 2.23 for Bi and 1.71 for Gd, against 1.48 for Ag and 1.46 for I. Above their L-edges Pt, Au and Bi absorb more energy per unit mass than Ag over the whole filtered spectrum, including 15–25 keV, where Ag absorbs only through its L and outer shells. Gd, whose K-edge (50.2 keV) lies beyond the spectrum, exceeds Ag only below the Ag K-edge, a region that still contributes much of the tissue dose, and iodine resembles Ag but has a higher K-edge (33.2 keV). The Sn 0.1 mm filter lowers the DEF for Ag and I (1.44 for both) by attenuating the photons above 29.2 keV, where the Ag/tissue ratio reaches 100–139 and the iodine K-edge lies, but slightly raises it for the heavier elements, whose ratio varies much less, through the stronger suppression of soft photons (HVL 0.90 versus 0.83 mm Al).

Per unit mass, Ag is thus a weaker physical sensitizer at 45 kV than Gd, Pt, Au and Bi and comparable to iodine: with Al 1 mm its $\mathrm{DEF}-1$ (0.48) is less than half that of Au. Its advantages lie elsewhere: a K-edge inside the 45 kV spectrum, which allows the enhancement to be tuned by filtration; the biological activity of silver nanoparticles (AgNPs), which generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) [@asharani2009]; and a much lower cost than gold or platinum.

## 3.9. Monte Carlo verification

The analytical DEFs neglect scattered radiation and assume that the energy of Ag K-fluorescence photons, excluded from $\mu_{en}/\rho$ of Ag, escapes. Both assumptions were tested with the Monte Carlo (MC) code of Section 2.7: a laterally infinite 30 mm slab of ICRU-44 soft tissue with 1 % Ag in a 10 mm layer at 2–12 mm depth was irradiated by a broad parallel beam with the unfiltered and Al 1 mm-filtered SpekPy spectra ($4\times10^{6}$ histories in 5 batches, 0.25 mm dose bins). The analytical reference is the primary-beam kerma model for the same geometry, including the attenuation by Ag.

Both methods give the same shape of the depth profile ({{REF:fig22_mc}}; regional averages in {{REF:t3_11}}). With Al 1 mm the DEF rises sharply at the layer entrance and falls towards the exit through self-shielding. For the unfiltered beam an entrance peak is followed by a minimum at about 3–4 mm and a rise (a broad analytical maximum near 7–8 mm): near the entrance Ag attenuates mainly the soft components that still dominate the dose, whereas deeper, after their absorption, beam hardening prevails. The agreement is within 1.2 % (unfiltered) and 3.0 % (Al 1 mm) at the entrance and 6.5 % and 10.1 % at the exit, the MC values being higher in all regions.

{{FIG:fig22_mc|Depth profiles of the dose enhancement factor in soft tissue with a 10 mm layer of 1 % Ag at 2–12 mm (grey band) for the unfiltered beam (left) and Al 1.0 mm (right): Monte Carlo (solid line; shaded band ±2 standard errors) and the analytical primary-beam kerma model (dashed line).}}

{{TABLE:t3_11}}

Two effects explain this excess. Part of the Ag K-fluorescence (about 22 keV) is reabsorbed within the layer, since its mean free path in tissue (about 1.5 cm) is comparable to the layer thickness and obliquely emitted photons travel long paths in the laterally infinite layer; in addition, scattered photons contribute to the dose. Both contributions build up with depth, hence the growing difference; backward fluorescence also lifts the MC DEF slightly above unity in front of the layer for the filtered beam. Behind the layer the dose drops to 0.80 of the Ag-free value (0.76 analytically), which protects normal tissue behind a loaded tumour but also lowers the dose in tumour regions behind a heavily loaded region. The analytical model thus underestimates the DEF in a thick loaded region by up to 10 % and is in this respect conservative, while near the entrance it agrees within 1–3 %.

## 3.10. Uncertainty and sensitivity analysis

The central result, DEF = 1.482 for 1 % Ag with Al 1 mm, was tested by varying each parameter in turn within its plausible uncertainty ({{REF:fig23_sensitivity}}, {{REF:t3_12}}). The tissue coefficient was varied in the loaded and the reference medium together, so that it acts only through $\langle R\rangle$ in equation {{REF:eq:def_lin}}; the spectrum model is represented by the DM–SpekPy difference, and the depth factor $\eta$ (±25 %) by changes relative to the DM value of 1.464.

Spectrum model: DM versus SpekPy; reference medium: ICRU four-component tissue (larger change) and water; η: relative to the DM value of 1.464.}}

{{TABLE:t3_12}}

The largest change, 0.057, comes from the reference medium (1.539 for the ICRU four-component tissue, 1.496 for water). Both lack the trace elements Na, P, S, Cl and K, which raise the energy absorption of ICRU-44 tissue, and a less absorbing reference medium increases $\langle R\rangle$ and hence the DEF. This is a matter of definition, excluded from the combined uncertainty, but the reference medium must always be stated. Among the other parameters the spectrum model dominates (0.018), followed by the Ag coefficient, the anode angle and the tube voltage (0.009–0.010 each). A smaller anode angle raises the DEF (1.492 at 9°, 1.476 at 15°) through stronger self-filtration in the anode, whereas the Be window is irrelevant (0.0002), since the Al filter removes the soft photons it would attenuate. Treating each maximum change as the half-width of a rectangular distribution and the parameters as uncorrelated, the combined standard uncertainty is

$$
u_c(\mathrm{DEF}) = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{\Delta_i^{2}}{3}}
$$ {#eq:uc_def}

where $\Delta_i$ is the maximum absolute change of the DEF caused by the $i$-th parameter and $n = 8$ is the number of parameters included (all except the reference medium). Thus $u_c = 0.016$, i.e. DEF = 1.48 ± 0.02 with the coverage factor $k = 1$, or ± 0.03 with $k = 2$, more than an order of magnitude below both the enhancement ($\mathrm{DEF}-1 = 0.48$) and the differences between the main filter options, so the conclusions about filtration are robust. Incorrect interaction data would matter far more: the xraylib 4.3.0 function CS_Energy would give 1.72 instead of 1.48 (Section 2.8).

## 3.11. Discussion

The results agree with earlier work on dose enhancement by high-Z agents. Enhancement in iodine-loaded tumours irradiated with kilovoltage x-rays was calculated in early studies [@mello1983; @solberg1992]; MC estimates for gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) showed a much larger macroscopic enhancement for kilovoltage than for megavoltage beams [@cho2005]; and the enhancement by nanoparticles of various elements has been characterised versus photon energy with a kerma-based approach like the present one [@roeske2007]. The depth dependence in orthovoltage beams has been studied by MC calculations in phantoms [@chow2023], and Section 3.7 shows that for Ag a single surface value cannot characterise a thick target. This work adds a low-voltage Cu-anode tube, whose spectrum is dominated by characteristic lines below the Ag K-edge and whose attainable DEF is determined by filtration rather than by tube design.

MC studies of silver address AgNPs implanted in a brain tumour during brachytherapy [@taha2019], the effect of tumour dose enhancement by AgNPs on the surrounding healthy tissues [@caglar2024] and a comparison of Ag and Au nanoparticles in layered skin geometries [@esitmez2026]. Their conditions differ from the present ones, so only qualitative points of contact exist: the dose reduction to about 0.80 behind the loaded layer bears on the healthy-tissue doses addressed in [@caglar2024], and the per-mass DEFs of Au and Ag (2.16 versus 1.48 with Al 1 mm) on the comparison in [@esitmez2026]. Measurements of dose enhancement in AgNP-enriched dose gels [@merkis2023] could test the calculated DEF if the beam spectrum is specified.

A larger discrepancy arises with radiobiological studies. AgNPs have been reported to sensitize cancer cells to ionising radiation [@xu2009] and to act as radiosensitizers for glioma [@liu2013], and they outperformed AuNPs in U251 glioma cells in vitro and in an intracranial mouse model [@liu2016]. Such effects are quantified by the sensitizer enhancement ratio (SER), the ratio of doses giving the same effect without and with the sensitizer, which, unlike the DEF, reflects all mechanisms of sensitization [@hall2018]. Macroscopic physics predicts the opposite ranking of Ag and Au at equal mass fraction and only a small enhancement at low loading (DEF 1.048 for 0.1 % Ag with Al 1 mm). A direct numerical comparison is not possible, as the biological effect also depends on radiation quality, concentration and cellular localisation, but the reported radiosensitization by AgNPs, especially their advantage over AuNPs, is unlikely to result from macroscopic dose enhancement alone. Two mechanisms may account for the difference: AgNPs cause ROS formation, DNA damage, cell-cycle arrest and impaired mitochondrial function in human cells [@asharani2009], and the dose enhancement around gold nanoparticles is highly localised [@jones2010], nanoscale energy deposition explaining biological effects beyond the macroscopic enhancement [@mcmahon2011]. The macroscopic DEF is thus only the physical baseline of a radio-enhancement that also has chemical and biological stages [@kuncic2018].

The results have several limitations. The kerma approximation ignores the nanoscale dose heterogeneity, and Ag was assumed to be distributed homogeneously, whereas radiosensitization by nanoparticles depends on their size, concentration and location [@lechtman2011]. The DM assumes isotropic bremsstrahlung emission and neglects electron backscattering from the anode, which together with the approximate Born cross-section overestimates the absolute output (DM/SpekPy air-kerma ratio 1.08–1.42) without affecting the DEF; its 25–43 % deficit in the Cu K lines is why the main DEF results were obtained with SpekPy spectra. The heel effect was not modelled, the analytical depth calculations neglect scatter and fluorescence reabsorption (underestimating the DEF in a thick loaded layer by up to 10 %), and the MC geometry is a simple slab. Finally, no spectrum of an actual tube was measured, although SpekPy has been validated against measured spectra and HVLs [@bujila2020], and no biological response was modelled.

For an experiment with a 45 kV Cu-anode tube, the beam should be filtered with 1–2 mm of Al (or 0.1–0.3 mm of Ti); with Al this gives a DEF of 1.48–1.62 for 1 % Ag and 2 Gy in 14.6–32.5 s at 10 cm and 40 mA ({{REF:t3_7}}). Thin Cu filters (0.05–0.1 mm) and K-edge filters with edges at 20–30 keV (Mo, Ag, Sn) should be avoided, and 0.2 mm Cu, despite the largest DEF (1.80), requires 158 s for 2 Gy. The beam quality should be verified by an HVL measurement according to the kilovoltage dosimetry protocol [@ma2001] against the calculated first HVL (0.83 mm Al with 1 mm Al, 1.27 mm Al with 2 mm Al). The Ag mass fraction should be at least 0.5 % (DEF 1.24 with 1 mm Al), since at 0.1 % the enhancement is below 5 %. For samples thicker than a few millimetres the DEF should be calculated for the actual depth and loading with NIST coefficients rather than the xraylib function CS_Energy, and it should always be reported with the spectrum (or at least tube voltage, filtration and HVL), the reference medium and the depth.

## 3.12. Conclusions to Chapter 3

1. The DM (one fitted parameter, $\eta = 0.40$) agrees with SpekPy for Al-filtered beams within −4.6 to −3.5 % in HVL and 1–2 % in DEF but underestimates the Cu K lines by 25–43 %.
2. The unfiltered spectrum is dominated by the Cu K lines (68.5 % of the photons) and gives a DEF of only 1.20 for 1 % Ag; tube design parameters change mainly the output, not the DEF (1.19–1.21).
3. Filtration is decisive: for 1 % Ag the DEF is 1.48 with Al 1 mm, 1.62 with Al 2 mm and 1.80 with Cu 0.2 mm, at a 120–1300-fold lower dose rate; at equal transmission Al and Ti are optimal and K-edge filters at 20–30 keV unfavourable.
4. With Al 1 mm the DEF rises with the depth of a loaded region in Ag-free tissue (to 1.69 at 20 mm) but falls inside a uniformly loaded region through self-shielding (to 1.21 at 10 mm for 1 % Ag).
5. The DEF increases with tube voltage, most steeply once photons above the Ag K-edge appear (above about 27 kV); per unit mass, Ag is a weaker physical sensitizer than Gd, Pt, Au and Bi and comparable to iodine.
6. MC confirms the analytical model within 1–10 % (MC higher owing to fluorescence reabsorption and scatter) and shows a dose reduction to about 0.80 behind the loaded layer.
7. The DEF for 1 % Ag with Al 1 mm is 1.48 ± 0.02 ($k = 1$); the reference medium changes it by up to 0.057 and must be stated.
