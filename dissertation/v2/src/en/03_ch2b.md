## 2.6. Dose enhancement factor: definitions and calculation

The physical radio-enhancement is quantified by the DEF, the ratio of absorbed doses at a point with and without silver under identical irradiation:

$$
\mathrm{DEF} = \frac{D_{t+\mathrm{Ag}}}{D_t}
$$ {#eq:def}

where $D_{t+\mathrm{Ag}}$ and $D_t$ are the absorbed doses in silver-loaded and unloaded (reference) tissue. This macroscopic DEF assumes a homogeneous silver distribution and doses averaged over volumes much larger than cells and electron ranges.

Below 50 keV secondary electrons have sub-millimetre ranges in tissue, so charged-particle equilibrium (CPE) holds except near interfaces [@attix1986; @andreo2017] and the dose equals the collision kerma {{REF:eq:dose_cpe}}, practically the kerma in low-Z media [@icru85]. By analogy with {{REF:eq:kerma}},

$$
D = \int_{0}^{T_0} \Psi(E)\,\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}(E)\,\mathrm{d}E
$$ {#eq:dose_spec}

where $\Psi(E) = E\,\Phi(E)$ is the spectral energy fluence, $\Phi(E)$ the spectral photon fluence {{REF:eq:fluence}}, $\mu_{en}/\rho$ the mass energy-absorption coefficient of the medium and $T_0$ = 45 keV the maximum photon energy. Since $\mu_{en}/\rho$ excludes the energy of scattered and fluorescence photons [@seltzer1993], the Ag K x-rays ($\omega_K = 0.831$) are assumed to escape. For a silver mass fraction $w$ the mixture rule gives [@nist_xraymass]

$$
\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_{t+\mathrm{Ag}} = (1-w)\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_t + w\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_{\mathrm{Ag}}
$$ {#eq:mixture}

where $t$ and Ag denote tissue and silver; mass fractions of 0.1–2 % are considered. Substituting {{REF:eq:dose_spec}} and {{REF:eq:mixture}} into {{REF:eq:def}} gives the surface DEF:

$$
\mathrm{DEF} = \frac{\int \Psi(E)\left[(1-w)\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_t + w\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_{\mathrm{Ag}}\right]\mathrm{d}E}{\int \Psi(E)\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_t\mathrm{d}E}
$$ {#eq:def_spec}

The fluence normalisation cancels, so the DEF depends only on the spectral shape, and DM spectra are usable despite their less accurate absolute output. As the numerator is linear in $w$, {{REF:eq:def_spec}} becomes exactly

$$
\mathrm{DEF} = 1 + w\left(\langle R\rangle - 1\right)
$$ {#eq:def_lin}

with

$$
\langle R\rangle = \frac{\int \Psi(E)\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_{\mathrm{Ag}}\mathrm{d}E}{\int \Psi(E)\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_t\mathrm{d}E} = \frac{\int R(E)\,d_t(E)\,\mathrm{d}E}{\int d_t(E)\,\mathrm{d}E}
$$ {#eq:ratio_avg}

where $R(E) = (\mu_{en}/\rho)_{\mathrm{Ag}}/(\mu_{en}/\rho)_t$ and $d_t(E) = \Psi(E)\,(\mu_{en}/\rho)_t$ is the dose spectrum of unloaded tissue, so $\langle R\rangle$, the dose-weighted mean of $R(E)$, depends on the spectrum alone. For ICRU-44 tissue, $R(E)$ jumps at the Ag K-edge (25.514 keV) from about 32 to about 76 and reaches about 139 at 40–45 keV. Shifting the dose spectrum above the K-edge therefore raises $\langle R\rangle$ and the slope, the reason why filtration increases the DEF (Section 3.3).

Where silver is present only locally at depth $z$, without perturbing the beam, the primary-beam DEF is

$$
\mathrm{DEF}(z) = \frac{\int \Psi(E)\,e^{-\mu_t(E)\,z}\left[(1-w)\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_t + w\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_{\mathrm{Ag}}\right]\mathrm{d}E}{\int \Psi(E)\,e^{-\mu_t(E)\,z}\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_t\mathrm{d}E}
$$ {#eq:def_depth}

where $\Psi(E)$ is the surface energy fluence and $\mu_t(E) = \rho_t\,(\mu/\rho)_t(E)$ the linear attenuation coefficient of tissue of density $\rho_t$ = 1.06 g/cm³; scatter and inverse-square fall-off are neglected, and the denominator normalised to $z = 0$ is the relative primary dose in unloaded tissue. Tissue removes first the low-energy photons, for which $R(E)$ is small, so the DEF increases with depth. Silver filling the region above the point also attenuates the beam, most strongly just above the K-edge, where its $\mu/\rho$ increases 5.8-fold. For a slab loaded from the surface to beyond $z$,

$$
\mathrm{DEF}_{\mathrm{slab}}(z) = \frac{\int \Psi(E)\,e^{-\mu_{t+\mathrm{Ag}}(E)\,z}\left[(1-w)\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_t + w\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_{\mathrm{Ag}}\right]\mathrm{d}E}{\int \Psi(E)\,e^{-\mu_t(E)\,z}\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_t\mathrm{d}E}
$$ {#eq:def_slab}

where $\mu_{t+\mathrm{Ag}}(E) = \rho_t\left[(1-w)(\mu/\rho)_t + w\,(\mu/\rho)_{\mathrm{Ag}}\right]$ is the linear attenuation coefficient of the loaded medium (density approximated by $\rho_t$). The added attenuation preferentially removes the most effective photons, so $\mathrm{DEF}_{\mathrm{slab}}$ decreases with depth (self-shielding) and is no longer linear in $w$. At $w$ = 1 % silver attenuates just above its K-edge about as much as the tissue itself, and behind a loaded region the dose falls below its silver-free value.

The reference medium is ICRU-44 soft tissue (H 10.2, C 14.3, N 3.4, O 70.8, Na 0.2, P 0.3, S 0.3, Cl 0.2 and K 0.3 % by mass; 1.06 g/cm³) [@icru44], with water and the ICRU four-component tissue as alternatives; the choice affects the DEF and must be reported. On the subcellular scale, short-range photo- and Auger electrons deposit much of their energy close to nanoparticles [@jones2010], which can produce biological effects beyond the macroscopic enhancement (local effect model) [@mcmahon2011] depending on photon energy and particle size, concentration and location [@lechtman2011]. The calculated DEF is thus the physical baseline for identifying chemical, biological and nanoscale contributions.

## 2.7. Cross-sections of photon interaction with silver

When photons pass through a material, they interact with its atoms, atomic nuclei and electrons. The main interaction mechanisms are coherent scattering, the photoelectric effect, Compton scattering, electron–positron pair production and the nuclear photoeffect [@chernyaev2004; @hubbell2006], and the total cross-section of photon interaction is the sum of their contributions. For kilovoltage photons only the first three processes are possible, since pair production and the nuclear photoeffect have thresholds of the order of megaelectronvolts.

In the photoelectric effect a photon is absorbed by a bound atomic electron, which is ejected with the kinetic energy $E_\gamma - E_b$, where $E_\gamma$ is the photon energy and $E_b$ the binding energy of the electron. The process is most probable on the most tightly bound electrons whose binding energy does not exceed the photon energy, i.e. on the K-shell when $E_\gamma > E_K$. In the Born approximation the photoelectric cross-section of the K-shell (in cm²) is [@chernyaev2004]

$$
(\sigma_{\mathrm{ph}})_K = 1.09\times10^{-16}\,Z^{5}\left(\frac{13.61}{E_\gamma}\right)^{7/2}
$$ {#eq:sig_k_nr}

for $E_K \ll E_\gamma \ll m_ec^2$, with $E_\gamma$ in eV, and

$$
(\sigma_{\mathrm{ph}})_K = 1.34\times10^{-33}\,\frac{Z^{5}}{E_\gamma}
$$ {#eq:sig_k_rel}

for $E_\gamma \gg m_ec^2$, with $E_\gamma$ in MeV; here $Z$ is the atomic number and $m_ec^2$ = 0.511 MeV the rest energy of the electron. The contributions of the L- and M-shells to the photoelectric effect are relatively small: the photoelectric cross-section of the L-shell is about 20 % and that of the M-shell about 5 % of the K-shell value. The total photoelectric cross-section is therefore 1.25 times the K-shell cross-section:

$$
\sigma_{\mathrm{ph}} = \frac{5}{4}\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{ph}})_K
$$ {#eq:sig_ph_tot}

Expressions {{REF:eq:sig_k_nr}}–{{REF:eq:sig_ph_tot}} show the strong dependence of the photoeffect on the atomic number ($Z^5$ per atom, i.e. about $Z^4$ per unit mass, cf. {{REF:eq:tau_scaling}}) and on the photon energy ($E_\gamma^{-7/2}$ for $E_\gamma \ll m_ec^2$) that underlies the radio-enhancement by high-Z elements. Near the absorption edges they are only estimates: for silver at 30 keV, {{REF:eq:sig_k_nr}} and {{REF:eq:sig_ph_tot}} give 6.2×10⁻²⁰ cm², about ten times the tabulated value, so the calculations use the evaluated cross-sections of the NIST XCOM database [@nist_xcom].

The Compton effect is the scattering of a photon by free electrons of the material [@compton1923]. For the Compton effect to occur, the binding energy $E_e$ of the electrons must be much smaller than the photon energy, $E_e \ll E_\gamma$. There are two types of Compton process:

1. Direct Compton effect: a photon interacts with a stationary free electron, changes its direction and transfers part of its energy to the electron.
2. Inverse Compton effect: a photon is scattered by a fast-moving electron and gains energy in the process.

In the irradiation of biological matter only the direct effect is relevant. The total cross-section of Compton scattering on a free electron follows from the Klein–Nishina formula [@klein1929]; for $\varepsilon \ll 1$ it is

$$
\sigma_{C} = \frac{8\pi}{3}\,\frac{e^{4}}{m_e^{2}c^{4}}\left(1 - 2\varepsilon + \frac{26}{5}\varepsilon^{2} + \ldots\right)
$$ {#eq:sig_c_low}

and for $\varepsilon \gg 1$

$$
\sigma_{C} = \pi r_e^{2}\,\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left(\frac{1}{2} + \ln 2\varepsilon\right)
$$ {#eq:sig_c_high}

where $\varepsilon = E_\gamma/(m_ec^2)$ is the photon energy in units of the electron rest energy, $e$ the elementary charge, $r_e = e^2/(m_ec^2)$ = 2.818×10⁻¹³ cm the classical electron radius and $8\pi r_e^2/3$ = 6.65×10⁻²⁵ cm² the Thomson cross-section, the low-energy limit of {{REF:eq:sig_c_low}}. The Compton cross-section per atom is $Z$ times larger. For bound electrons at low photon energies it is smaller than this value; for silver at 30 keV the difference is about one third.

The formation of an electron–positron pair is the process in which a photon interacts with the Coulomb field of an atomic nucleus or the field of an atomic electron, resulting in the creation of an electron and a positron:

$$
\gamma \rightarrow e^{-} + e^{\text{+}}
$$ {#eq:pair}

Pair production occurs only when the photon energy exceeds a threshold. In the field of a nucleus, which takes up a negligible recoil energy, the threshold energy is

$$
E_{\mathrm{thr},1} \approx 2m_ec^{2} = 1.022~\mathrm{MeV}
$$ {#eq:thr1}

and in the field of an atomic electron, which shares the momentum with the pair (triplet production), it is

$$
E_{\mathrm{thr},2} \approx 4m_ec^{2} = 2.044~\mathrm{MeV}
$$ {#eq:thr2}

In the Coulomb field of a nucleus, disregarding the screening by atomic electrons, the total cross-section of pair production for photon energies $m_ec^2 \ll E_\gamma \ll m_ec^2/(\alpha Z^{1/3})$ is [@chernyaev2004]

$$
\sigma_{\mathrm{pair}} = Z^{2}\alpha r_e^{2}\left(\frac{28}{9}\ln 2\varepsilon - \frac{218}{27}\right)
$$ {#eq:pair_noscr}

and with complete screening, for $E_\gamma \gg m_ec^2/(\alpha Z^{1/3})$,

$$
\sigma_{\mathrm{pair}} = Z^{2}\alpha r_e^{2}\left(\frac{28}{9}\ln\frac{183}{Z^{1/3}} - \frac{2}{27}\right)
$$ {#eq:pair_scr}

where $Z$ is the atomic number of the nucleus, $\alpha \approx 1/137$ the fine-structure constant, $r_e$ the classical electron radius and $\varepsilon = E_\gamma/(m_ec^2)$. For silver $m_ec^2/(\alpha Z^{1/3})$ = 19.4 MeV, and {{REF:eq:pair_scr}} gives 1.55×10⁻²³ cm², close to the XCOM value in the nuclear field at very high energies (1.50×10⁻²³ cm² at 100 GeV). The cross-sections of the photoelectric effect, Compton and coherent scattering and electron–positron pair production for silver are shown in {{REF:f2_xsec_ag}}.

## 2.8. Mass attenuation coefficients of soft tissue, brain, bone, water and silver

A narrow beam of monoenergetic photons with an incident intensity $I_0$, penetrating a layer of material with mass thickness $x$ and density $\rho$, emerges with the intensity $I$ given by the exponential attenuation law [@nist_xraymass]:

$$
I = I_0\,e^{-(\mu/\rho)\,x}
$$ {#eq:att_law}

from which $\mu/\rho$ can be obtained from measured values of $I_0$, $I$ and $x$. The mass thickness is the mass per unit area, obtained by multiplying the thickness $t$ by the density $\rho$, i.e. $x = \rho t$.

The total cross-section per atom can be written as the sum of the contributions of the principal photon interactions:

$$
\sigma_{\mathrm{tot}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{pe}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{coh}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{incoh}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{pair}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{trip}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{ph.n}}
$$ {#eq:sig_tot}

where $\sigma_{\mathrm{pe}}$ is the atomic photoeffect cross-section, $\sigma_{\mathrm{coh}}$ and $\sigma_{\mathrm{incoh}}$ are the coherent (Rayleigh) and incoherent (Compton) scattering cross-sections, $\sigma_{\mathrm{pair}}$ and $\sigma_{\mathrm{trip}}$ the cross-sections of electron–positron pair production in the fields of the nucleus and of the atomic electrons, and $\sigma_{\mathrm{ph.n}}$ the photonuclear cross-section. The mass attenuation coefficient of an element is calculated as

$$
\frac{\mu}{\rho} = \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{tot}}}{uA}
$$ {#eq:mu_from_sigma}

where $u$ = 1.6605×10⁻²⁴ g is the atomic mass unit and $A$ the relative atomic mass of the element. For mixtures and compounds, assumed homogeneous, $\mu/\rho$ is obtained by simple additivity [@nist_xraymass]:

$$
\frac{\mu}{\rho} = \sum_{i} w_i\left(\frac{\mu}{\rho}\right)_i
$$ {#eq:mu_mix}

where $w_i$ is the fraction by weight of the $i$-th element. The mass attenuation coefficients of water, soft tissue, brain, cortical bone and silver obtained in this way for photon energies of 1–100 keV are shown in {{REF:f2_media_mu}} and listed for selected energies in {{REF:tab:media_mu}}.

In the whole range the coefficients decrease steeply with energy, approximately as $E^{-3}$ where photoabsorption dominates: for soft tissue $\mu/\rho$ falls from 3.7×10³ cm²/g at 1 keV to 5.4 cm²/g at 10 keV and 0.17 cm²/g at 100 keV. Water, soft tissue and brain consist mainly of hydrogen, carbon, nitrogen and oxygen and have practically equal coefficients (within 1 % at 10–30 keV), so water is a good substitute for soft tissue. Cortical bone, which contains calcium and phosphorus, attenuates photons 5.3 times more strongly than soft tissue at 10 keV and 3.5 times at 30 keV. The difference nearly vanishes at 100 keV (a factor of 1.1), where Compton scattering, which is proportional to the electron density, prevails. The calcium K-edge at 4.04 keV is visible in the bone curve. Silver has the largest coefficient in the whole range. Its curve shows the L-edges at 3.35–3.81 keV and the K-edge at 25.514 keV, where $\mu/\rho$ jumps from 9.6 to 55 cm²/g. Just above the K-edge, silver attenuates photons per unit mass about 108 times more strongly than soft tissue; the factor is 97 at 30 keV and 51 at 45 keV, against 22 at 20 keV. The photons of the 45 kV tube spectrum above the Ag K-edge are thus absorbed preferentially by silver, which is the physical basis of the radio-enhancement considered in Chapter 3.

## 2.9. Radiation safety when working with a kilovoltage x-ray tube

A 45 kV copper-anode tube emits very intense low-energy x-rays: the unfiltered air kerma at 10 cm is 383 mGy per mAs, i.e. 15.3 Gy/s at 40 mA, and the tissue surface receives 2 Gy in 0.12 s, so accidental hand exposure causes severe local injuries within seconds. The primary beam, scatter from the sample and holder, and housing leakage must be controlled. Protection follows the principles of justification, optimisation (dose as low as reasonably achievable) and dose limitation of the International Commission on Radiological Protection [@icrp103], implemented in the Basic Safety Standards of the International Atomic Energy Agency [@iaea_gsr3] and, in Uzbekistan, in the Law "On Radiation Safety" [@uz_law_radsafety]. Occupational effective and equivalent doses (skin, extremities, eye lens) must stay below the limits.

The main measures are technical: a shielded housing, an enclosure whose door interlocks switch off the high voltage or close the shutter, warning lights, and fixed holders and collimators that keep hands out of the beam. Staff training, local rules, personal dosimetry and dose-rate surveys, particularly after maintenance, complement them. The high-voltage generator is a further hazard, and the beryllium window must not be touched (beryllium compounds are toxic).

Filtration also serves safety: the filters that markedly raise the DEF (1 mm Al to 0.2 mm Cu) lower the air kerma rate 120–1300-fold; with 1 mm Al the surface tissue kerma rate at 40 mA falls from 16.5 to 0.137 Gy/s and the time for 2 Gy rises from 0.12 to 14.6 s, controllable by a timer or shutter. The removed soft component would be absorbed superficially: the relative primary dose at 10 mm is 0.009 of the surface value without a filter and 0.44 with 1 mm Al. The DEF-maximising filter, whose beam can be specified by its HVL [@ma2001], thus also reduces unnecessary skin dose, in line with the optimisation principle.

## 2.10. X-ray irradiation facility

The compact portable REIS-I x-ray generator available in the laboratory was adapted for experimental studies on photon activation therapy. The generator consists of two compact units: an irradiation unit containing a BS1-type x-ray tube and an electronic unit equipped with a control panel.

A series of commissioning and adjustment procedures was carried out to establish the operating modes and determine the technical characteristics of the generator. Based on these investigations, the geometry and parameters of the radiation shielding chamber were determined, together with the irradiation position of the sample and the corresponding dosimetric characteristics.

To ensure radiation safety during the operation of the REIS-I x-ray generator, the following reference safety parameters were established on the basis of the manufacturer's technical specifications. The x-ray generator complies with the radiation-safety requirements of the Basic Sanitary Rules OSP-72. Under nominal operating conditions (a tube voltage of 45 kV and a tube current of 100 µA) and with the output window of the tube closed, the protective housing of the tube reduces the exposure rate at a distance of 5–10 cm from the generator to below the maximum permissible level of 0.8 µR/s, which corresponds to an air kerma rate of about 25 µGy/h.

The lowest exposure rate (below 0.08 µR/s) is observed at the rear and lateral sides of the high-voltage (high-frequency) unit of the x-ray generator. Therefore, when the high-voltage unit is operated outside the radiation shielding chamber, the control panel and the operating personnel must be positioned behind or alongside it at a distance of at least 1.5 m.

The shielding efficiency of the chamber follows from the strong attenuation of the soft radiation of the tube in lead. At 45 keV, the highest photon energy, the linear attenuation coefficient of lead is about 120 cm⁻¹, so the 1-mm cover transmits only 6×10⁻⁶ of the primary photons. At 40 keV and 30 keV the transmission falls to 8×10⁻⁸ and 10⁻¹⁵. The residual dose rate outside the chamber, caused by leakage through joints and openings, must nevertheless be verified by dosimetric surveys (Section 2.9).

## 2.11. Conclusions to Chapter 2

1. A semi-analytical thick-target model (DM) of a reflection copper-anode x-ray tube has been formulated: Bethe electron slowing-down, non-relativistic Bethe–Heitler bremsstrahlung with the Elwert factor, direct and bremsstrahlung-induced K radiation, anode self-absorption, attenuation in window, air and filters, and one adjustable parameter, the electron depth factor $\eta$.
2. Air kerma and beam-quality parameters (HVLs, homogeneity coefficient, mean and effective energies) are calculated from the fluence spectra, the latter identically for DM and SpekPy.
3. Under CPE the DEF is a spectrum-weighted ratio of $\mu_{en}/\rho$; at the surface $\mathrm{DEF} - 1$ is exactly proportional to $w$, with a slope set by the dose spectrum above the Ag K-edge; the depth and loaded-slab forms describe beam hardening and self-shielding.
4. In silver the photoelectric effect dominates up to 0.24 MeV, Compton scattering between 0.24 and 6.6 MeV and pair production above 6.6 MeV. In the spectrum of a 45 kV tube the interaction is almost purely photoelectric, with a 5.8-fold rise of the cross-section at the K-edge (25.514 keV). The analytical cross-sections describe the dependence on $Z$ and energy, while the calculations use the NIST XCOM data.
5. Water, soft tissue and brain have practically equal mass attenuation coefficients at 1–100 keV; cortical bone attenuates 3.5–5.3 times more strongly than soft tissue at 10–30 keV, and silver 97–108 times more strongly between its K-edge and 30 keV.
6. The unfiltered output (15.3 Gy/s at 10 cm and 40 mA) requires shielding, interlocks and dosimetric control; filtration reduces dose rate and superficial dose.
7. The REIS-I x-ray generator with a BS1 tube (45 kV, 100 µA) in a lead shielding chamber with a 1-mm lead cover forms a facility for irradiating biological samples. With the output window closed the housing keeps the exposure rate below 0.8 µR/s at 5–10 cm, and the personnel must stay at least 1.5 m from the generator.
