# CHAPTER 1. RADIATION DAMAGE OF BIOLOGICAL MATTER AND RADIOSENSITIZATION BY SILVER: LITERATURE REVIEW

## 1.1. Radiation effects in biological matter: physical, chemical and biological stages

Against the background of the global cancer burden [@bray2024], radiotherapy (RT) is one of the principal treatment modalities [@baskar2012], with an evidence-based optimal utilisation rate of approximately 52 % [@delaney2005]. RT damages cellular deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) directly through secondary electrons or indirectly through the free radicals of water radiolysis [@hall2018], but energy deposition is not selective, so healthy tissues in the beam are damaged as well. When the sigmoid dose–response curves for tumour control and normal-tissue complications lie close together (a small therapeutic ratio), normal-tissue toxicity becomes the main factor limiting RT [@hall2018; @baskar2012]. Hypoxic tumour cells, less sensitive to x-rays because of the oxygen effect, aggravate the problem [@hall2018].

The effects of ionising radiation in biological matter develop in successive stages separated by many orders of magnitude in time [@hall2018; @azzam2012]. In the physical stage (about $10^{-18}$–$10^{-15}$ s) photons transfer energy to electrons, which ionise and excite the atoms and molecules along their tracks. In the physico-chemical stage (up to about $10^{-12}$ s) the ionised and excited molecules, mostly water, which forms about 70 % of the mass of soft tissue, dissociate into radicals and ions; the hydroxyl radical ·OH, the hydrated electron and the hydrogen atom are the main products of water radiolysis. In the chemical stage (up to about $10^{-6}$ s) the radicals diffuse and react with each other and with biomolecules, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) are formed in the presence of oxygen. The biological stage, from seconds to years, comprises enzymatic repair, cell-cycle arrest, cell death and, if damage is misrepaired, mutations, carcinogenesis and late tissue effects [@hall2018; @desouky2015].

Damage to DNA, the principal target of radiation in the cell, arises in two ways [@hall2018; @lomax2013]. In direct action the DNA molecule itself is ionised or excited by a secondary electron; in indirect action it is attacked by radicals, mainly ·OH, produced in the surrounding water within a few nanometres. For sparsely ionising radiation such as kilovoltage x-rays, about two thirds of the DNA damage is caused indirectly, which is why radical scavengers protect cells and oxygen, which fixes radical damage, sensitizes them (the oxygen effect) [@hall2018]. Oxidative stress does not end with irradiation: perturbed mitochondrial metabolism and inflammatory responses keep the levels of ROS elevated for long periods, and redox signalling can spread damage to non-irradiated bystander cells [@azzam2012; @desouky2015].

Fractionation [@hall2018] and conformal, image-guided techniques such as intensity-modulated RT [@khan2014] spare normal tissue but do not change the ratio of the energy absorbed per unit mass in tumour and normal cells in the same beam region. Radiosensitizers, which act more on tumour than on normal cells, include oxygen mimetics such as the nitroimidazoles [@wardman2007] and transition-metal compounds (platinum, gold, ruthenium) combining chemical and biological activity with heavy atoms [@gill2019]. Elements of high atomic number $Z$ (high-Z elements) act physically: concentrated in the tumour, they absorb more photon energy per unit mass than soft tissue, raising the tumour dose but not that of element-free surrounding tissue [@hainfeld2004; @kuncic2018]. The gain is expressed by the dose enhancement factor (DEF), the ratio of the doses in tissue with and without the agent (Section 3.3.1), which, unlike chemical sensitization, can be calculated from interaction data once the radiation field is known.

Monte Carlo (MC) calculations for gold nanoparticles showed a much larger macroscopic dose enhancement with kilovoltage than with megavoltage beams [@cho2005], because the strongly Z-dependent photoelectric effect dominates only at low energies (Section 2.1). Kilovoltage beams are used in superficial and orthovoltage therapy [@khan2014], and their dosimetry protocols specify the beam quality by the half-value layer (HVL) [@ma2001; @iaea_trs398]. The DEF therefore cannot be stated without the x-ray spectrum, which depends on the tube design and filtration; this work addresses the problem for silver and a 45 kV copper-anode tube.

## 1.2. DNA damage, mutations and chromosomal aberrations

DNA damage is a change in the chemical structure of DNA, whereas a mutation is a change in its base sequence; damage leads to a mutation when it is repaired inaccurately or replicated before repair [@jackson2009]. Ionising radiation produces single-strand breaks (SSBs), double-strand breaks (DSBs), damaged or lost bases, DNA–protein crosslinks and inter- and intra-strand crosslinks [@ward1988; @lomax2013]. One gray produces in a mammalian cell about 1000 SSBs and about 40 DSBs [@ward1988; @hall2018]. A distinctive feature of ionising radiation is clustered damage, two or more lesions within one or two helical turns formed by a single track; such sites, including complex DSBs, are difficult to repair and largely determine cell killing, which radiotherapy exploits [@lomax2013].

Misrepaired or unrepaired lesions change the DNA sequence: deletions, duplications, inversions, insertions and translocations of DNA segments [@jackson2009; @obe2002]. Mutations may be silent, may alter the encoded protein or may inactivate a gene; inactivation of the tumour suppressor gene *TP53*, whose product p53 activates DNA repair, arrests the cell cycle and triggers apoptosis when the damage is irreparable, leaves the cell vulnerable to malignant transformation [@lane1992]. At the chromosome level, misrejoined DSBs give the microscopically visible structural chromosomal aberrations (deletions, rings, dicentrics, translocations, inversions), whereas numerical aberrations change the number of chromosomes [@obe2002]. The yield of dicentric chromosomes in lymphocytes increases with dose and is used for biological dosimetry [@hall2018].

## 1.3. Effects of radiation on mitochondria and DNA repair

Mitochondria contain their own circular DNA (mtDNA) in several copies. Lacking protective histones and having a limited repair capacity, mtDNA suffers strand breaks, base damage and large deletions after irradiation and accumulates mutations more readily than nuclear DNA [@kam2013]. Radiation also changes the mtDNA copy number, the activity of the electron transport chain and oxidative phosphorylation; since the electron transport chain is the main intracellular source of ROS, damaged mitochondria sustain the oxidative stress that contributes to delayed and persistent radiation effects [@kam2013; @azzam2012]. Mitochondria are therefore regarded as a second, extranuclear target of radiation, relevant also to nanoparticles that act through ROS [@kam2013].

The cell responds to DNA damage with the DNA-damage response, a signalling network that detects lesions, arrests the cell cycle and activates repair or, if repair fails, apoptosis or senescence [@jackson2009]. Base damage and SSBs are removed by base excision repair; bulky lesions by nucleotide excision repair; replication errors by mismatch repair. DSBs, the most lethal lesions, are repaired by non-homologous end joining, active throughout the cell cycle but error-prone, and by homologous recombination, accurate but limited to the S and G2 phases, when a sister chromatid is available [@santivasi2014; @jackson2009]. Differences in these pathways between tumour and normal cells, and their inhibition, are used to widen the therapeutic window of radiotherapy [@santivasi2014; @lomax2013], and a radiosensitizer that increases the number of lesions or impairs their repair acts at this stage.

## 1.4. High-Z radiosensitizers: from iodine contrast to metal nanoparticles

Spiers first analysed how increased energy absorption in bone and secondary-electron ranges determine the soft-tissue dose in and near bone [@spiers1949], and Das and Chopra quantified the backscatter dose perturbation at tissue–high-Z interfaces in kilovoltage beams [@das1995]. Iodinated contrast media, the first agents studied systematically, sensitized cultured mammalian cells to x-rays but not to gamma rays [@matsudaira1980], implicating photoabsorption by iodine. Dose enhancement in iodine-loaded tumours was calculated for kilovoltage x-rays [@mello1983] and brain-tumour therapy [@solberg1992], and a therapy-modified computed tomography scanner delivered the first such treatment of human brain metastases [@rose1999]. Monochromatic synchrotron beams treated rats bearing iodine-loaded F98 gliomas [@adam2003] and identified the optimal energy for iodinated compounds in cells [@corde2004]; contrast-enhanced kilovoltage RT was analysed by MC [@verhaegen2005], and modified megavoltage beams were combined with iodine and gadolinium contrast [@robar2002]. Platinum drugs add a high-Z atom to a chemotherapeutic agent: cisplatin combined with monochromatic synchrotron x-rays exploiting platinum K-edge photoactivation cured rats bearing radioresistant F98 gliomas [@biston2004]. Pignol et al. analysed inner-shell ionisation and Auger cascades in radiosensitization by halogenated deoxyuridines, platinum salts and gadolinium porphyrins [@pignol2003]; when the heavy atom lies in or near DNA, short-range Auger electrons deposit their energy at this critical target, so the subcellular location of the high-Z atom matters, not only its concentration.

The modern stage began when gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) 1.9 nm in diameter markedly improved the one-year survival of tumour-bearing mice irradiated with 250 kVp (kilovolt peak) x-rays [@hainfeld2004], and the key parameters of efficacy (material, size, coating, photon energy) have since been analysed systematically [@retif2015]. In vitro, AuNP radiosensitization was shown with superficial and orthovoltage [@rahman2009; @khoshgard2014] and monoenergetic synchrotron beams [@rahman2014]; uptake and radiosensitization depend on particle size [@chithrani2010], and DNA experiments identified photon energy, size and concentration as governing parameters [@brun2009]. Reviews [@mesbahi2010; @jain2012; @haume2016; @song2017; @penninckx2020] and a clinical roadmap [@schuemann2016] summarise these results.

The physics has been studied mainly by MC simulation [@cho2005]: the tumour-selective enhancement was quantified for various photon sources [@zhang2009] and therapeutic beams [@kakade2015], and kilovoltage energies proved optimal for AuNP-enhanced arc therapy [@koger2016]. Radiosensitization also depends on nanoparticle location [@lechtman2011]; microscopically, the enhancement is highly localised [@jones2010], and Geant4 simulations characterised the spatial distribution of the secondary electrons [@leung2011]. Radio-enhancement, however, is not a mere dose increase: it comprises a physical stage (additional photon absorption; emission of photoelectrons, Auger electrons and fluorescence), a chemical stage (production of reactive oxygen species, ROS) and a biological stage (oxidative stress, cell-cycle perturbation, inhibition of DNA repair) [@kuncic2018; @her2017; @butterworth2012; @rosa2017]. With a local effect model, McMahon et al. showed that highly inhomogeneous nanoscale energy deposition near heavy-atom nanoparticles can explain a sensitizer enhancement ratio (SER) in cells exceeding the macroscopic DEF [@mcmahon2011], which nevertheless remains the baseline for separating the physical contribution from the chemical and biological ones.

Although gold dominates the literature, the optimal element has been sought through kerma-based comparisons over photon energy [@roeske2007] and physical modelling [@mcmahon2016]. In the kerma approximation the comparison reduces to the ratio $R(E)$ of the $\mu_{en}/\rho$ of the element to that of tissue, since tissue containing a mass fraction $w$ of the element has, at photon energy $E$, a DEF of $1 + w\,[R(E) - 1]$ (Section 3.3.1). {{REF:fig03_ratio}} shows $R(E)$ for Ag, I, Gd, Pt, Au and Bi at 5–45 keV, covering practically the whole spectrum of the 45 kV tube.

Below about 12 keV the ratios for silver, platinum, gold and bismuth are similar and that for iodine is somewhat higher. Above its L-edges (7.2–8.4 keV) and up to about 13 keV gadolinium leads, absorbing the Cu Kα lines at about 8.0 keV particularly efficiently. The L-edges of platinum, gold and bismuth at about 12–16 keV raise their ratios several-fold, leaving silver the lowest of the six between about 12 and 25.5 keV. Above its K-edge silver first equals and then exceeds gadolinium (K-edge 50.239 keV, above the maximum photon energy) and exceeds iodine up to about 42 keV, above the iodine K-edge (33.169 keV) only slightly. At 40–45 keV iodine, platinum and silver become comparable, while gold and bismuth remain the highest.

Two conclusions follow. First, per unit mass silver should be a weaker physical sensitizer than platinum, gold and bismuth, and it surpasses iodine only above its K-edge and gadolinium only above its K-edge and below the L3 edge of gadolinium (7.2 keV), so its ranking depends on the spectrum. Second, as its ratio to tissue rises from 21–32 between 8 keV and the edge (which includes the Cu K lines at 8.0–8.9 keV) to 76–139 above it, the tube design parameters and especially the filtration, which redistribute the energy fluence relative to 25.514 keV, are expected to be decisive for the DEF, as tested in Chapter 3.
