# ANNOTATION

::: {custom-style="Annotation"}
The effectiveness of anticancer radiotherapy is still limited by the tolerance of healthy tissues, and one strategy to optimise the dose delivered in external radiotherapy is the use of radiosensitizers. This work studies the radio-enhancement effect of silver (Ag) in biological matter irradiated with kilovoltage x-rays and quantifies it by the dose enhancement factor (DEF). The spectrum of a 45 kV copper-anode x-ray tube needed for the DEF calculations is modelled as a function of the tube design, technical parameters and filters.

The literature on radiosensitization by high-Z elements, the interaction of kilovoltage photons with biological matter and x-ray spectrum models is reviewed. A semi-analytical thick-target model of the copper-anode tube spectrum is formulated, together with methods of calculating the air kerma, beam quality and DEF. The photon interaction cross-sections of silver, the mass attenuation coefficients of soft tissue, brain, bone, water and silver, an x-ray irradiation facility based on the REIS-I generator and the radiation-safety requirements are described. The spectrum of a 45 kVp tube with a 100 µm copper transmission anode was calculated with the Kramers formulas without a filter and with 30 µm and 2.11 mm aluminium filters. The self-filtration of the anode suppresses the photons between the Cu K-edge (8.98 keV) and about 14 keV, and the 2.11 mm filter removes the Cu K lines and concentrates the spectrum above the Ag K-edge (25.514 keV). The DEF of soft tissue grows linearly with the mass fraction of the element; for 1 % Ag it increases from 1.40 with the thin filter to 1.98 with the thick one. With thin filtration samarium (Sm), whose L-edges lie just below the Cu K lines, is more effective than silver, whereas with thick filtration silver is more effective, because its K-edge, unlike that of samarium (46.83 keV), lies inside the spectrum. The results support the choice of filtration and exposure conditions for experiments with Ag-containing radiosensitizers.

**Keywords:** x-ray tube spectrum; copper anode; bremsstrahlung; characteristic radiation; filtration; silver; samarium; radiosensitization; dose enhancement factor; kilovoltage x-rays.

# INTRODUCTION

**Relevance of the topic.** Cancer is one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide [1], and radiotherapy is one of its main treatment methods: about half of all cancer patients have an indication for it [2]. The effectiveness of radiotherapy is limited by the tolerance of the healthy tissues surrounding the tumour [3, 4]. One of the strategies to optimise the dose delivered in external radiotherapy is therefore the use of radiosensitizers, agents that increase the effect of radiation on the tumour more than on normal tissue [5]. Among them, compounds and nanoparticles of heavy elements are now studied intensively [6, 7].

The physical basis of radiosensitization by heavy elements is the photoelectric effect. Its cross-section per unit mass increases approximately as $Z^4$ and decreases approximately as $E^{-3}$, so at kilovoltage photon energies elements of high atomic number $Z$ absorb radiation tens to hundreds of times more strongly than the light elements of soft tissue [8]. A tumour containing such an element therefore receives a higher dose than the surrounding tissue. Combined with 250 kVp (kilovolt peak) x-rays, gold nanoparticles markedly prolonged the survival of tumour-bearing mice [9], and Monte Carlo (MC) calculations showed that the dose enhancement by gold is much larger for kilovoltage than for megavoltage beams [10]. Inexpensive and widely available kilovoltage x-ray tubes are thus natural photon sources for photon activation therapy with heavy elements, as calculations for gadolinium compounds show [11].

Silver is a promising alternative to gold. Silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) were proposed as a radiosensitizer for glioma [12] and outperformed gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) in vitro and in mice [13]; silver is also cheaper than gold. Most importantly for the choice of the beam, the K-edge of silver at 25.514 keV lies inside the spectrum of any x-ray tube operated above about 26 kV, whereas the K-edge of gold (80.725 keV) requires much harder beams. Above its K-edge silver absorbs energy per unit mass 76–139 times more strongly than soft tissue, against only 21–32 times between 8 keV and the edge (Chapter 1). Comparison with samarium ($Z$ = 62), whose K-edge (46.83 keV) lies just above the maximum photon energy of a 45 kV tube, shows how the position of the absorption edges determines the dose enhancement [14].

The physical radio-enhancement is quantified by the dose enhancement factor (DEF), the ratio of the absorbed doses in tissue with and without the heavy element. Since the absorption ratio changes several-fold at the K-edge, the DEF depends on the fraction of the photon energy fluence above the edge and thus on the whole spectrum of the beam [10, 15]. Kilovoltage beams, however, are usually specified only by the tube voltage and the half-value layer (HVL) [16], which do not fix the spectrum around the edge, and data on the spectra of x-ray tubes are often missing. The spectrum must therefore be calculated from the design and technical parameters of the tube [17]. Most spectrum models have been developed for tungsten anodes [18, 19], whereas copper-anode tubes serve mainly x-ray diffraction [20]. The unfiltered beam of a 45 kV copper-anode tube consists mainly of the Cu K lines at 8.0–8.9 keV, far below the Ag K-edge; these photons are absorbed superficially and contribute little to the enhancement. The filtration that shifts the spectrum above the Ag K-edge, and the loss of dose rate it causes, have to be determined by modelling.

The problem is also of direct practical importance. The compact REIS-I x-ray generator with a 45 kV BS1 tube, available in the laboratory, has been adapted for experiments on photon activation therapy (Section 2.10). Planning irradiations of silver-containing biological samples with this facility requires knowledge of its spectrum, of the filtration that maximises the DEF at an acceptable exposure time and of the radiation-safety conditions for the personnel. The modelling of the spectrum of a 45 kV copper-anode x-ray tube on the basis of its design, technical parameters and filters, and the calculation of the DEF of biological matter containing silver, are therefore a relevant problem of medical physics.

**Degree of study of the problem.** Dose enhancement near high-Z media goes back to Spiers's analysis of energy absorption near irradiated bone [21]; iodine enhancement was later calculated for kilovoltage beams [22, 23] and used to treat brain metastases with a modified computed tomography scanner [24]. Gold nanoparticles have been studied in cells and by MC simulation [25–28], their mechanisms reviewed [29–31] and elements compared across photon energies [15, 32]; the enhancement around nanoparticles is highly localised [33], which can explain biological effects beyond the macroscopic enhancement [34]. Radiosensitization by AgNPs was shown in cancer cells [35], glioma [36, 37], breast cancer cells [38] and colorectal cancer [39]. AgNPs are themselves cytotoxic and genotoxic, inducing reactive oxygen species [40], so part of their effect may be chemical or biological. Silver dosimetry was studied by MC for brachytherapy [41], tissues around a loaded tumour [42] and skin [43] and in dose gels [44]; depth-dependent enhancement in orthovoltage beams was examined in an MC phantom [45].

X-ray spectrum models range from empirical [18] and semi-empirical [46, 47] to deterministic physics-based [19, 48, 49] and MC models [50, 51], mostly for tungsten anodes; SpekPy, validated against measurements [52], supports a copper anode in version 2 [17]. The analytical models go back to Kramers [53] and to the Bethe–Heitler cross-section [54], and Kramers' model was later extended to thick targets [55]. The research group calculated the dose enhancement by gadolinium compounds [11] and modelled the spectrum of a 45 kVp tube for radio-enhancement with Ag and Sm [14]. However, the dependence of the spectrum of a low-voltage copper-anode tube on its design and filtration, and of the resulting DEF of silver-containing tissue, has been studied insufficiently.

**Aim of the work.** The aim is to model the spectrum of a 45 kV x-ray tube with a copper anode on the basis of its design and technical parameters and filters, and to calculate with this spectrum the DEF of biological matter (soft tissue) containing silver.

**Tasks of the research.** To achieve this aim, the following tasks were solved:

1. to review radiosensitization by high-Z elements, the interaction of kilovoltage photons with biological matter and the methods of modelling x-ray tube spectra;
2. to develop a physical model of the spectrum of a copper-anode x-ray tube (electron slowing-down, bremsstrahlung, characteristic radiation, self-absorption and filtration) and methods of calculating the air kerma, beam quality and DEF;
3. to analyse the cross-sections of photon interaction with silver and the mass attenuation coefficients of soft tissue, brain, bone, water and silver;
4. to describe the x-ray irradiation facility based on the REIS-I generator and the radiation-safety requirements for work with a kilovoltage x-ray tube;
5. to calculate the spectra of a 45 kVp tube with a 100 µm copper transmission anode without a filter and with aluminium filters;
6. to analyse the K- and L-edge characteristics of the elements with $Z$ = 30–65 and the energy dependence of the ratios of the mass energy-absorption coefficients of Ag and Sm to that of soft tissue;
7. to calculate the DEF of soft tissue containing Ag and Sm as a function of the element mass fraction and the filtration.

**Object of the research.** The object is the x-ray radiation of a 45 kV copper-anode tube and its interaction with silver-containing soft tissue.

**Subject of the research.** The subject is the formation of the spectrum, its dependence on the tube design and filtration, and the resulting DEF.

**Research methods.** The spectrum is calculated with the developed model (DM), a semi-analytical thick-target model based on the Bethe stopping power, the non-relativistic Bethe–Heitler cross-section with the Elwert factor and the Gryziński K-shell ionisation cross-section [54, 56–58], with SpekPy v2 as the reference [17], and with the Kramers formulas for a transmission anode [53] together with the Kanaya–Okayama electron range [59]. Photon interaction data are taken from the XCOM database and the x-ray mass attenuation tables of the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) [60, 61] and atomic data from xraylib [62], with the ICRU-44 soft tissue of the International Commission on Radiation Units and Measurements (ICRU) as the reference medium [63]. The DEF is calculated in the kerma approximation [64]. The calculations use Python with NumPy, SciPy and Matplotlib [65–67].

**Scientific novelty.** The novelty of the work is as follows:

1. A semi-analytical model of the spectrum of a low-voltage copper-anode x-ray tube was developed. It takes into account electron slowing-down in the anode, bremsstrahlung with the Elwert correction, direct and bremsstrahlung-induced K radiation, self-absorption in the anode and attenuation in the window, air and filters, with a single parameter fitted to SpekPy.
2. For a 45 kVp tube with a 100 µm copper transmission anode it was shown that the self-filtration of the anode suppresses the photons between the Cu K-edge and about 14 keV, and that a 2.11 mm aluminium filter removes the Cu K lines and concentrates the spectrum above the silver K-edge.
3. It was established that the DEF of soft tissue containing silver increases linearly with its mass fraction and that thick aluminium filtration raises the DEF for 1 % Ag from 1.40 to 1.98. With thin filtration samarium is more effective than silver, owing to its L-edges just below the Cu K lines, whereas with thick filtration silver is more effective, because its K-edge, unlike that of samarium, lies inside the spectrum.

**Practical significance.** The results can be used to choose the filtration and exposure conditions for radiobiological experiments with silver-containing radiosensitizers on the REIS-I facility and other kilovoltage x-ray tubes. The model allows the spectrum, air kerma and beam quality of low-voltage copper-anode tubes to be calculated from their design and technical parameters when measured spectra are not available. The analysis of the absorption edges shows how the radiosensitizer and the filter should be selected jointly: for silver, filtration that removes the photons below its K-edge (about 2 mm of aluminium) should be used. The radiation-safety analysis gives the measures required for work with the facility.

**Reliability of the results.** Reliability is ensured by the use of evaluated data: the NIST photon cross-sections and attenuation and energy-absorption coefficients [60, 61], the stopping-power parameters of ICRU Report 37 [68] and the xraylib atomic data [62]. The Bethe stopping power reproduces the NIST ESTAR data for water [69], and the spectrum of the DM reproduces the bremsstrahlung shape of SpekPy v2 at 45 kV with a root-mean-square deviation of 2.46 % [17]. The pair-production cross-section of silver with complete screening agrees with the XCOM data within 4 %, and the calculated DEF values are consistent with the energy dependence of the mass energy-absorption coefficients.

# CHAPTER 1. RADIOSENSITIZATION BY HIGH-Z ELEMENTS AND X‑RAY SPECTRUM MODELING: LITERATURE REVIEW

## 1.1. Radiotherapy and the problem of normal-tissue tolerance

Against the background of the global cancer burden [1], radiotherapy is one of the principal treatment modalities [4], with an evidence-based optimal utilisation rate of approximately 52 % [2]. Radiotherapy damages cellular deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) directly through secondary electrons or indirectly through the free radicals of water radiolysis [3], but energy deposition is not selective, so healthy tissues in the beam are damaged as well. When the sigmoid dose–response curves for tumour control and normal-tissue complications lie close together (a small therapeutic ratio), normal-tissue toxicity becomes the main factor limiting radiotherapy [3, 4]. Hypoxic tumour cells, less sensitive to x-rays because of the oxygen effect, aggravate the problem [3].

Fractionation [3] and conformal, image-guided techniques such as intensity-modulated radiotherapy [70] spare normal tissue but do not change the ratio of the energy absorbed per unit mass in tumour and normal cells in the same beam region. Radiosensitizers, which act more on tumour than on normal cells, include oxygen mimetics such as the nitroimidazoles [5] and transition-metal compounds (platinum, gold, ruthenium) combining chemical and biological activity with heavy atoms [6]. Elements of high atomic number $Z$ (high-Z elements) act physically: concentrated in the tumour, they absorb more photon energy per unit mass than soft tissue, raising the tumour dose but not that of element-free surrounding tissue [7, 9]. The gain is expressed by the dose enhancement factor (DEF), the ratio of the doses in tissue with and without the agent (Section 2.6), which, unlike chemical sensitization, can be calculated from interaction data once the radiation field is known.

Monte Carlo (MC) calculations for gold nanoparticles showed a much larger macroscopic dose enhancement with kilovoltage than with megavoltage beams [10], because the strongly Z-dependent photoelectric effect dominates only at low energies (Section 1.2). Kilovoltage beams are used in superficial and orthovoltage therapy [70], and their dosimetry protocols specify the beam quality by the half-value layer (HVL) [16, 71]. The DEF therefore cannot be stated without the x-ray spectrum, which depends on the tube design and filtration; this work addresses the problem for silver and a 45 kV copper-anode tube.

## 1.2. Interaction of kilovoltage photons with biological matter

At energies up to 50 keV, photons interact through photoelectric absorption, incoherent (Compton) and coherent (Rayleigh) scattering; pair production (threshold $2m_ec^2 = 1.022$ MeV) is impossible [8, 64]. The mass attenuation coefficient is the sum of the partial coefficients [64, 72]:

$$\frac{\mu}{\rho} = \frac{\tau}{\rho} + \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{coh}}}{\rho} + \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{inc}}}{\rho}$$

EQNUM[1.1]


where $\mu$ is the linear attenuation coefficient, $\rho$ the density, and $\tau/\rho$, $\sigma_{\mathrm{coh}}/\rho$ and $\sigma_{\mathrm{inc}}/\rho$ the mass coefficients (cm²/g) of photoelectric absorption, coherent and incoherent scattering. Mixture coefficients are mass-fraction-weighted sums of elemental ones [64], taken from the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) tables [60, 61]. In photoabsorption a bound electron, preferentially of the innermost accessible shell, is ejected with the photon energy $E$ minus its binding energy; between absorption edges [8]

$$\frac{\tau}{\rho} \propto \frac{Z^{3}}{E^{3}}$$

EQNUM[1.2]


where $Z$ is the atomic number of the absorber; the atomic cross-section scales as $Z^{4}/E^{3}$ [8, 64]. Per unit mass, a heavy element thus absorbs far more strongly than H, C, N and O, which make up almost all the mass of soft tissue [63]. Below its K-edge, however, the K-shell electrons cannot take part, and the advantage is much smaller than the atomic numbers suggest.

Absorption edges are abrupt increases of $\tau/\rho$ at the shell binding energies [8]; for silver the K-edge lies at 25.514 keV and the L-subshell edges between 3.35 and 3.81 keV. The vacancy is filled from an outer shell, with emission of a fluorescence x-ray or an Auger electron [73, 74]. The K-shell fluorescence yield $\omega_K$ rises steeply with $Z$ and equals 0.831 for silver (Table 2.2) [62, 74]. In H, C, N and O (K-shell binding energies below 1 keV) practically all the absorbed energy stays local, whereas silver re-emits much of it as Kα photons of about 22 keV with a mean free path of about 1.5 cm in soft tissue; its low-energy Auger electrons deposit their energy close to the atom (Section 1.3).

In Compton scattering at these energies the photon retains most of its energy [8, 64], and electron binding, described by the incoherent scattering function $S(x,Z)$, reduces the Klein–Nishina cross-section at low energies and small angles; Rayleigh scattering, described by the atomic form factor $F(x,Z)$ ($x$ is the momentum-transfer variable), only deflects the photon [75]. In soft tissue incoherent scattering overtakes photoabsorption in the upper part of the range, whereas in silver photoabsorption dominates throughout [8, 60]. The mass energy-absorption coefficient, tabulated by NIST from Seltzer's calculations [61, 76], counts only the energy transferred to charged particles and not re-emitted as bremsstrahlung [64]:

$$\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho} = \left[\frac{\tau}{\rho}\left(1-\frac{X}{E}\right) + \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{inc}}}{\rho}\,\frac{\bar{T}_{\mathrm{inc}}}{E}\right]\left(1-\bar{g}\right)$$

EQNUM[1.3]


where $X$ is the mean fluorescence energy emitted per photoabsorption, $\bar{T}_{\mathrm{inc}}$ the mean kinetic energy of the Compton recoil electron and $\bar{g}$ the mean fraction of the secondary-electron kinetic energy converted into bremsstrahlung, negligible here; other symbols are as in (1.1) [64].

According to the International Commission on Radiation Units and Measurements (ICRU), kerma $K$ is the total initial kinetic energy of the charged particles liberated by photons per unit mass, the collision kerma $K_{\mathrm{col}}$ its part not converted into bremsstrahlung, and the absorbed dose $D$ the mean energy imparted per unit mass [64, 77]. Under charged-particle equilibrium (CPE), when the charged-particle energy entering a small volume equals that leaving it, the absorbed dose equals the collision kerma [64, 72]:

$$D = K_{\mathrm{col}} = \Psi\,\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}$$

EQNUM[1.4]


where $\Psi = \Phi E$ is the energy fluence of monoenergetic photons of energy $E$ and fluence $\Phi$; for a polyenergetic beam the expression is integrated over the spectrum (Section 2.5). Secondary electrons from photons below 50 keV have sub-millimetre ranges in tissue, so CPE holds except close to boundaries, and by the Fano theorem it is not disturbed by density variations in a medium of uniform composition [78]. Two media in the same spectrum therefore receive doses in the ratio of their spectrum-averaged $\mu_{en}/\rho$, which underlies the macroscopic, kerma-based DEF (Section 2.6); the relation fails only within the electron ranges from a high-Z inclusion.

Fig. 1.1 compares the NIST coefficients [61] of silver and ICRU-44 soft tissue [63]. Above the L-edges both coefficients of silver exceed those of tissue by more than an order of magnitude, the ratio of the $\mu_{en}/\rho$ values rising from 21.1 at 8 keV to 31.9 at 25 keV. At the K-edge $\mu/\rho$ of silver jumps 5.8-fold (from 9.53 to 55.4 cm²/g) but $\mu_{en}/\rho$ only 2.4-fold (from 8.53 to 20.3 cm²/g). The reason is K-shell fluorescence: after most K-shell photoabsorptions the K x-ray energy escapes the interaction site and is excluded from $\mu_{en}/\rho$.

For tissue the two coefficients diverge above about 10 keV, as scattering, which deposits little energy, gains weight. Above the K-edge, $\mu_{en}/\rho$ of silver falls more slowly than its $\mu/\rho$, since the fixed fluorescence energy becomes a smaller fraction of the photon energy, and more slowly than that of tissue. After the jump at the edge from about 32 to about 76, the silver-to-tissue ratio therefore keeps growing, to 102.8 at 30 keV, 120.1 at 35 keV and a maximum of about 139 at 40–45 keV. The fraction of the energy fluence above the Ag K-edge is thus the key beam characteristic for silver.

## 1.3. High-Z radiosensitizers: from iodine contrast to metal nanoparticles

Spiers first analysed how increased energy absorption in bone and secondary-electron ranges determine the soft-tissue dose in and near bone [21], and Das and Chopra quantified the backscatter dose perturbation at tissue–high-Z interfaces in kilovoltage beams [79]. Iodinated contrast media, the first agents studied systematically, sensitized cultured mammalian cells to x-rays but not to gamma rays [80], implicating photoabsorption by iodine. Dose enhancement in iodine-loaded tumours was calculated for kilovoltage x-rays [22] and brain-tumour therapy [23], and a therapy-modified computed tomography scanner delivered the first such treatment of human brain metastases [24]. Monochromatic synchrotron beams treated rats bearing iodine-loaded F98 gliomas [81] and identified the optimal energy for iodinated compounds in cells [82]; contrast-enhanced kilovoltage radiotherapy was analysed by MC [83], and modified megavoltage beams were combined with iodine and gadolinium contrast [84]. Platinum drugs add a high-Z atom to a chemotherapeutic agent: cisplatin combined with monochromatic synchrotron x-rays exploiting platinum K-edge photoactivation cured rats bearing radioresistant F98 gliomas [85]. Pignol et al. analysed inner-shell ionisation and Auger cascades in radiosensitization by halogenated deoxyuridines, platinum salts and gadolinium porphyrins [86]; when the heavy atom lies in or near DNA, short-range Auger electrons deposit their energy at this critical target, so the subcellular location of the high-Z atom matters, not only its concentration.

The modern stage began when gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) 1.9 nm in diameter markedly improved the one-year survival of tumour-bearing mice irradiated with 250 kVp (kilovolt peak) x-rays [9], and the key parameters of efficacy (material, size, coating, photon energy) have since been analysed systematically [87]. In vitro, AuNP radiosensitization was shown with superficial and orthovoltage [26, 88] and monoenergetic synchrotron beams [89]; uptake and radiosensitization depend on particle size [25], and DNA experiments identified photon energy, size and concentration as governing parameters [90]. Reviews [91–95] and a clinical roadmap [31] summarise these results.

The physics has been studied mainly by MC simulation [10]: the tumour-selective enhancement was quantified for various photon sources [27] and therapeutic beams [96], and kilovoltage energies proved optimal for AuNP-enhanced arc therapy [97]. Radiosensitization also depends on nanoparticle location [98]; microscopically, the enhancement is highly localised [33], and Geant4 simulations characterised the spatial distribution of the secondary electrons [28]. Radio-enhancement, however, is not a mere dose increase: it comprises a physical stage (additional photon absorption; emission of photoelectrons, Auger electrons and fluorescence), a chemical stage (production of reactive oxygen species) and a biological stage (oxidative stress, cell-cycle perturbation, inhibition of DNA repair) [7, 29, 30, 99]. With a local effect model, McMahon et al. showed that highly inhomogeneous nanoscale energy deposition near heavy-atom nanoparticles can explain a sensitizer enhancement ratio in cells exceeding the macroscopic DEF [34], which nevertheless remains the baseline for separating the physical contribution from the chemical and biological ones.

Although gold dominates the literature, the optimal element has been sought through kerma-based comparisons over photon energy [15] and physical modelling [32]. In the kerma approximation the comparison reduces to the ratio $R(E)$ of the $\mu_{en}/\rho$ of the element to that of tissue, since tissue containing a mass fraction $w$ of the element has, at photon energy $E$, a DEF of $1 + w\,[R(E) - 1]$ (Section 2.6). Fig. 1.2 shows $R(E)$ for Ag, I, Gd, Pt, Au and Bi at 5–45 keV, covering practically the whole spectrum of the 45 kV tube.



![Fig. 1.2. Ratio of the mass energy-absorption coefficients of Ag, I, Gd, Pt, Au and Bi to that of ICRU-44 soft tissue at 5–45 keV (NIST data). The vertical dashed line marks the Ag K-edge at 25.51 keV.](/home/user/atlant-impossible/dissertation/v2/figures/en/fig03_ratio.png){width=13.5cm}



Below about 12 keV the ratios for silver, platinum, gold and bismuth are similar and that for iodine is somewhat higher. Above its L-edges (7.2–8.4 keV) and up to about 13 keV gadolinium leads, absorbing the Cu Kα lines at about 8.0 keV particularly efficiently. The L-edges of platinum, gold and bismuth at about 12–16 keV raise their ratios several-fold, leaving silver the lowest of the six between about 12 and 25.5 keV. Above its K-edge silver first equals and then exceeds gadolinium (K-edge 50.239 keV, above the maximum photon energy) and exceeds iodine up to about 42 keV, above the iodine K-edge (33.169 keV) only slightly. At 40–45 keV iodine, platinum and silver become comparable, while gold and bismuth remain the highest.

Two conclusions follow. First, per unit mass silver should be a weaker physical sensitizer than platinum, gold and bismuth, and it surpasses iodine only above its K-edge and gadolinium only above its K-edge and below the L3 edge of gadolinium (7.2 keV), so its ranking depends on the spectrum. Second, as its ratio to tissue rises from 21–32 between 8 keV and the edge (which includes the Cu K lines at 8.0–8.9 keV) to 76–139 above it, the tube design parameters and especially the filtration, which redistribute the energy fluence relative to 25.514 keV, are expected to be decisive for the DEF, as tested in Chapter 3.


## 1.4. Silver and silver nanoparticles as radiosensitizers

Silver ($Z = 47$) lies between the light elements of soft tissue and the heavy elements, such as gold ($Z = 79$), that dominate the literature on high-Z radiosensitizers. Reviews cover the synthesis, properties and therapeutic applications of silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) [100] and their antimicrobial and anticancer uses [101]; AgNPs are cheaper than gold and can be made by green synthesis, for example in egg white [102]. For radiotherapy, two properties of silver matter: its biological activity and the position of its K-edge within kilovoltage tube spectra. The former is linked to toxicity: AgNPs are cytotoxic and genotoxic in human cells, inducing reactive oxygen species, DNA damage and cell-cycle arrest with mitochondrial involvement [40]. Such effects belong to the chemical and biological stages of radio-enhancement (Section 1.3) [7, 99], so AgNPs may sensitize cells beyond their physical dose enhancement. Their cytotoxicity, however, makes selective tumour accumulation a prerequisite of therapy, and the effect of dose enhancement on healthy tissues must be assessed [42].

Experimental evidence is summarised in Table 1.1: since the early report by Xu et al. [35], glioma has been the most studied model [12, 13, 36, 37], including radioresistant hypoxic cells [3]; breast [38] and colorectal [39] cancers have also been used. The studies differ in particles, coatings, biological models and irradiation conditions and are not quantitatively comparable. Because the physical dose enhancement depends strongly on the spectrum (Section 1.3), an enhancement ratio reported without the spectrum cannot be split into physical and non-physical parts.

The reported advantage of AgNPs over AuNPs [13] is instructive. Per unit mass, silver and gold absorb photon energy comparably below about 12 keV, but gold absorbs more strongly between about 12 and 45 keV (Fig. 1.2), and in megavoltage beams the macroscopic enhancement is much smaller even for gold [10] because Compton scattering, whose mass coefficient depends only weakly on $Z$, dominates [8]. A larger macroscopic physical dose enhancement is thus unlikely to explain the advantage of silver, either below 45 keV or at megavoltage energies. This points to substantial contributions from the chemical and biological activity of silver [40] and from nanoscale energy deposition [34]; confirming this requires quantifying the physical part for a well-defined beam.

Dosimetric studies of silver are fewer than biological ones: MC simulations addressed AgNPs in a brain tumour during brachytherapy [41], surrounding healthy tissues [42] and silver versus gold in layered skin [43]; dose enhancement was also measured in AgNP-enriched dose gels [44], and its depth dependence in orthovoltage beams was studied for nanoparticles [45]. A systematic analysis of how kilovoltage tube design and filtration determine the DEF of silver-loaded tissue has not been found in the literature reviewed.

The choice of beam follows from the atomic data of silver (Table 2.2): K-edge 25.514 keV, L3 edge 3.351 keV and Kα1 line 22.163 keV. With $\omega_K = 0.831$, only about 17 % of K vacancies relax by Auger transitions that deposit their energy locally [73, 74]; the other 83 % emit mainly Kα photons that carry their energy away (Section 1.2). Nevertheless, the silver-to-tissue ratio of $\mu_{en}/\rho$ is 76–139 above the edge, against 21–32 between 8 keV and the edge (Sections 1.2 and 1.3). Unlike the gold K-edge (80.725 keV), usable only above about 81 kV, the silver K-edge lies inside the spectrum of any tube operated above about 26 kV. The most favourable spectra therefore carry much of their energy fluence between the Ag K-edge and the end-point energy (45 keV here), far above the Cu K lines at 8.0–8.9 keV that dominate the photon fluence of the unfiltered copper-anode beam.

## 1.5. Methods of modelling x-ray tube spectra

Measuring the spectrum needed for the DEF with semiconductor detectors [46] requires detector-response corrections and is impractical for every configuration, so parametric studies use calculated spectra; four groups of methods are compared in Table 1.2. The empirical model TASMIP (tungsten anode spectral model using interpolating polynomials) interpolates measured tungsten-anode spectra from 30 to 140 kV [18], and the spektr 3.0 tool implements a similar interpolation model [103]. Such models are tied to the anode material, angle and inherent filtration of the measured tubes: added filtration can be modelled as attenuation, but the anode material and angle, studied in this work, cannot.

Semi-empirical models go back to Kramers' theory, in which the energy emitted from a thick target per unit photon-energy interval is proportional to $Z(T_0 - E)$, where $Z$ is the target atomic number, $T_0$ the initial electron kinetic energy and $E$ the photon energy [8, 53]. Birch and Marshall compared their model with Ge(Li) detector measurements [46], and Tucker, Barnes and Chakraborty proposed a similar model for tungsten [47]. Such models combine a simplified bremsstrahlung cross-section with approximate electron penetration and target self-absorption; they show the dependence on voltage, anode angle and filtration explicitly, but their fitted parameters are target-specific, usually for tungsten.

Physics-based deterministic models replace most empirical parameters with theoretical or MC-derived data. MC-derived electron-penetration characteristics [48] were used to calculate x-ray production and filtration in the target [49] and implemented for tungsten in SpekCalc [19]. SpekPy v2, its successor, offers several physics models, including the casim model used here, and several anodes, including copper [17], available for 10–50 kV in version 2.5.4 [104]. It builds on models of K and L emission [105] and of bremsstrahlung energy and angular distributions [55], validated from 20 to 300 kV [106] and against measured spectra and HVLs [52].

MC simulation of coupled electron–photon transport in the tube gives the most complete description, for example MCNP4C for diagnostic and mammography spectra [51] or EGS4/BEAM for kilovoltage radiotherapy units [50]; other general-purpose codes are EGSnrc [107], Geant4 [108] and PENELOPE [109]. It includes electron backscattering, the bremsstrahlung angular distribution and the real tube geometry, but is slow and needs detailed geometric data, so it suits benchmarks rather than extensive parametric studies.

Table: Table 1.2. Comparison of x-ray spectrum modelling approaches.

| Approach | Examples | Advantages | Limitations |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Empirical | TASMIP [18]; spektr 3.0 [103] | Fast; accurate within the data range | Fixed anode material, angle and filtration |
| Semi-empirical | Kramers [53]; Birch–Marshall [46]; Tucker et al. [47] | Simple; explicit parameter dependence | Simplified physics; fitted parameters, mostly tungsten |
| Physics-based deterministic | SpekCalc [19]; SpekPy v2 [17]; developed model (this work) | Fast; physically founded; few parameters | Approximate electron transport; limited anodes and voltages |
| Monte Carlo | MCNP4C [51]; EGS4/BEAM, EGSnrc [50, 107]; Geant4 [108]; PENELOPE [109] | Most complete physics; real tube geometry | Slow; detailed geometry needed; statistical noise |

In kilovoltage dosimetry, beam quality is specified by the HVL together with the tube voltage [110], as in the protocol of the American Association of Physicists in Medicine for 40–300 kV beams [16], the code of practice TRS-398 of the International Atomic Energy Agency [71] and the standard radiation qualities of the International Electrotechnical Commission [111]. The HVL is thus the natural quantity for comparing models and measurements. It does not, however, fix the energy-fluence fraction above the Ag K-edge, so beams with similar HVLs can give different DEFs for silver.

Most of these models were developed for tungsten, the usual target of medical tubes [112]. Copper-anode tubes serve mainly x-ray diffraction, where Cu Kα is the working line, Kβ is suppressed by a nickel filter [20] and the bremsstrahlung continuum is unwanted background. For silver dose enhancement, by contrast, the continuum is the useful part, since only bremsstrahlung photons can exceed the Ag K-edge; yet the full spectrum of copper-anode tubes and its dependence on tube parameters have received much less attention. This work therefore constructed a semi-analytical physics-based model of the copper-anode tube, the developed model (DM), combining the Bethe collision stopping power in the continuous slowing-down approximation (CSDA) [56, 68], the non-relativistic Bethe–Heitler bremsstrahlung cross-section with the Elwert factor [54, 57, 113] and the Gryziński K-shell ionisation cross-section [58], with one adjustable parameter, the electron depth factor (Chapter 2). All dependences on tube parameters remain explicit.

## 1.6. Conclusions to Chapter 1

The physical dose enhancement by a high-Z element in kilovoltage beams is governed by its mass energy-absorption coefficient relative to that of tissue, which for silver rises sharply above the K-edge at 25.514 keV; the physical efficacy of silver is thus set mainly by the fraction of the energy fluence above this edge, and a DEF is meaningful only together with its spectrum. AgNPs radiosensitize glioma, breast and colorectal cancer models, in one study more effectively than AuNPs, and are themselves cytotoxic and generate reactive oxygen species. However, the macroscopic physical contribution of silver for a well-characterised kilovoltage beam, needed to separate physical from chemical and biological effects, has not been determined systematically.

Spectrum models have been developed mainly for tungsten anodes. This work therefore develops a semi-analytical model of the 45 kV copper-anode tube spectrum, analyses the photon cross-sections of silver and the attenuation coefficients of soft tissue, brain, bone and water, describes an x-ray irradiation facility and its radiation safety, and calculates the spectrum of a 45 kVp tube with aluminium filters and the DEF of soft tissue containing silver (and, for comparison, samarium) as a function of the element concentration and filtration.

## 2.1. Design and technical parameters of an x-ray tube with a copper anode

Electrons from the heated cathode are focused onto the anode focal spot, which they reach with the kinetic energy $T_0 = eU$, where $U$ is the tube voltage and $e$ the elementary charge (45 keV in the baseline); only a small fraction of this energy is emitted as x-rays [8, 112]. The photon number is proportional to the tube current–time product (mAs), while the spectral shape is independent of the current, so all spectra are normalised to 1 mAs. In the modelled reflection-target tube (Fig. 2.1), the beam leaves through the electron-irradiated face of the thick anode. The anode angle $\theta$ between the anode surface and the central ray, which is perpendicular to the electron beam, is thus both the angle of electron incidence to the surface normal and the photon take-off angle. A small $\theta$ reduces the effective focal spot but makes the beam on the anode side weaker and harder (heel effect) [112]; the DM treats the central ray only. The beryllium window and anode self-absorption form the inherent filtration; added metal filters remove the soft part of the spectrum and harden the beam [70] before the photons cross the air gap to the sample at the focus–sample distance $r$.

## 2.2. Electron slowing-down in the anode

Neglecting radiative losses, the DM uses the continuous-slowing-down approximation (CSDA) with the Bethe mass collision stopping power [56] in the form of Report 37 of the International Commission on Radiation Units and Measurements (ICRU) [68]:

$$\frac{S(T)}{\rho} = \frac{\kappa}{2}\,\frac{Z}{A}\,\frac{1}{\beta^{2}}\left[\ln\frac{\tau^{2}(\tau + 2)}{2\,(I/m_ec^{2})^{2}} + F^{-}(\tau)\right]$$

EQNUM[2.1]


where $S(T)$ is the linear collision stopping power at the electron kinetic energy $T$, $\rho$ the density of the anode, $\kappa = 4\pi N_A r_e^{2} m_ec^{2} = 0.307075$ MeV cm²/mol, $N_A$ the Avogadro constant, $r_e$ the classical electron radius, $m_ec^{2}$ = 510.999 keV the electron rest energy, $Z$ and $A$ the atomic number and molar mass (g/mol) of the anode, $\tau = T/m_ec^{2}$, $\beta$ the electron velocity in units of the speed of light, $\beta^{2} = 1 - (1 + \tau)^{-2}$, $I$ the mean excitation energy, and $F^{-}(\tau)$ the electron term derived from the Møller cross-section:

$$F^{-}(\tau) = 1 - \beta^{2} + \frac{\tau^{2}/8 - (2\tau + 1)\ln 2}{(\tau + 1)^{2}}$$

EQNUM[2.2]


For copper $I$ = 322 eV; the density-effect correction (negligible below 100 keV) and the shell corrections (relevant only at the lowest energies) are omitted; for water the code reproduces the ESTAR stopping power of the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) [69]. The path length travelled from $T_0$ to $T$ is

$$s(T) = \int_{T}^{T_0} \frac{\mathrm{d}T'}{S(T')}$$

EQNUM[2.3]


where $T'$ is the integration variable. Evaluated by the trapezoidal rule (step 0.02 keV) down to $T_{\min}$ = 1 keV, it gives practically the CSDA range, 6.48 µm for 45 keV electrons in copper. A constant logarithm in (2.1) and $\beta^{2} \approx 2T/m_ec^{2}$ give $S \propto 1/T$, and (2.3) then yields $T_0^{2} - T^{2} \propto s$, the empirical Thomson–Whiddington law [114]. Since multiple elastic scattering makes the electrons diffuse, the CSDA path is not the photon production depth; instead of the MC-derived penetration distributions of Poludniowski and Evans [48], the DM uses one mean depth for each electron energy, the projected depth

$$d(T) = \eta\, s(T)\cos\theta$$

EQNUM[2.4]


where $d(T)$ is the depth (along the normal) at which the electron has slowed down to $T$, $\eta$ is the electron depth factor ($\eta = 1$ for straight-line motion, $\eta < 1$ for diffusion), and $\theta$ is the angle of incidence. This single adjustable parameter of the DM was fitted to the bremsstrahlung shape of SpekPy [17], which gave $\eta$ = 0.40 and a maximum projected depth of 2.54 µm for 45 keV electrons at $\theta$ = 12°. Backscattering (about 30 % of the electrons in copper) is not modelled, contributing to an overestimate of the absolute output. As panel (a) of Fig. 2.2 shows, the stopping power rises steeply as the electron slows down, so the depth grows rapidly as the energy falls from 45 to about 20 keV and then saturates. Photons close to $T_0$ are therefore emitted only near the surface, whereas soft photons are produced along the whole path and suffer most from self-absorption.

## 2.3. Bremsstrahlung production in a thick target

Kramers' classical theory of the continuous spectrum [53] gives the cross-section

$$\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\mathrm{Kr}}}{\mathrm{d}E} = \frac{16\pi}{3\sqrt{3}}\,\frac{\alpha Z^{2} r_e^{2}}{\beta_0^{2}\,E}$$

EQNUM[2.5]


where $\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\mathrm{Kr}}/\mathrm{d}E$ is the cross-section per atom per unit photon energy (cm²/keV) for $E < T$ (zero otherwise), $\alpha$ the fine-structure constant, and $\beta_0$ the velocity of an electron of energy $T$ in units of the speed of light. With $\beta_0^{2} \propto T$ and $S \propto 1/T$, integration over the slowing-down yields Kramers' law (Section 1.5), an energy spectrum proportional to $Z(T_0 - E)$. The DM retains it only as an option, using by default the non-relativistic, unscreened Born-approximation cross-section of Bethe and Heitler [54] as compiled by Koch and Motz [113]:

$$\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\mathrm{B}}}{\mathrm{d}E} = \frac{16}{3}\,\frac{\alpha Z^{2} r_e^{2}}{\beta_0^{2}\,E}\,\ln\frac{p_0 + p}{p_0 - p}$$

EQNUM[2.6]


where $p_0$ and $p$ are the electron momenta before and after the emission, $p_0c = \sqrt{T(T + 2m_ec^{2})}$ and $pc = \sqrt{(T - E)(T - E + 2m_ec^{2})}$. The Born approximation requires $2\pi Z\alpha/\beta_0 \ll 1$ and $2\pi Z\alpha/\beta \ll 1$, $\beta$ being the electron velocity after the emission [113], whereas for copper $2\pi Z\alpha/\beta_0$ exceeds 3 even at 45 keV. The error is partly corrected by the Elwert factor for the Coulomb distortion of the electron wave functions [57]:

$$f_E = \frac{\beta_0}{\beta}\,\frac{1 - \exp(-2\pi Z\alpha/\beta_0)}{1 - \exp(-2\pi Z\alpha/\beta)}$$

EQNUM[2.7]


The DM uses the product $\mathrm{d}\sigma/\mathrm{d}E = f_E\,\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\mathrm{B}}/\mathrm{d}E$ (the Elwert-corrected Bethe–Heitler cross-section). As $E \to T$, (2.6) vanishes while $f_E$ grows as $1/\beta$, so the corrected cross-section remains finite at the high-energy end. Unlike the tabulated cross-sections of Seltzer and Berger [115], this cross-section neglects atomic screening and electron–electron bremsstrahlung, contributing to the overestimated absolute yield of hard bremsstrahlung relative to SpekPy. The DEF, however, depends only on the spectral shape, which is reproduced at 45 kV with a root-mean-square deviation of 2.46 % after the fit of $\eta$.

In a thick target the electron passes $n_a\,\mathrm{d}T/S(T)$ atoms per unit area while losing the energy $\mathrm{d}T$, and a photon produced at the depth $d(T)$ is attenuated along the exit path $d(T)/\sin\theta$. Assuming isotropic emission, the number of bremsstrahlung photons per unit energy interval, per steradian and per incident electron along the central ray is

$$N_b(E) = \frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{E}^{T_0} \frac{n_a}{S(T)}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma}{\mathrm{d}E}(T, E)\,\exp\left[-\frac{\mu(E)\,d(T)}{\sin\theta}\right]\mathrm{d}T$$

EQNUM[2.8]


where $n_a = \rho N_A/A$ is the number of atoms per unit volume of the anode, $\mathrm{d}\sigma/\mathrm{d}E$ the Elwert-corrected Bethe–Heitler cross-section, and $\mu(E)$ the linear attenuation coefficient of the anode from the xraylib library [62]. Without the exponential and the factor $1/4\pi$, it gives the generated spectrum $N_g(E)$, emitted in all directions. The integrals are evaluated by the trapezoidal rule in photon bins of 0.05 keV; a similar formulation with MC-derived penetration data underlies SpekCalc [48, 49]. The self-absorption grows as $\theta$ decreases and is strongest for soft photons and just above the copper K-edge. Multiple scattering smooths the anisotropy of single-interaction bremsstrahlung, but the isotropic approximation, avoided by more detailed models [55], remains a limitation of the DM.

## 2.4. Characteristic radiation

An electron with an energy above the K-shell binding energy $E_K$ (8.979 keV for copper) can ionise the K shell; the vacancy is filled by emitting either a characteristic photon, with the probability $\omega_K$ (the fluorescence yield, 0.454 for copper), or an Auger electron [73, 74]. The DM includes the four strongest lines, K$\alpha_1$ (K–L~3~, 8.048 keV), K$\alpha_2$ (K–L~2~, 8.028 keV), K$\beta_1$ (K–M~3~) and K$\beta_3$ (K–M~2~), the last two at 8.905 keV, with relative intensities $p_j$ (radiative rates normalised over the four lines) of 0.583, 0.299, 0.078 and 0.040; the copper L lines (below 1 keV) are absorbed in the window. K vacancies arise directly from electron impact and indirectly from the absorption of bremsstrahlung photons above $E_K$. The K-shell ionisation cross-section is Gryziński's binary-encounter formula [58]:

$$\sigma_K(T) = \frac{\pi e^{4} n_K}{E_K^{2}}\,\frac{1}{U_K}\left(\frac{U_K - 1}{U_K + 1}\right)^{3/2}\left[1 + \frac{2}{3}\left(1 - \frac{1}{2U_K}\right)\ln\left(2.7 + \sqrt{U_K - 1}\right)\right]$$

EQNUM[2.9]


where $U_K = T/E_K$ is the overvoltage ratio ($\sigma_K = 0$ for $U_K \le 1$), $n_K = 2$ the number of K-shell electrons, and $\pi e^{4} = 6.5141\times10^{-20}$ cm² keV² in Gaussian units. The direct yield of line $j$ along the central ray, per steradian and per incident electron, is

$$N_j^{\mathrm{dir}} = \frac{\omega_K\,p_j}{4\pi}\int_{E_K}^{T_0} \frac{n_a\,\sigma_K(T)}{S(T)}\,\exp\left[-\frac{\mu_j\,d(T)}{\sin\theta}\right]\mathrm{d}T$$

EQNUM[2.10]


where $\mu_j = \mu(E_j)$ at the line energy $E_j$ and the other symbols are as in (2.8); lying below the copper K-edge, the lines are weakly absorbed in the anode and cannot themselves ionise the K shell. For the indirect fluorescence, since the electrons penetrate only a few micrometres, less than the attenuation lengths above the K-edge, the bremsstrahlung is represented by an isotropic point source on the anode surface (surface-source approximation). Then $u = \cos\vartheta$, $\vartheta$ being the angle between the photon direction and the inward normal, has the probability density 1/2 on $-1 \le u \le 1$, and a photon with $u > 0$ interacts at depths $z$ to $z + \mathrm{d}z$ with the probability $(\mu/u)\exp(-\mu z/u)\,\mathrm{d}z$, where $\mu = \mu(E)$, so the interaction-depth density per emitted photon is

$$P(z) = \frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1} \frac{\mu}{u}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mu z/u}\,\mathrm{d}u = \frac{\mu}{2}\,\mathrm{E}_1(\mu z)$$

EQNUM[2.11]


where $t = 1/u$ was substituted and $\mathrm{E}_1(x) = \int_{1}^{\infty} t^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{-xt}\,\mathrm{d}t$ is the exponential integral. A fraction $\tau_K(E)/\mu(E)$ of the interactions are K-shell photoabsorptions, $\tau_K(E)$ being the linear K-shell photoelectric coefficient of copper (from xraylib). A line-$j$ photon, emitted per vacancy with the probability $\omega_K p_j$, is attenuated along the central ray by $\exp(-b_j z)$, where $b_j = \mu_j/\sin\theta$. Per steradian and per generated bremsstrahlung photon of energy $E$, the yield of line $j$ is therefore

$$Y_j(E) = \frac{\omega_K\,p_j}{4\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty} \frac{\tau_K(E)}{\mu(E)}\,P(z)\,\mathrm{e}^{-b_j z}\,\mathrm{d}z = \frac{\omega_K\,p_j}{4\pi}\,\frac{\tau_K(E)}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty} \mathrm{E}_1(\mu z)\,\mathrm{e}^{-b_j z}\,\mathrm{d}z$$

EQNUM[2.12]


Interchanging the order of integration gives the Laplace transform $\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{E}_1(x)\,\mathrm{e}^{-sx}\,\mathrm{d}x = \int_{1}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t/[t(t + s)] = \ln(1 + s)/s$, with $x = \mu z$ and $s = b_j/\mu$:

$$\int_{0}^{\infty} \mathrm{E}_1(\mu z)\,\mathrm{e}^{-b_j z}\,\mathrm{d}z = \frac{1}{b_j}\ln\left(1 + \frac{b_j}{\mu}\right)$$

EQNUM[2.13]


Weighting $Y_j(E)$ with $N_g(E)$ and integrating above the K-edge gives the indirect yield per steradian and per incident electron:

$$N_j^{\mathrm{ind}} = \frac{\omega_K\,p_j}{4\pi}\int_{E_K}^{T_0} N_g(E)\,\frac{\tau_K(E)}{2\,b_j}\,\ln\left[1 + \frac{b_j}{\mu(E)}\right]\mathrm{d}E$$

EQNUM[2.14]


The total yield of line $j$ is $N_j = N_j^{\mathrm{dir}} + N_j^{\mathrm{ind}}$. For the baseline tube the indirect contribution is 4.5 % of the Kα and 4.8 % of the Kβ intensity; it is slightly larger for Kβ because the indirect vacancies lie deeper on average and Kβ photons are attenuated less. The surface-source approximation and the neglect of scattered photons therefore matter little for the line intensities.

## 2.5. Filtration, beam-quality parameters and air kerma

Attenuation in the window, added filters and air follows the narrow-beam Beer–Lambert law, which neglects scattered photons:

$$\mathcal{T}(E) = \exp\left[-\mu_{\mathrm{Be}}(E)\,t_{\mathrm{Be}} - \sum_{i}\mu_i(E)\,t_i - \mu_{\mathrm{air}}(E)\,r\right]$$

EQNUM[2.15]


where $\mathcal{T}(E)$ is the transmission, $\mu_{\mathrm{Be}}$, $\mu_i$ and $\mu_{\mathrm{air}}$ are the linear attenuation coefficients of beryllium, the $i$-th added filter and air, $t_{\mathrm{Be}}$ and $t_i$ the thicknesses of the window and the filters, and $r$ the focus–sample distance. For the elements, the xraylib total mass attenuation coefficients, including coherent scattering, are multiplied by the densities in Table 2.2; for dry air the mixture rule is applied to the NIST data for C, N, O and Ar [61] with the density $1.205\times10^{-3}$ g/cm³, and treating the whole distance as air slightly overestimates the attenuation. The spectral photon fluence at the sample is then

$$\Phi(E) = \frac{n_e\,Q}{r^{2}}\,\mathcal{T}(E)\left[N_b(E) + \sum_{j} N_j\,\delta(E - E_j)\right]$$

EQNUM[2.16]


where $\Phi(E)$ is the number of photons per unit area and energy interval (cm^−2^ keV^−1^), $Q$ the exposure in mAs, $n_e = 6.242\times10^{15}$ the number of electrons per mAs, $N_j$ the total yield of line $j$, and $\delta$ the Dirac delta function. Numerically, each delta function is replaced by $N_j/\Delta E$ in the bin of width $\Delta E$ = 0.05 keV that contains $E_j$. With the spectral energy fluence $\Psi(E) = E\,\Phi(E)$, the tube output is characterised by the air kerma [64, 77]:

$$K_{\mathrm{air}} = \int_{0}^{T_0} \Phi(E)\,E\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_{\mathrm{air}}\mathrm{d}E$$

EQNUM[2.17]


where $(\mu_{en}/\rho)_{\mathrm{air}}$ is the mass energy-absorption coefficient of dry air, obtained in the same way, and 1 keV/g = $1.602\times10^{-13}$ Gy. Strictly, (2.17) gives the collision kerma, which for kilovoltage photons in air practically equals the total kerma [64]. With ICRU-44 soft tissue [63] instead of air, it gives the tissue kerma at the sample surface without backscatter, used in Section 2.9 to estimate exposure times. For the SpekPy spectra, filtered with SpekPy's own attenuation data, the SpekPy air kerma is used, whereas the half-value layers (HVL) and mean energies of all copper-anode spectra are computed identically for both models.

Kilovoltage beam quality is specified primarily by the first HVL, the thickness of a reference absorber (usually aluminium or copper) that halves the narrow-beam air kerma [16, 71, 111]. In aluminium, HVL1 = $x_1$ follows from

$$\int_{0}^{T_0} \Phi(E)\,E\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_{\mathrm{air}}\exp\left[-\mu_{\mathrm{Al}}(E)\,x_1\right]\mathrm{d}E = \frac{1}{2}\,K_{\mathrm{air}}$$

EQNUM[2.18]


where $\mu_{\mathrm{Al}}(E)$ is the linear attenuation coefficient of aluminium. The thickness $x_2$ that reduces the air kerma to one quarter gives HVL2 = $x_2 - x_1$ (both found by bisection) and the homogeneity coefficient $h$ = HVL1/HVL2. The effective energy $E_{\mathrm{eff}}$, that of the monoenergetic beam with the same HVL1, is found from $\mu_{\mathrm{Al}}(E_{\mathrm{eff}}) = \ln 2/\mathrm{HVL1}$ by Brent's method, and the mean energy of the photon fluence spectrum is

$$\bar{E} = \frac{\int_{0}^{T_0} E\,\Phi(E)\,\mathrm{d}E}{\int_{0}^{T_0} \Phi(E)\,\mathrm{d}E}$$

EQNUM[2.19]


where both integrals include the characteristic lines. The fraction of photons above the Ag K-edge, $f_{\mathrm{Ag}} = \int_{E_{K,\mathrm{Ag}}}^{T_0}\Phi(E)\,\mathrm{d}E \big/ \int_{0}^{T_0}\Phi(E)\,\mathrm{d}E$ with $E_{K,\mathrm{Ag}}$ = 25.514 keV, and the corresponding energy-fluence fraction describe the part of the spectrum in which silver absorbs most strongly relative to tissue (Sections 1.2 and 1.3). Like the DEF, the HVLs and the mean energy depend only on the spectral shape, not on the absolute output.


## 2.6. Dose enhancement factor: definitions and calculation

The physical radio-enhancement is quantified by the DEF, the ratio of absorbed doses at a point with and without silver under identical irradiation:

$$\mathrm{DEF} = \frac{D_{t+\mathrm{Ag}}}{D_t}$$

EQNUM[2.20]


where $D_{t+\mathrm{Ag}}$ and $D_t$ are the absorbed doses in silver-loaded and unloaded (reference) tissue. This macroscopic DEF assumes a homogeneous silver distribution and doses averaged over volumes much larger than cells and electron ranges.

Below 50 keV secondary electrons have sub-millimetre ranges in tissue, so charged-particle equilibrium (CPE) holds except near interfaces [64, 72] and the dose equals the collision kerma (1.4), practically the kerma in low-Z media [77]. By analogy with (2.17),

$$D = \int_{0}^{T_0} \Psi(E)\,\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}(E)\,\mathrm{d}E$$

EQNUM[2.21]


where $\Psi(E) = E\,\Phi(E)$ is the spectral energy fluence, $\Phi(E)$ the spectral photon fluence (2.16), $\mu_{en}/\rho$ the mass energy-absorption coefficient of the medium and $T_0$ = 45 keV the maximum photon energy. Since $\mu_{en}/\rho$ excludes the energy of scattered and fluorescence photons [76], the Ag K x-rays ($\omega_K = 0.831$) are assumed to escape. For a silver mass fraction $w$ the mixture rule gives [61]

$$\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_{t+\mathrm{Ag}} = (1-w)\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_t + w\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_{\mathrm{Ag}}$$

EQNUM[2.22]


where $t$ and Ag denote tissue and silver; mass fractions of 0.1–2 % are considered. Substituting (2.21) and (2.22) into (2.20) gives the surface DEF:

$$\mathrm{DEF} = \frac{\int \Psi(E)\left[(1-w)\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_t + w\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_{\mathrm{Ag}}\right]\mathrm{d}E}{\int \Psi(E)\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_t\mathrm{d}E}$$

EQNUM[2.23]


The fluence normalisation cancels, so the DEF depends only on the spectral shape, and DM spectra are usable despite their less accurate absolute output. As the numerator is linear in $w$, (2.23) becomes exactly

$$\mathrm{DEF} = 1 + w\left(\langle R\rangle - 1\right)$$

EQNUM[2.24]


with

$$\langle R\rangle = \frac{\int \Psi(E)\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_{\mathrm{Ag}}\mathrm{d}E}{\int \Psi(E)\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_t\mathrm{d}E} = \frac{\int R(E)\,d_t(E)\,\mathrm{d}E}{\int d_t(E)\,\mathrm{d}E}$$

EQNUM[2.25]


where $R(E) = (\mu_{en}/\rho)_{\mathrm{Ag}}/(\mu_{en}/\rho)_t$ and $d_t(E) = \Psi(E)\,(\mu_{en}/\rho)_t$ is the dose spectrum of unloaded tissue, so $\langle R\rangle$, the dose-weighted mean of $R(E)$, depends on the spectrum alone. For ICRU-44 tissue, $R(E)$ jumps at the Ag K-edge (25.514 keV) from about 32 to about 76 and reaches about 139 at 40–45 keV. Shifting the dose spectrum above the K-edge therefore raises $\langle R\rangle$ and the slope, the reason why filtration increases the DEF (Section 3.3).

Where silver is present only locally at depth $z$, without perturbing the beam, the primary-beam DEF is

$$\mathrm{DEF}(z) = \frac{\int \Psi(E)\,e^{-\mu_t(E)\,z}\left[(1-w)\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_t + w\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_{\mathrm{Ag}}\right]\mathrm{d}E}{\int \Psi(E)\,e^{-\mu_t(E)\,z}\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_t\mathrm{d}E}$$

EQNUM[2.26]


where $\Psi(E)$ is the surface energy fluence and $\mu_t(E) = \rho_t\,(\mu/\rho)_t(E)$ the linear attenuation coefficient of tissue of density $\rho_t$ = 1.06 g/cm³; scatter and inverse-square fall-off are neglected, and the denominator normalised to $z = 0$ is the relative primary dose in unloaded tissue. Tissue removes first the low-energy photons, for which $R(E)$ is small, so the DEF increases with depth. Silver filling the region above the point also attenuates the beam, most strongly just above the K-edge, where its $\mu/\rho$ increases 5.8-fold. For a slab loaded from the surface to beyond $z$,

$$\mathrm{DEF}_{\mathrm{slab}}(z) = \frac{\int \Psi(E)\,e^{-\mu_{t+\mathrm{Ag}}(E)\,z}\left[(1-w)\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_t + w\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_{\mathrm{Ag}}\right]\mathrm{d}E}{\int \Psi(E)\,e^{-\mu_t(E)\,z}\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_t\mathrm{d}E}$$

EQNUM[2.27]


where $\mu_{t+\mathrm{Ag}}(E) = \rho_t\left[(1-w)(\mu/\rho)_t + w\,(\mu/\rho)_{\mathrm{Ag}}\right]$ is the linear attenuation coefficient of the loaded medium (density approximated by $\rho_t$). The added attenuation preferentially removes the most effective photons, so $\mathrm{DEF}_{\mathrm{slab}}$ decreases with depth (self-shielding) and is no longer linear in $w$. At $w$ = 1 % silver attenuates just above its K-edge about as much as the tissue itself, and behind a loaded region the dose falls below its silver-free value.

The reference medium is ICRU-44 soft tissue (H 10.2, C 14.3, N 3.4, O 70.8, Na 0.2, P 0.3, S 0.3, Cl 0.2 and K 0.3 % by mass; 1.06 g/cm³) [63], with water and the ICRU four-component tissue as alternatives; the choice affects the DEF and must be reported. On the subcellular scale, short-range photo- and Auger electrons deposit much of their energy close to nanoparticles [33], which can produce biological effects beyond the macroscopic enhancement (local effect model) [34] depending on photon energy and particle size, concentration and location [98]. The calculated DEF is thus the physical baseline for identifying chemical, biological and nanoscale contributions.

## 2.7. Cross-sections of photon interaction with silver

When photons pass through a material, they interact with its atoms, atomic nuclei and electrons. The main interaction mechanisms are coherent scattering, the photoelectric effect, Compton scattering, electron–positron pair production and the nuclear photoeffect [119, 120], and the total cross-section of photon interaction is the sum of their contributions. For kilovoltage photons only the first three processes are possible, since pair production and the nuclear photoeffect have thresholds of the order of megaelectronvolts.

In the photoelectric effect a photon is absorbed by a bound atomic electron, which is ejected with the kinetic energy $E_\gamma - E_b$, where $E_\gamma$ is the photon energy and $E_b$ the binding energy of the electron. The process is most probable on the most tightly bound electrons whose binding energy does not exceed the photon energy, i.e. on the K-shell when $E_\gamma > E_K$. In the Born approximation the photoelectric cross-section of the K-shell (in cm²) is [119]

$$(\sigma_{\mathrm{ph}})_K = 1.09\times10^{-16}\,Z^{5}\left(\frac{13.61}{E_\gamma}\right)^{7/2}$$

EQNUM[2.28]


for $E_K \ll E_\gamma \ll m_ec^2$, with $E_\gamma$ in eV, and

$$(\sigma_{\mathrm{ph}})_K = 1.34\times10^{-33}\,\frac{Z^{5}}{E_\gamma}$$

EQNUM[2.29]


for $E_\gamma \gg m_ec^2$, with $E_\gamma$ in MeV; here $Z$ is the atomic number and $m_ec^2$ = 0.511 MeV the rest energy of the electron. The contributions of the L- and M-shells to the photoelectric effect are relatively small: the photoelectric cross-section of the L-shell is about 20 % and that of the M-shell about 5 % of the K-shell value. The total photoelectric cross-section is therefore 1.25 times the K-shell cross-section:

$$\sigma_{\mathrm{ph}} = \frac{5}{4}\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{ph}})_K$$

EQNUM[2.30]


Expressions (2.28)–(2.30) show the strong dependence of the photoeffect on the atomic number ($Z^5$ per atom, i.e. about $Z^4$ per unit mass, cf. (1.2)) and on the photon energy ($E_\gamma^{-7/2}$ for $E_\gamma \ll m_ec^2$) that underlies the radio-enhancement by high-Z elements. Near the absorption edges they are only estimates: for silver at 30 keV, (2.28) and (2.30) give 6.2×10⁻²⁰ cm², about ten times the tabulated value, so the calculations use the evaluated cross-sections of the NIST XCOM database [60].

The Compton effect is the scattering of a photon by free electrons of the material [121]. For the Compton effect to occur, the binding energy $E_e$ of the electrons must be much smaller than the photon energy, $E_e \ll E_\gamma$. There are two types of Compton process:

1. Direct Compton effect: a photon interacts with a stationary free electron, changes its direction and transfers part of its energy to the electron.
2. Inverse Compton effect: a photon is scattered by a fast-moving electron and gains energy in the process.

In the irradiation of biological matter only the direct effect is relevant. The total cross-section of Compton scattering on a free electron follows from the Klein–Nishina formula [122]; for $\varepsilon \ll 1$ it is

$$\sigma_{C} = \frac{8\pi}{3}\,\frac{e^{4}}{m_e^{2}c^{4}}\left(1 - 2\varepsilon + \frac{26}{5}\varepsilon^{2} + \ldots\right)$$

EQNUM[2.31]


and for $\varepsilon \gg 1$

$$\sigma_{C} = \pi r_e^{2}\,\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left(\frac{1}{2} + \ln 2\varepsilon\right)$$

EQNUM[2.32]


where $\varepsilon = E_\gamma/(m_ec^2)$ is the photon energy in units of the electron rest energy, $e$ the elementary charge, $r_e = e^2/(m_ec^2)$ = 2.818×10⁻¹³ cm the classical electron radius and $8\pi r_e^2/3$ = 6.65×10⁻²⁵ cm² the Thomson cross-section, the low-energy limit of (2.31). The Compton cross-section per atom is $Z$ times larger. For bound electrons at low photon energies it is smaller than this value; for silver at 30 keV the difference is about one third.

The formation of an electron–positron pair is the process in which a photon interacts with the Coulomb field of an atomic nucleus or the field of an atomic electron, resulting in the creation of an electron and a positron:

$$\gamma \rightarrow e^{-} + e^{\text{+}}$$

EQNUM[2.33]


Pair production occurs only when the photon energy exceeds a threshold. In the field of a nucleus, which takes up a negligible recoil energy, the threshold energy is

$$E_{\mathrm{thr},1} \approx 2m_ec^{2} = 1.022~\mathrm{MeV}$$

EQNUM[2.34]


and in the field of an atomic electron, which shares the momentum with the pair (triplet production), it is

$$E_{\mathrm{thr},2} \approx 4m_ec^{2} = 2.044~\mathrm{MeV}$$

EQNUM[2.35]


In the Coulomb field of a nucleus, disregarding the screening by atomic electrons, the total cross-section of pair production for photon energies $m_ec^2 \ll E_\gamma \ll m_ec^2/(\alpha Z^{1/3})$ is [119]

$$\sigma_{\mathrm{pair}} = Z^{2}\alpha r_e^{2}\left(\frac{28}{9}\ln 2\varepsilon - \frac{218}{27}\right)$$

EQNUM[2.36]


and with complete screening, for $E_\gamma \gg m_ec^2/(\alpha Z^{1/3})$,

$$\sigma_{\mathrm{pair}} = Z^{2}\alpha r_e^{2}\left(\frac{28}{9}\ln\frac{183}{Z^{1/3}} - \frac{2}{27}\right)$$

EQNUM[2.37]


where $Z$ is the atomic number of the nucleus, $\alpha \approx 1/137$ the fine-structure constant, $r_e$ the classical electron radius and $\varepsilon = E_\gamma/(m_ec^2)$. For silver $m_ec^2/(\alpha Z^{1/3})$ = 19.4 MeV, and (2.37) gives 1.55×10⁻²³ cm², close to the XCOM value in the nuclear field at very high energies (1.50×10⁻²³ cm² at 100 GeV). ## 2.8. Mass attenuation coefficients of soft tissue, brain, bone, water and silver

A narrow beam of monoenergetic photons with an incident intensity $I_0$, penetrating a layer of material with mass thickness $x$ and density $\rho$, emerges with the intensity $I$ given by the exponential attenuation law [61]:

$$I = I_0\,e^{-(\mu/\rho)\,x}$$

EQNUM[2.38]


from which $\mu/\rho$ can be obtained from measured values of $I_0$, $I$ and $x$. The mass thickness is the mass per unit area, obtained by multiplying the thickness $t$ by the density $\rho$, i.e. $x = \rho t$.

The total cross-section per atom can be written as the sum of the contributions of the principal photon interactions:

$$\sigma_{\mathrm{tot}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{pe}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{coh}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{incoh}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{pair}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{trip}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{ph.n}}$$

EQNUM[2.39]


where $\sigma_{\mathrm{pe}}$ is the atomic photoeffect cross-section, $\sigma_{\mathrm{coh}}$ and $\sigma_{\mathrm{incoh}}$ are the coherent (Rayleigh) and incoherent (Compton) scattering cross-sections, $\sigma_{\mathrm{pair}}$ and $\sigma_{\mathrm{trip}}$ the cross-sections of electron–positron pair production in the fields of the nucleus and of the atomic electrons, and $\sigma_{\mathrm{ph.n}}$ the photonuclear cross-section. The mass attenuation coefficient of an element is calculated as

$$\frac{\mu}{\rho} = \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{tot}}}{uA}$$

EQNUM[2.40]


where $u$ = 1.6605×10⁻²⁴ g is the atomic mass unit and $A$ the relative atomic mass of the element. For mixtures and compounds, assumed homogeneous, $\mu/\rho$ is obtained by simple additivity [61]:

$$\frac{\mu}{\rho} = \sum_{i} w_i\left(\frac{\mu}{\rho}\right)_i$$

EQNUM[2.41]


where $w_i$ is the fraction by weight of the $i$-th element. The mass attenuation coefficients of water, soft tissue, brain, cortical bone and silver obtained in this way for photon energies of 1–100 keV are shown in Fig. 2.4 and listed for selected energies in Table 2.4.

In the whole range the coefficients decrease steeply with energy, approximately as $E^{-3}$ where photoabsorption dominates: for soft tissue $\mu/\rho$ falls from 3.7×10³ cm²/g at 1 keV to 5.4 cm²/g at 10 keV and 0.17 cm²/g at 100 keV. Water, soft tissue and brain consist mainly of hydrogen, carbon, nitrogen and oxygen and have practically equal coefficients (within 1 % at 10–30 keV), so water is a good substitute for soft tissue. Cortical bone, which contains calcium and phosphorus, attenuates photons 5.3 times more strongly than soft tissue at 10 keV and 3.5 times at 30 keV. The difference nearly vanishes at 100 keV (a factor of 1.1), where Compton scattering, which is proportional to the electron density, prevails. The calcium K-edge at 4.04 keV is visible in the bone curve. Silver has the largest coefficient in the whole range. Its curve shows the L-edges at 3.35–3.81 keV and the K-edge at 25.514 keV, where $\mu/\rho$ jumps from 9.6 to 55 cm²/g. Just above the K-edge, silver attenuates photons per unit mass about 108 times more strongly than soft tissue; the factor is 97 at 30 keV and 51 at 45 keV, against 22 at 20 keV. The photons of the 45 kV tube spectrum above the Ag K-edge are thus absorbed preferentially by silver, which is the physical basis of the radio-enhancement considered in Chapter 3.

## 2.9. Radiation safety when working with a kilovoltage x-ray tube

A 45 kV copper-anode tube emits very intense low-energy x-rays: the unfiltered air kerma at 10 cm is 383 mGy per mAs, i.e. 15.3 Gy/s at 40 mA, and the tissue surface receives 2 Gy in 0.12 s, so accidental hand exposure causes severe local injuries within seconds. The primary beam, scatter from the sample and holder, and housing leakage must be controlled. Protection follows the principles of justification, optimisation (dose as low as reasonably achievable) and dose limitation of the International Commission on Radiological Protection [123], implemented in the Basic Safety Standards of the International Atomic Energy Agency [124] and, in Uzbekistan, in the Law "On Radiation Safety" [125]. Occupational effective and equivalent doses (skin, extremities, eye lens) must stay below the limits.

The main measures are technical: a shielded housing, an enclosure whose door interlocks switch off the high voltage or close the shutter, warning lights, and fixed holders and collimators that keep hands out of the beam. Staff training, local rules, personal dosimetry and dose-rate surveys, particularly after maintenance, complement them. The high-voltage generator is a further hazard, and the beryllium window must not be touched (beryllium compounds are toxic).

Filtration also serves safety: the filters that markedly raise the DEF (1 mm Al to 0.2 mm Cu) lower the air kerma rate 120–1300-fold; with 1 mm Al the surface tissue kerma rate at 40 mA falls from 16.5 to 0.137 Gy/s and the time for 2 Gy rises from 0.12 to 14.6 s, controllable by a timer or shutter. The removed soft component would be absorbed superficially: the relative primary dose at 10 mm is 0.009 of the surface value without a filter and 0.44 with 1 mm Al. The DEF-maximising filter, whose beam can be specified by its HVL [16], thus also reduces unnecessary skin dose, in line with the optimisation principle.

For this purpose, x-ray microfocus devices intended for scientific research, x-ray diagnostics and radiography can be used. The source of x-rays in such a device is the BS-15 x-ray tube (or its analogue), whose anode target is made of tungsten (W), molybdenum (Mo), copper (Cu) or silver (Ag). The functional capabilities and technical level of x-ray equipment are largely determined by the parameters of its radiation source, the most important of which is the radiation spectrum. However, data on the spectra of x-ray tubes are often missing and are replaced by qualitative estimates of the half-value layer of the dose rate in a standard material and of the "effective" and mean energies. This problem can be solved by calculating the radiation spectrum of the tube from its design and technical parameters.

By adding the bremsstrahlung and characteristic components, an idealised x-ray spectrum is obtained that depends on the anode current, the accelerating voltage and the target material. To determine the true spectrum of the tube, the attenuation of the radiation in the target material, the output window, the filter and the medium between the filter and the irradiated sample must be taken into account. The mechanism of x-ray generation in a tube with a transmission anode is shown in Fig. 3.1, where $x_1$ is the average penetration depth of electrons into the target, $x_2$ the thickness of the target layer in which the radiation is attenuated and $x_3$ the thickness of the output window.



![Fig. 3.1. Mechanism of x-ray generation in a tube with a transmission anode: x₁ – average penetration depth of electrons into the target; x₂ – thickness of the target layer attenuating the radiation; x₃ – thickness of the output window](/home/user/atlant-impossible/dissertation/v2/figures/en/f3_mechanism.png){width=13.5cm}



The electrons with the energy $E_0 = eU$ lose their energy in a thin surface layer of the target. The x-rays generated at depths up to $x_1$ are emitted in all directions, and those emitted in the direction of the beam cross the remaining target thickness $x_2$ and the window $x_3$. Since the electron range is much smaller than the target thickness, the target of a transmission tube acts at the same time as the first filter of the beam (Section 3.3). In general, the attenuation of the x-ray flux by a layer of matter is described by

$$I(E) = I_0(E)\,e^{-\mu(E)\,d}$$

EQNUM[3.1]


where $I_0(E)$ is the spectrum before attenuation in the layer, $\mu(E)$ the linear attenuation coefficient for photons of energy $E$ and $d$ the thickness of the layer. The experimental data on the maximum penetration depth of electrons into the target are described most accurately by the Kanaya–Okayama expression [59]:

$$R_0 = \frac{0.0276\,A\,E_0^{1.67}}{\rho\,Z^{0.889}}$$

EQNUM[3.2]


where $R_0$ is in µm, $E_0$ is the energy of the primary electrons in keV, $\rho$ the density of the material in g/cm³, $A$ its atomic weight in g/mol and $Z$ its atomic number. For copper ($A$ = 63.546 g/mol, $\rho$ = 8.96 g/cm³, $Z$ = 29) at $E_0$ = 45 keV, (3.2) gives $R_0$ = 5.7 µm, close to the CSDA range of 6.5 µm calculated from the Bethe formula (Section 2.2). The range increases with the electron energy as $E_0^{1.67}$ and decreases with the density of the target.

The thickness of the layer in which the radiation is attenuated is determined by the design of the anode. Structurally, anodes are made massive or transmission (shoot-through) (Fig. 3.2). With a massive anode (Fig. 3.2, a) the working beam of the tube can be formed within the range of angles $\varphi$ = 0–87°. Such an anode usually consists of an anode body 4 and a target 3 (composite anode). The material of the anode body must have a high thermal conductivity, since the heat is removed to the cooling device through the anode body. Most often the anode body is made of oxygen-free copper, which has a fairly high melting point (1358 K), good vacuum properties and a high heat capacity and thermal conductivity.

The target must have a high melting point and a low vapour pressure at high temperatures. In tubes intended for producing bremsstrahlung, targets are made of tungsten (melting point about 3700 K). To obtain characteristic radiation of a certain hardness, targets are made of various materials (chromium, iron, copper, molybdenum, silver, etc.). In some cases there is no separate target, and its function is performed by the surface of the anode body (homogeneous anode).

A transmission anode is used in x-ray tubes, as a rule, when the axis and direction of the working beam must coincide with those of the electron beam (Fig. 3.2, b). Transmission anodes can also be composite or homogeneous. A composite anode includes a fairly thin substrate and a target. The substrate must transmit the working beam with low attenuation; in many cases it also serves as the output window of the tube and must then be vacuum-tight. As a rule, the substrates of transmission anodes are made of beryllium.



![Fig. 3.2. Anode designs: a – massive; b – transmission (shoot-through); 1 – electron beam; 2 – x-ray radiation; 3 – target; 4 – anode body (substrate)](/home/user/atlant-impossible/dissertation/v2/figures/en/f3_anodes.png){width=13.5cm}



Thus, for a transmission anode the thickness of the attenuating layer is

$$x_2 = X - x_1$$

EQNUM[3.3]


where $X$ is the thickness of the anode and $x_1$ the depth of electron penetration into the anode. For a massive anode the thickness of the attenuating layer is

$$x_2 = x_1\,\frac{\cos\alpha}{\cos\beta}$$

EQNUM[3.4]


where $\alpha$ is the angle between the electron beam and the normal to the target surface and $\beta$ the angle between the normal and the direction in which the x-ray beam is taken off.

The two designs differ in the path of the photons in the anode material (Fig. 3.2). In a massive anode the photons leave through the irradiated surface, and their path (3.4) is of the order of the electron range, i.e. a few micrometres. Self-absorption is therefore important only for soft photons and at grazing take-off angles: as $\beta \to 90°$, $\cos\beta \to 0$ and the path grows without limit, which causes the heel effect (Section 2.1). In a transmission anode the photons must cross the whole remaining thickness (3.3), about 95 µm for a 100 µm copper target, so the target strongly filters the beam. Its thickness must exceed the electron range $R_0$, so that the electrons are stopped, but it should be as small as possible, so that the useful radiation is transmitted.

Having calculated the radiation spectrum of the x-ray tube, i.e. the number of photons in the radiation flux as a function of their energy, one obtains the total intensity (energy flux) of the radiation:

$$I = \int_{0}^{E_0} N_T(E)\,E\,\mathrm{d}E + \sum_{q} N_{X_q}\,E_{X_q}$$

EQNUM[3.5]


where $N_T(E)$ is the bremsstrahlung spectrum (the number of photons per unit energy interval), $N_{X_q}$ the number of photons of the characteristic line $q$ and $E_{X_q}$ their energy.

It should be noted that with increasing tube voltage the radiation intensity increases, and not only the quantity but also the quality of the x-rays, i.e. their energy spectrum, changes. The x-ray beam has a continuous energy spectrum with a wide range of energies, and photons of different energies pass through matter differently. Photons with energies below 10 keV are practically unable to penetrate a 1-cm layer of muscle: almost all of them are absorbed by the biological tissue. In the range 10–25 keV the penetrating ability increases rapidly with energy; the transmission of 1 cm of soft tissue is 0.3 % at 10 keV, 17 % at 15 keV, 42 % at 20 keV and 57 % at 25 keV. Low-energy photons (below 20 keV) therefore create an additional radiation load on the patient and should be removed from the spectrum.

Filters made of various materials can be used for this purpose. In a filter the x-rays are attenuated unevenly: the number of low-energy photons decreases more than the number of high-energy photons. The unevenness of attenuation depends on the material and thickness of the filter, which should be chosen so that the spectrum at high energies ($E \ge$ 50 keV) changes insignificantly. Irradiation with filters is widely used in x-ray therapy and avoids radiation burns of the skin. In deep x-ray therapy, filters of copper, tin and lead 0.5–2 mm thick are used, and in superficial x-ray therapy aluminium filters 1–4 mm thick. When an x-ray tube is used as the source of photon radiation in photon activation therapy, aluminium filters are applicable, their thickness depending on the spectrum of the tube.

The selective action of filters follows from the energy dependence of photoelectric absorption, which per unit mass is roughly proportional to $Z^4/E^3$ (Section 2.7). A filter of thickness $d$ multiplies the spectrum by $e^{-\mu(E)\,d}$, which is small for soft photons and close to unity for hard ones, so the mean energy of the beam increases (beam hardening). Aluminium ($Z$ = 13), whose K-edge (1.56 keV) lies below the useful energy range, attenuates the spectrum monotonically and does not create transmission windows at absorption edges.

## 3.2. Calculation of the x-ray tube spectrum

The energy distribution of the photons (spectrum) produced in x-ray generators, or x-ray tubes, depends on the current, the voltage and the target/filter combination, and the dose in turn depends on the spectrum [126]. Knowledge of the spectral distribution of the x-rays emitted from an x-ray tube is important for the quantitative determination of the radiation dose in medical imaging. This has motivated the development of various computational approaches for predicting the x-ray emission, including MC simulation of coupled electron–photon transport. Although the MC method is probably the most complete method available, it is impractical for specific applications because of the heavy computational load of simulating the electron transport in the x-ray target (the anode of the tube). Alternative methods that use efficient deterministic solutions for the electron penetration and the resulting x-ray production have therefore found common practical use. Kramers, and Bethe and Heitler, developed analytical x-ray emission models from the principles of classical electrodynamics and quantum mechanics, respectively. Their results were valid strictly for thin x-ray targets, since the filtration of bremsstrahlung by the overlying target layer was not considered; Kramers' model was later modified to extend its application to conventional thick-target x-ray tubes [55].

In this work, the photon spectrum of the 45 kVp x-ray tube (BS1) with a thin anode (x-ray target) is modelled. To calculate the spectrum of the x-ray tube, the Kramers formulas were used [14, 53]: the bremsstrahlung x-ray spectrum

$$\frac{\partial\Psi_X(E)}{\partial E} = k_1\,i_A\,Z\left(\frac{E_0}{E} - 1\right)$$

EQNUM[3.6]


and the characteristic x-ray spectrum

$$\Psi_{X_q} = k_2\,\frac{i_A\,G\,\gamma\,\omega_q\,p}{Z}\left(\frac{E_0}{E_q} - 1\right)^{1.67}$$

EQNUM[3.7]


Here $k_1$ = 8.8×10⁸ and $k_2$ = 5×10¹⁴ are constants; $Z$ is the atomic number of the chemical element of the target; $i_A$ the current of the x-ray tube; $E_0$ the energy of the electron beam, determined by the tube voltage; $E$ the photon energy; $E_q$ the ionisation energy of the $q$-shell; $\omega_q$ the fluorescence yield of the $q$-shell; $p$ the fraction of the given characteristic line in its series; $G = 1 - (7Z - 80)/(14Z - 80)$; $\gamma$ = 3.8×10⁻² for the K-series and $\gamma$ = 0.11 for the L-series of characteristic radiation. By adding the bremsstrahlung and characteristic spectra, an idealised x-ray tube spectrum is obtained, which is then attenuated in the target, the window and the filters according to (3.1).

Expression (3.6) is Kramers' law: the number of bremsstrahlung photons per unit energy is proportional to the atomic number of the target and to the tube current, decreases with photon energy as $E_0/E - 1$ and vanishes at the maximum photon energy $E = E_0 = eU$ (the Duane–Hunt limit). The corresponding energy spectrum, $E\,\partial\Psi_X/\partial E = k_1 i_A Z (E_0 - E)$, decreases linearly from low energies to $E_0$. Characteristic radiation (3.7) is produced only when the electron energy exceeds the ionisation energy of the shell. For the K-series of copper ($E_K$ = 8.979 keV) at 45 kV the overvoltage ratio $E_0/E_K$ is 5.0, the factor $(E_0/E_K - 1)^{1.67}$ equals 10.2 and $G$ = 0.62. The K-shell fluorescence yield of copper is about 0.45, so more than half of the K vacancies relax by Auger emission without producing x-rays.

The Kramers formulas used in this chapter and the DM of Chapter 2 describe the same physical processes at different levels of detail. The DM follows the slowing-down of the electrons in a thick reflection target and uses the Bethe–Heitler cross-section with the Elwert factor and the Gryziński K-shell cross-section, so that it reproduces the spectral shape of SpekPy. The Kramers formulas give the bremsstrahlung and characteristic yields in closed form with empirical constants. They are convenient for a transmission tube, whose spectrum is determined mainly by the attenuation in the target and filters (3.1). Since the DEF depends only on the spectral shape (Section 2.6), this simpler description is sufficient to show the effect of the anode self-filtration and of the added filters.

The possibility of radio-enhancement in soft tissue in the presence of heavy elements irradiated with the obtained x-ray tube spectrum is assessed by the DEF calculated as [11]

$$\mathrm{DEF} = 1 + \frac{k_Z\int_{0}^{E_{\max}}\Psi'(E)\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_Z\mathrm{d}E}{\int_{0}^{E_{\max}}\Psi'(E)\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_{\mathrm{s.t.}}\mathrm{d}E}$$

EQNUM[3.8]


Here $(\mu_{en}/\rho)_Z$ and $(\mu_{en}/\rho)_{\mathrm{s.t.}}$ are the mass energy-absorption coefficients of the element with atomic number $Z$ and of soft tissue; $k_Z$ is the mass fraction of the element $Z$ in soft tissue; $\Psi'(E)$ is the differential (spectral) energy fluence of the x-ray beam; and the limits of integration correspond to the minimum and maximum energies of the spectrum.

The ratio of the integrals in (3.8) is the dose-weighted mean $\langle R\rangle$ of the ratio $R(E) = (\mu_{en}/\rho)_Z/(\mu_{en}/\rho)_{\mathrm{s.t.}}$ (cf. (2.25)), so $\mathrm{DEF} = 1 + k_Z\langle R\rangle$ increases linearly with the concentration of the element, with the slope $\langle R\rangle$. Expression (3.8) adds the energy absorbed by the element to that absorbed by the whole tissue mass. With the exact mixture rule (2.22), in which the element replaces the same mass of tissue, $\mathrm{DEF} = 1 + k_Z(\langle R\rangle - 1)$ (2.24). The difference, $k_Z$, does not exceed 0.05 for the concentrations considered here and is small compared with the enhancement itself.

To understand whether radio-enhancement by x-rays with such a spectrum (Fig. 3.3) is possible, and in the presence of which elements, the excitation energies of the K-shell should be considered first. For the elements with atomic numbers between 30 and 65, the binding energies of the K-shell lie between 10 and 50 keV (Fig. 3.4). The excitation energies of the different thresholds are all the more important the heavier the material, owing to the increase of the total photoelectric cross-section. The photoelectric effect induces above all an attenuation of the primary radiation. As shown in Sections 1.2 and 2.7, this attenuation depends very strongly on the energy of the radiation and especially on the atomic number $Z$ of the material. For photon energies below 500 keV, the introduction of a high-Z compound into the tissues makes it possible to improve the radiological contrast significantly, owing to the difference in photon absorption between these elements and the biological matter.

The K-shell binding energy increases approximately as $(Z - 1)^2$ (Moseley's law), from 9.66 keV for zinc ($Z$ = 30) to 52.0 keV for terbium ($Z$ = 65), because the K electrons are screened by only about one other electron and feel almost the whole nuclear charge. For the 45 kVp tube, K-shell photoabsorption is therefore possible only in elements with $Z \le 60$ (neodymium, $E_K$ = 43.57 keV). For promethium ($E_K$ = 45.18 keV) and heavier elements, including samarium ($E_K$ = 46.83 keV), the K-edge lies above the maximum photon energy, and only the L- and M-shells can be ionised. The silver K-edge (25.51 keV) lies just below the maximum of the filtered spectrum (27–33 keV), so most photons of the hardened beam can be absorbed by silver in the K-shell, where the absorption is strongest.

The variations of the total mass attenuation coefficients of the pure elements were characterised on either side of their absorption thresholds: the amplitude of the discontinuities of the total mass attenuation coefficient was evaluated for the K, L1, L2 and L3 shells (Fig. 3.5). For a given element, the absolute increase of the total mass attenuation coefficient is the larger, the lower the threshold energy, i.e. the less bound the electrons of the shell. This is mainly due to the greater value of the total mass attenuation coefficient at low energies (Fig. 3.5, a). However, to characterise the reaction probability, the ratio of the total mass attenuation coefficients just above and just below the K, L1, L2 and L3 thresholds of the various elements must be considered, and then the most tightly bound shells prove to be the most favourable (Fig. 3.5, b). Thus, for the whole set of elements, a photon with an energy a little higher than the K threshold has a probability of being absorbed 4.8 to 7.2 times higher than a photon with a slightly lower energy. This factor does not exceed 1.5 for the L1 and L2 shells and lies between 2.6 and 4.7 for the L3 shell.



![Fig. 3.5. Discontinuities of the total mass attenuation coefficient at the K, L1, L2 and L3 thresholds for the elements with Z = 30–65: (a) amplitude of the discontinuity (μ/ρ)E+ − (μ/ρ)E−; (b) gain (μ/ρ)E+/(μ/ρ)E− at the passing of the thresholds (data of [61])](/home/user/atlant-impossible/dissertation/v2/figures/en/f3_discont.png){width=16cm}



The absolute jumps (Fig. 3.5, a) decrease with $Z$ by more than an order of magnitude between $Z$ = 30 and 65 (at the K-edge from about 210 to 14 cm²/g), because the edges of heavier elements lie at higher energies, where the photoabsorption per unit mass is smaller. The L-edges lie at energies about 7–10 times lower than the K-edge, and their absolute jumps are correspondingly larger. The relative jump (Fig. 3.5, b) shows the share of the shell in the photoabsorption just above its edge. A K-edge jump ratio $r_K$ means that the fraction $(r_K - 1)/r_K$ of the photons absorbed just above the edge interacts with the two K electrons: 0.83 for silver ($r_K$ = 5.8) and 0.80 for samarium ($r_K$ = 5.0), in agreement with the estimate (2.30), by which the K-shell accounts for 80 % of the photoeffect. The ratio decreases slowly with $Z$ because the outer shells take an increasing part of the absorption. Of the L subshells, L3 with four 2p₃/₂ electrons gives the largest jump (3.3 for silver and 2.7 for samarium), whereas the jumps at L2 (two 2p₁/₂ electrons, about 1.4) and L1 (two 2s electrons, about 1.15) are small. For silver the K-edge at 25.51 keV thus gives the largest relative increase of absorption, and this edge lies within the spectrum of Fig. 3.3. Compared with the heavy elements, the Compton effect becomes dominant at lower energies in soft tissue, which consists of light elements. This early contribution of Compton scattering changes the energy dependence of the total mass attenuation coefficient of soft tissue with respect to the heavier elements. The slowing of the decrease of the total attenuation coefficient, observed as early as 20 keV for soft tissue, is shifted to energies of the order of 500 keV for heavy materials, owing to the great predominance of the photoelectric effect at lower energies. It is therefore important to consider not only the absolute contribution of the heavy element to the photoelectric effect but also the gain in absorption compared with the biological matter. In this respect, the optimal energy to maximise the difference in absorption from soft tissue is located just above the absorption thresholds. Fig. 3.6 shows the ratio of the mass energy-absorption coefficients of Ag and Sm to that of soft tissue for photon energies from 1 to 1000 keV. Whereas the energy just above the K thresholds of these elements indeed seems optimal to strengthen the absorption of photons compared with soft tissue, this does not apply exactly to the L thresholds, since above them the ratio keeps increasing and its optimum appears at higher energies, especially for Ag. It also appears in Fig. 3.6 (a, b) that Ag and Sm reach the maximum of the ratio $(\mu_{en}/\rho)_Z/(\mu_{en}/\rho)_{\mathrm{s.t.}}$ differently near the K threshold, whereas after passing the K threshold the maxima are similar. The ratio gains more at the L thresholds of Sm owing to the higher atomic number of this element.



![Fig. 3.6. Ratio of the mass energy-absorption coefficients of pure Ag (a) and Sm (b) to that of soft tissue for photon energies between 1 and 1000 keV](/home/user/atlant-impossible/dissertation/v2/figures/en/f3_ratio_agsm.png){width=16cm}



The shape of these curves follows from the different energy dependences of the energy absorption in the heavy element and in tissue. Between the absorption edges the ratio increases slowly with energy, because the photoabsorption of the light elements of tissue decreases with energy faster than that of the heavy element. At each edge the ratio jumps, but by less than the attenuation coefficient: at the K-edge the ratio increases about 2.4-fold for silver and 1.8-fold for samarium, against 5.8- and 5.0-fold jumps of $\mu/\rho$ (Fig. 3.5). The reason is that $\mu_{en}/\rho$ excludes the energy carried away by fluorescence photons: 83 % of the K vacancies in silver and 93 % in samarium are filled with emission of K x-rays (Kα of 22.2 keV for Ag and 40.1 keV for Sm), which escape from the absorption site. Above about 30–50 keV the energy-absorption coefficient of tissue decreases ever more slowly, because Compton scattering contributes an increasing part of it, while the photoabsorption of the heavy element continues to fall. The ratio therefore reaches a maximum of about 150 at 40–50 keV for Ag and about 160 near 60 keV for Sm and then decreases steeply. At 1 MeV, where Compton scattering dominates in both media, the ratio is 0.85–0.95. It tends to the ratio of the numbers of electrons per unit mass ($Z/A$ = 0.44 for Ag and 0.41 for Sm against 0.55 for soft tissue), slightly increased by the residual photoabsorption of the heavy element.

Within the 45 kVp spectrum the ratio for Ag rises from about 20 at the Cu K lines (8 keV) and about 30–35 just below 25.5 keV to 85–150 above the K-edge. For Sm it is about 40 at 8 keV, because its L-edges (6.72–7.74 keV) lie just below the Cu K lines, and increases only to about 85 at 30–40 keV, since its K-edge (46.83 keV) lies outside the spectrum. Samarium therefore absorbs the soft part of the spectrum, in particular the Cu K lines, more effectively than silver, whereas silver is more effective in the hard part above 25.5 keV.

For the calculated x-ray spectra (Fig. 3.3), the DEF values were determined with (3.8) and are presented in Fig. 3.7. The DEF values grow linearly with increasing $k_Z$, the fraction of the element $Z$ in soft tissue. The use of filters for the x-ray tube spectrum can increase the DEF value. This is achieved by removing from the spectrum the energy region in which the ratio $(\mu_{en}/\rho)_Z/(\mu_{en}/\rho)_{\mathrm{s.t.}}$ is lower.

The linearity follows from (3.8): the energy absorbed by the element is proportional to its mass fraction, and the slope of each line equals the spectrum-averaged ratio $\langle R\rangle$ (Table 3.4). With the thin 30 µm filter the spectrum retains the intense Cu K lines, for which the ratio is only about 20 for silver, and the slope is about 40: the DEF for 1 % Ag is 1.40. The 2.11 mm filter removes the lines and the photons below about 20 keV and leaves mainly photons above the Ag K-edge, where the ratio is 85–150. The slope increases to about 98 and the DEF for 1 % Ag to 1.98, i.e. the enhancement $\mathrm{DEF} - 1$ increases 2.45-fold.

For samarium the effect of filtration is weaker (the slope increases from about 56 to 81), and the order of the two elements is reversed. With the thin filter Sm gives the larger DEF (1.56 against 1.40 at $k_Z$ = 0.01), because its L-edges lie just below the Cu K lines, which are absorbed by Sm about twice as strongly as by Ag. With the thick filter Ag is more effective (1.98 against 1.81), because its K-edge lies inside the hardened spectrum, whereas the K-edge of Sm (46.83 keV) lies above the maximum photon energy of 45 keV. The DEF thus depends on the position of the absorption edges of the element relative to the spectrum; for silver, filtration that concentrates the spectrum above 25.5 keV is the most effective.

## 3.4. Discussion of the results

The obtained results prove the possibility of realising the radiosensitization effect with Ag and Sm and kilovoltage x-ray tubes. Such x-ray tubes, widely used in medicine, give hope for a first step towards solving the problem of the clinical application of radiosensitizers in photon therapy.

At the same time, it is important to underline that the effects occurring after the ionisation of heavy-element atoms should be carefully analysed. The ionisation of an inner shell can trigger a multistep decay of the resulting excited state. Firstly, an atom transformed into a highly charged ion can distort and destroy chemical bonds with its electric field. Secondly, photons and electrons emitted from deeper shells at the first stage of the atomic decay can ionise neighbouring atoms, thereby giving rise to new cascade processes. All these factors should be considered when determining the absorbed dose, as well as in evaluating the relative biological effectiveness of the partial components of the absorbed dose. To solve the latter problem, the inhomogeneity of the microdistribution of the absorbed dose in the biological medium, formed when atoms of heavy elements are activated by photons, must be estimated correctly.

The atomic data of silver illustrate the scale of these processes. About 83 % of the K vacancies are filled with emission of Kα (22.2 keV) and Kβ (24.9 keV) photons, which have mean free paths of about 1.5–2 cm in soft tissue and carry their energy away from the silver atom. The remaining 17 % of the K vacancies and most of the subsequent L-shell vacancies (L3 fluorescence yield 0.05) relax by Auger transitions. The emitted Auger and photoelectrons deposit their energy at distances from nanometres to tens of micrometres from the atom, so the microscopic dose distribution is much more inhomogeneous than the macroscopic DEF suggests (Section 2.6). The calculated DEF is therefore the physical baseline of the effect, to which chemical and biological contributions of the silver compounds and nanoparticles may be added.

The calculations also have limitations that should be kept in mind. The spectra were obtained with the Kramers formulas, whose constants are empirical, and the attenuation in the anode was calculated along the beam axis for an average generation depth. The DEF was calculated in the kerma approximation for a homogeneous distribution of the element, assuming charged-particle equilibrium and neglecting scattered radiation and the reabsorption of fluorescence photons in the sample. These assumptions are adequate for comparing filters and elements, since the comparison depends on the spectral shape. However, the absolute DEF of a particular sample, especially at high concentrations and in thick samples, where beam hardening and self-shielding occur (Section 2.6), should be verified by Monte Carlo calculations and dosimetric measurements.

## 3.5. Conclusions to Chapter 3

1. The spectrum of a 45 kVp tube with a 100 µm Cu transmission anode and a 40 µm Be window, calculated with the Kramers formulas and attenuation in the anode, window and filters, consists of the Cu K lines (8.05 and 8.91 keV) and a bremsstrahlung continuum at 15–45 keV with a maximum at about 27–28 keV. The self-filtration of the anode (about 95 µm of Cu) suppresses the photons between the Cu K-edge (8.98 keV) and about 14 keV.
2. The 30 µm Al filter practically does not change the spectrum above 15 keV, whereas the 2.11 mm Al filter removes the Cu K lines and the photons below about 15–20 keV and shifts the maximum to 30–33 keV, concentrating the spectrum above the Ag K-edge at the cost of a lower intensity.
3. The K-shell binding energies of the elements with $Z$ = 30–65 lie between 9.7 and 52 keV, and for the 45 kVp tube K-shell absorption is possible only for $Z \le 60$. The relative jump of the mass attenuation coefficient is 4.8–7.2 at the K-edge (5.8 for silver), 2.6–4.7 at the L3 edge and below 1.5 at the L1 and L2 edges.
4. The ratio of the mass energy-absorption coefficients of Ag and soft tissue rises from about 20–35 between 8 and 25.5 keV to 85–150 above the K-edge, with a maximum at 40–50 keV. For Sm it is about 40–85 within the 45 kVp spectrum, since its K-edge (46.83 keV) lies above the maximum photon energy.
5. The DEF grows linearly with the mass fraction $k_Z$ of the element. With the 2.11 mm Al filter the DEF of tissue containing 1 % Ag increases from 1.40 to 1.98 (5.88 at $k_Z$ = 0.05). With the thin filter Sm is more effective than Ag (1.56 against 1.40 at $k_Z$ = 0.01), and with the thick filter Ag is more effective (1.98 against 1.81). Filtration that removes the photons below the Ag K-edge is therefore the main means of increasing the dose enhancement by silver.


# CONCLUSIONS

1. High-Z dose enhancement is much larger for kilovoltage than for megavoltage photons, and silver nanoparticles were reported to radiosensitize glioma, breast and colorectal cancer models. The Ag/tissue ratio of mass energy-absorption coefficients rises from 21–32 below the Ag K-edge (25.514 keV) to 103–139 at 30–45 keV, so the dose enhancement by silver is determined by the tube spectrum and its filtration.

2. The developed model (DM) is a semi-analytical thick-target model of the copper-anode tube spectrum (Bethe stopping power, non-relativistic Bethe–Heitler cross-section with the Elwert factor, Gryziński K-shell cross-section) that includes bremsstrahlung-induced K fluorescence, anode self-absorption and filtration. Its single parameter, the electron depth factor $\eta$ = 0.40, was fitted to the SpekPy v2 bremsstrahlung shape at 45 kV with a root-mean-square deviation of 2.46 %.

3. Under charged-particle equilibrium the DEF is the spectrum-weighted ratio of the mass energy-absorption coefficients of the loaded and unloaded tissue. At the surface $\mathrm{DEF} - 1$ is proportional to the silver mass fraction, with a slope determined by the share of the dose spectrum above the Ag K-edge; inside the tissue the DEF increases with depth owing to beam hardening and decreases in a heavily loaded region owing to self-shielding.

4. In silver the photoelectric effect dominates up to 0.24 MeV, so in the spectrum of a 45 kV tube the interaction is almost purely photoelectric, with a 5.8-fold rise of the attenuation coefficient at the K-edge. Water, soft tissue and brain have practically equal mass attenuation coefficients at 1–100 keV, cortical bone attenuates 3.5–5.3 times more strongly at 10–30 keV, and silver 97–108 times more strongly than soft tissue between its K-edge and 30 keV.

5. The unfiltered 45 kV copper-anode tube is a very intense source (15.3 Gy/s at 10 cm and 40 mA) that requires shielding, interlocks and dosimetric control. The REIS-I facility with a BS1 tube (45 kV, 100 µA) in a lead shielding chamber with a 1-mm lead cover, which transmits at most 6×10⁻⁶ of the primary photons, provides safe irradiation of biological samples.

6. The spectrum of a 45 kVp tube with a 100 µm copper transmission anode consists of the Cu K lines (8.05 and 8.91 keV) and a bremsstrahlung continuum at 15–45 keV with a maximum at about 27–28 keV; the self-filtration of the anode suppresses the photons between 9 and 14 keV. A 2.11 mm aluminium filter removes the Cu K lines and the photons below about 20 keV and concentrates the spectrum above the Ag K-edge, whereas a 30 µm filter changes the spectrum only slightly.

7. The K-shell binding energies of the elements with $Z$ = 30–65 lie between 9.7 and 52 keV, and the absorption increases 4.8–7.2-fold at the K-edge (5.8-fold for silver), 2.6–4.7-fold at the L3 edge and less than 1.5-fold at the L1 and L2 edges. The K-edge of silver (25.5 keV) lies inside the spectrum of the 45 kVp tube, and that of samarium (46.8 keV) above it.

8. The DEF of soft tissue grows linearly with the mass fraction of the element. For 1 % of silver it is 1.40 with the 30 µm Al filter and 1.98 with the 2.11 mm Al filter (5.88 for 5 % of silver). With thin filtration samarium is more effective than silver (DEF 1.56 for 1 %), because its L-edges lie just below the Cu K lines; with thick filtration silver is more effective than samarium (1.98 against 1.81). Filtration is the decisive parameter for dose enhancement by silver.

9. The results show the possibility of radiosensitization with silver and kilovoltage x-ray tubes. The cascades of Auger electrons and fluorescence photons after inner-shell ionisation make the microscopic dose distribution strongly inhomogeneous, which must be taken into account in dosimetry and in estimating the biological effectiveness.

## Practical recommendations

1. For irradiation of silver-containing samples with a 45 kV copper-anode tube, use thick aluminium filtration (about 2 mm, e.g. 2.11 mm Al) that removes the Cu K lines and the photons below about 20 keV; avoid unfiltered and thinly filtered beams.
2. Specify the beam (voltage, anode, window, filters and measured first HVL) and report the DEF together with the spectrum and the reference medium.
3. Plan exposure times from the calculated tissue kerma rate for the chosen filter, taking into account the lower dose rate of thick filters, and verify the output dosimetrically.
4. Operate the REIS-I generator only in the shielding chamber with the 1-mm lead cover; keep the personnel at least 1.5 m behind or beside the high-voltage unit and carry out dose-rate surveys.
5. Choose the filter jointly with the radiosensitizer: for elements whose K-edge lies inside the spectrum, such as silver, hard filtration is advantageous, whereas for elements whose K-edge lies above the maximum photon energy, such as samarium, thin filtration may be preferable.
