# CHAPTER 3. RESULTS OF SPECTRUM MODELLING AND DOSE-ENHANCEMENT CALCULATIONS

The results were obtained with the developed model (DM) of Chapter 2 and with the reference code SpekPy v2.5.4 [@poludniowski2021; @spekpy_soft]. Sections 3.1–3.4 deal with the validation of the DM and with the spectrum of the 45 kV copper-anode tube, Sections 3.5–3.6 with the dose enhancement factor (DEF) of Ag-loaded soft tissue and the choice of filtration, and Sections 3.7–3.11 with depth effects, the tube voltage, other high-Z elements, Monte Carlo verification, uncertainties and the discussion. Unless stated otherwise, the baseline conditions of {{REF:t2_1}} apply: a constant potential of 45 kV, an anode angle θ = 12°, a 0.5 mm Be window, 10 cm of air between the focal spot and the sample, and an energy bin width of 0.05 keV. The DEF is calculated in the kerma approximation, which assumes charged-particle equilibrium (CPE), for ICRU-44 soft tissue [@icru44] with the NIST mass energy-absorption coefficients [@nist_xraymass]; $R(E)$ denotes the ratio of the mass energy-absorption coefficients of Ag and tissue (Section 2.6).

## 3.1. Validation of the developed spectrum model

No measured spectrum of the particular tube was available, so the DM was validated against SpekPy v2, a deterministic physics-based toolkit that supports copper anodes at 10–50 kV [@poludniowski2021] and has been validated against measured spectra and half-value layers (HVL) [@bujila2020; @omar2020b]. The only adjustable parameter of the DM, the electron depth factor $\eta$ of {{REF:eq:depth}}, was fitted to the bremsstrahlung continuum alone (characteristic lines switched off in both codes; 45 kV, θ = 12°, 0.5 mm Be, no air) by minimising the root-mean-square deviation of the area-normalised spectra,

$$
\delta(\eta) = \frac{1}{\max_i b_i}\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(a_i(\eta) - b_i\right)^2\right]^{1/2}
$$ {#eq:rms}

where $a_i(\eta)$ and $b_i$ are the bremsstrahlung fluences of the DM and of SpekPy in energy bin $i$, each divided by its sum over the $N$ bins with $E_i > 3$ keV, and the factor $1/\max_i b_i$ expresses the deviation as a fraction of the spectrum maximum. The fit gave $\eta = 0.40$ with $\delta = 2.46$ %, against $\delta = 8.47$ % for straight-line penetration ($\eta = 1$). Because of multiple elastic scattering, the photons are thus produced at less than half the depth implied by a straight path in the continuous slowing-down approximation (CSDA): the maximum projected depth of 45 keV electrons in copper is 2.536 µm, whereas their CSDA range is 6.481 µm. A shallower production depth reduces the self-absorption of the soft bremsstrahlung in the anode, which is why $\eta = 1$ yields too hard a spectrum. The value $\eta = 0.40$ was then kept fixed, so all other configurations are predictions rather than fits.

{{REF:t3_1}} compares the two codes for 25–50 kV and anode angles of 6–30° without the air path. With 1 mm Al, which removes the Cu K lines, the first HVL ({{REF:eq:hvl}}) of the DM is lower than that of SpekPy by 3.5–4.6 %, and the mean energies differ by no more than 0.54 keV. Without added filtration the HVL deviation ranges from −2.6 % to −19.4 %, because the DM underestimates the Cu K lines by about 25–43 %. The deficit is largest at low voltage, where the Gryzinski cross-section [@gryzinski1965] rises too slowly above the ionisation threshold compared with the distorted-wave Born calculations [@bote2008; @llovet2014] underlying the K-line model of SpekPy [@omar2018]. A weaker 8 keV line gives more weight to the softer 3–8 keV continuum in the air kerma and thus lowers the first HVL of the unfiltered beam.

The DM continuum is slightly richer in photons at 10–20 keV and poorer above about 25 keV, the ratio falling to 0.80–0.85 at 40 keV; this mild softening explains the HVL deficit of about 4 % for filtered beams. The lower panels show the ratio of the developed model to SpekPy.}}

The absolute air kerma of the DM exceeds that of SpekPy by a factor of 1.08–1.22 without filtration and 1.21–1.42 with 1 mm Al, mainly because the DM neglects electron backscattering (the backscatter coefficient of copper is about 0.3) and assumes complete slowing-down of every electron. Since the DEF depends only on the spectral shape, it is unaffected, and absolute dose rates are taken from SpekPy. To check that the model is not tuned to copper only, the DM with the same $\eta$ was applied to a tungsten anode (θ = 12°) at 50, 80 and 100 kV with 2.5 mm Al, which removes the W L lines not included in the DM ({{REF:t3_1w}}). The HVL deviations are −5.7 %, −1.5 % and +0.5 %; for orientation, the IEC 61267 quality RQR3, a 50 kV tungsten beam, has a first HVL of 1.78 mm Al [@iec61267], comparable to both values at 50 kV. As for copper, the tungsten spectra of the DM are slightly narrower than those of SpekPy, with a weaker high-energy tail ({{REF:fig06_validation_w}}), and the agreement improves with voltage. Overall, one value of $\eta$ reproduces the HVL of filtered beams within about 4 % for copper and within 6 % for tungsten; the main weakness of the DM is the underestimated Cu K-line intensity, which matters for unfiltered and Kα-dominated beams. The main DEF results therefore use SpekPy spectra, with the DM as an independent check.

## 3.2. Spectrum of the 45 kV copper-anode tube

The baseline spectrum is shown in {{REF:fig07_baseline}}. The Be window and the air path remove practically all photons below about 3 keV; the small kink near 3.2 keV is the K-edge of argon in air. The continuum rises to a plateau at about 7–9 keV and drops abruptly at the Cu K-edge (8.98 keV), because photons just above this energy are absorbed in the anode much more strongly, so that the anode acts as its own K-edge filter. Above the edge the continuum recovers to a shallow maximum near 12–13 keV and decreases to zero at 45 keV, and the Ag K-edge falls into its weak high-energy part. The Cu Kα (8.05 keV) and Kβ (8.91 keV) lines exceed the neighbouring continuum by more than two orders of magnitude at the bin width of 0.05 keV. The DM reproduces this structure, with a higher continuum because of its higher absolute bremsstrahlung yield (Section 3.1).

{{FIG:fig07_baseline|Spectrum of the 45 kV Cu-anode tube (θ = 12°, 0.5 mm Be, 10 cm air) at 10 cm from the focal spot calculated with SpekPy v2 and with the developed model. The dashed line marks the Ag K-edge (25.514 keV).}}

The characteristic lines and the beam-quality parameters are summarised in {{REF:t3_2}}. According to SpekPy, characteristic photons make up 68.5 % of the photon fluence, so the unfiltered beam is essentially a source of Cu K radiation with an admixture of bremsstrahlung. In the DM the line intensities follow from 0.0159 K-shell ionisations per incident 45 keV electron, the fluorescence yield $\omega_K = 0.4538$ and the radiative rates of xraylib [@schoonjans2011]; indirect fluorescence contributes 4.5 % of the Kα and 4.8 % of the Kβ intensity. The Kβ/Kα fluence ratio of the SpekPy spectrum, 0.1431, slightly exceeds the ratio of the radiative rates (0.134), because the more energetic Kβ photons are attenuated less in the anode, the window and air, and because the integration windows contain some continuum. A 20 µm Ni filter, whose K-edge (8.333 keV, {{REF:t2_2}}) lies between the Kα and Kβ energies, reduces this ratio to 0.0023; this is the classical β-filter of x-ray diffraction [@cullity2001].

The quality of the unfiltered beam is very low: SpekPy gives a mean energy ({{REF:eq:emean}}) of 10.38 keV, a first HVL of 0.0478 mm Al and an effective energy of 7.82 keV, and the DM values are close to these. An HVL below 50 µm Al and an effective energy below the Kα energy show that the dose to soft tissue is delivered mainly by the Cu K lines and the soft continuum. Only 4.64 % of the photons lie above the Ag K-edge, carrying 14.1 % of the energy fluence, and their share in the kerma is smaller still because $\mu_{en}/\rho$ decreases steeply with photon energy. The output is very high: 383 mGy/mAs at 10 cm according to SpekPy, which corresponds to 15.3 Gy/s at 40 mA, with a photon fluence of $3.66 \times 10^{10}$ cm$^{-2}$ per mAs. Combined with the dominance of 8 keV photons, for which $R(E)$ is only 21.0, this predicts a modest DEF for the bare beam.

## 3.3. Influence of tube design parameters

Three design parameters were varied one at a time around the baseline: the anode angle, the tube voltage and the thickness of the Be window; the beam-quality parameters, the air kerma and the DEF for 1 % Ag are listed in {{REF:t3_3}}. Increasing the anode angle from 6° to 30° raises the air kerma by a factor of 1.56 and slightly softens the beam. The reason is self-filtration in the anode: a photon produced at depth $d$ leaves along the central ray over a path $d/\sin\theta$, which at 6° is about 4.8 times longer than at 30°. As {{REF:fig08_angle}} shows, the effect is strongest where copper is most opaque, at the soft end of the spectrum and just above the Cu K-edge, where the fluence at small angles is reduced several-fold, whereas above about 25 keV the curves nearly merge. The same mechanism causes the heel effect, the decrease of intensity towards the anode side of the field [@bushberg2012]; the present calculations refer to the central ray only. A small anode angle thus gives a smaller effective focal spot but a lower output and a slightly harder beam, while the DEF hardly changes.

{{FIG:fig08_angle|Influence of the anode angle (6–30°) on the spectrum of the 45 kV Cu-anode tube (0.5 mm Be, 10 cm air; SpekPy v2). The dashed line marks the Ag K-edge.}}

The tube voltage has the strongest influence on the output: from 25 to 50 kV the air kerma increases from 163 to 430 mGy/mAs. The gain comes largely from the Cu K lines, because at higher voltage the electrons travel a longer path at energies where the K-shell ionisation cross-section is close to its maximum of about 351 b, reached near 35 keV. The continuum extends up to $eU$, where $e$ is the elementary charge and $U$ the tube voltage, and its high-energy part grows strongly ({{REF:fig09_kvp}}), whereas the fluence just above the Cu K-edge hardly changes: such photons escape only from a thin surface layer of the anode, and the additional bremsstrahlung produced deeper by faster electrons is reabsorbed. The DEF increases only from 1.1932 at 25 kV to 1.2027 at 50 kV. At 25 kV no photon reaches the Ag K-edge, yet the DEF is already 1.19, so the enhancement of the unfiltered beam is caused almost entirely by photoabsorption in the L and M shells of Ag, where $R(E)$ is 21–32.

{{FIG:fig09_kvp|Spectra of the Cu-anode tube at tube voltages of 25–50 kV (θ = 12°, 0.5 mm Be, 10 cm air; SpekPy v2). The dashed line marks the Ag K-edge.}}

The Be window affects only the soft end of the spectrum ({{REF:fig10_be}}). Beryllium (Z = 4) absorbs weakly, so increasing the window thickness from 0.15 to 2.0 mm removes mainly photons below about 6 keV, weakens the Cu K lines by a smaller fraction and hardly changes the spectrum above 10 keV. The air kerma decreases from 423 to 271 mGy/mAs, while the DEF rises from 1.1989 to 1.2061. The window thickness is fixed by the tube design, but it must be known accurately for spectrum calculations, since it changes the output by more than one third over the studied range.

{{FIG:fig10_be|Influence of the Be window thickness (0.15–2.0 mm) on the low-energy part of the spectrum of the 45 kV Cu-anode tube (θ = 12°, 10 cm air; SpekPy v2).}}

The DEF values obtained with the DM spectra agree with those of SpekPy within 0.003 for all design variants. Overall, the design parameters change the output by factors of up to 1.6 (anode angle, window) and 2.6 (voltage), but the DEF for 1 % Ag remains between 1.19 and 1.21. In every unfiltered beam the tissue dose is dominated by the Cu K lines and by photons of 3–15 keV, for which $R(E)$ is only 21–27, and the design parameters shift the balance between these components only slightly. The tube design is therefore a lever for the dose rate rather than for the DEF; a substantial increase in the DEF requires reshaping the spectrum with added filters.

## 3.4. Influence of added filters on the spectrum

Added filters reshape the spectrum through the transmission $\exp[-\mu_f(E)\,t]$ of {{REF:eq:filter}}, where $\mu_f(E)$ is the linear attenuation coefficient of the filter material and $t$ is its thickness. Three groups were studied: aluminium, whose K-edge (1.56 keV) lies below the spectrum; nickel and copper, whose K-edges lie near the Cu K lines; and molybdenum, silver and tin, whose K-edges (20.0, 25.514 and 29.2 keV, {{REF:t2_2}}) lie in the hard part of the spectrum. The beam parameters are given in {{REF:t3_4}}.

Aluminium acts as a classical hardening filter ({{REF:fig11_al}}). Its photoelectric cross-section falls roughly as $E^{-3}$, so the soft continuum and the Cu K lines are removed far more efficiently than the photons above the Ag K-edge. With 0.25 mm Al the Kα line is weakened by more than an order of magnitude, and with 1–2 mm Al it practically disappears; the maximum of the continuum moves from below 9 keV to about 27 keV with 2 mm Al, while the fluence above 30 keV decreases only moderately. Accordingly, the fraction of photons above the Ag K-edge rises to 49.8 % with 1 mm and 65.0 % with 2 mm Al. The HVL rises abruptly from 0.104 to 0.454 mm Al between the 0.25 and 0.5 mm filters, because behind the thinner filter the residual Cu K lines still carry a large part of the air kerma. The price is the output: 1 mm Al reduces the dose rate about 120-fold and 2 mm Al about 270-fold.

{{FIG:fig11_al|Spectra of the 45 kV Cu-anode tube with added Al filters of 0.25–2.0 mm (θ = 12°, 0.5 mm Be, 10 cm air; SpekPy v2). The dashed line marks the Ag K-edge.}}

Copper filters behave differently ({{REF:fig12_cu_ni}}). The K-edge of copper (8.979 keV) lies just above its own Kα and Kβ energies, so a copper filter is relatively transparent to the Cu K radiation while absorbing the continuum at 9–20 keV very strongly. With 0.1 mm Cu the mean energy (24.08 keV) is close to that obtained with 0.5 mm Al (23.78 keV), yet the HVL is 6.5 times smaller: the mean energy is weighted by fluence and reflects the hard continuum, whereas the HVL is weighted by air kerma and is controlled by the transmitted Kα line, for which $\mu_{en}/\rho$ of air is much larger. Only 0.2 mm Cu suppresses the lines sufficiently, giving 91.2 % of the photons above the Ag K-edge but a relative air kerma of only $7.64 \times 10^{-4}$. A 0.02 mm Ni filter, with its K-edge between Kα and Kβ, suppresses the Kβ line and the continuum just above 8.33 keV while transmitting Kα with a moderate loss. The result is a quasi-monochromatic 8 keV beam, suitable for x-ray diffraction but not for dose enhancement by Ag.

{{FIG:fig12_cu_ni|Spectra of the 45 kV Cu-anode tube with a 0.02 mm Ni filter and with Cu filters of 0.05–0.2 mm (θ = 12°, 0.5 mm Be, 10 cm air; SpekPy v2). The dashed line marks the Ag K-edge.}}

The K-edge filters of Mo, Ag and Sn are compared with 1 mm Al in {{REF:fig13_kedge}}. Each of them transmits a band below its K-edge and cuts the fluence above the edge several-fold or more; narrow residual Cu K lines remain behind the Mo and thin Sn filters. The 0.05 mm Mo filter transmits the 12–20 keV band but strongly suppresses the photons above 20 keV, so that only 31.5 % of its photons exceed the Ag K-edge. The 0.05 mm Ag filter follows the Al-filtered curve up to 25.5 keV and then removes precisely the photons that are most useful for enhancement by Ag: although its HVL (0.624 mm Al) exceeds that of 0.5 mm Al (0.454 mm Al), only 20.1 % of its photons lie above the Ag K-edge, against 38.2 %. Tin preserves the 25.5–29.2 keV window directly above the Ag K-edge but removes the photons above 29.2 keV, where $R(E)$ is highest (102.7 at 30 keV and 138.8 at 45 keV). Consequently, 0.1 mm Sn gives a slightly larger HVL than 1 mm Al but a smaller fraction of photons above the Ag K-edge (40.4 % against 49.8 %).

{{FIG:fig13_kedge|Spectra of the 45 kV Cu-anode tube filtered by K-edge absorbers (Mo 0.05 mm, Ag 0.05 mm, Sn 0.05 and 0.1 mm) compared with 1.0 mm Al, relative to the maximum of the Al-filtered spectrum (SpekPy v2). The dashed line marks the Ag K-edge.}}

Neither the mean energy nor the HVL alone therefore characterises the part of the spectrum that matters for Ag. The DEF broadly follows the fraction of photons above the Ag K-edge as long as the kerma is not dominated by the Cu K lines. The 0.1 mm Cu filter, with 55.1 % of its photons above the edge, gives a DEF of only 1.288, because the transmitted Kα line delivers most of the tissue dose.

## 3.5. Dose enhancement factor of Ag-loaded soft tissue

In the kerma approximation the DEF is the ratio of two spectrum-weighted integrals ({{REF:eq:def_spec}}), so its value is determined by the distribution of the dose integrand $E\,\Phi(E)\,\mu_{en}/\rho$ over energy ({{REF:fig14_integrand}}). Without added filtration the integrand is dominated by the Cu K lines and the 3–15 keV continuum and falls below $10^{-4}$ of its maximum above about 20 keV: although 14.1 % of the energy fluence lies above the Ag K-edge, $\mu_{en}/\rho$ of tissue is so small there that these photons contribute almost nothing to the dose. In the dominant region $R(E)$ is only 21.0 at 8 keV, 23.0 at 10 keV and 26.9 at 15 keV, so 1 % Ag raises the integrand almost uniformly by about 20 %. With 1 mm Al the integrand is concentrated between 15 and 45 keV with a maximum near 18–19 keV, and the residual Kβ line exceeds the Kα line, which aluminium attenuates more strongly. At the Ag K-edge the integrand of the loaded tissue jumps, and above the edge $R(E)$ increases from 76.2 at 25.6 keV to 138.8 at 45 keV; since about half of the photons of this beam lie above the edge, this region provides the larger part of the enhancement.

{{FIG:fig14_integrand|Dose integrand (product of photon energy, fluence spectrum and mass energy-absorption coefficient) for ICRU-44 soft tissue (shaded) and for tissue containing 1 % Ag (line), without added filtration (left) and with 1.0 mm Al (right); 45 kV Cu-anode tube, SpekPy v2. The dashed line marks the Ag K-edge.}}

The DEF increases linearly with the Ag mass fraction $w$ for every filter ({{REF:fig15_def_conc}}, {{REF:t3_5}}). The linearity follows from the mixture rule for $\mu_{en}/\rho$: according to {{REF:eq:def_lin}}, $\mathrm{DEF} = 1 + w(\langle R\rangle - 1)$, where $\langle R\rangle$ is the mean of $R(E)$ weighted by the dose spectrum of unloaded tissue ({{REF:eq:ratio_avg}}). The slope $(\mathrm{DEF}-1)/w$ per 1 % Ag grows from 0.202 without a filter to 0.804 with 0.2 mm Cu, which corresponds to $\langle R\rangle$ of about 21 and 81. For 1 % Ag the DEF is 1.20 without a filter, 1.48 with 1 mm Al, 1.62 with 2 mm Al and 1.80 with 0.2 mm Cu. At 0.1 % Ag (1 mg of Ag per gram of loaded tissue) the enhancement is only 2–8 %, whereas at 0.5 % the DEF reaches 1.241 with 1 mm Al. The strict linearity holds at the surface of the sample; inside an extended Ag-loaded region the attenuation by Ag itself makes the dependence non-linear (Section 3.7).

{{FIG:fig15_def_conc|Dose enhancement factor of ICRU-44 soft tissue as a function of the Ag mass fraction for different added filters (45 kV Cu-anode tube, SpekPy v2 spectra, kerma approximation at the surface).}}

The ranking of the filters ({{REF:t3_4}}) confirms that the DEF is governed by the share of the dose integrand above the Ag K-edge rather than by the mean energy. After 0.2 mm Cu and 2 mm Al come 0.2 mm Sn (1.524) and 1 mm Al; the Ag and Mo filters remain below 0.5 mm Al, and the thin Cu and Ni filters improve only slightly on the unfiltered beam. The Ni-filtered beam, being nearly a pure Cu Kα source, gives practically the same DEF as the unfiltered beam (1.208 against 1.202). At a similar HVL, 0.1 mm Sn yields a lower DEF than 1 mm Al (1.444 against 1.482), because it removes the hardest photons.

The DM and SpekPy spectra give practically the same DEF without filtration and for all design variants. With 0.5–2 mm Al the DM is lower by 0.6–1.3 %, and for the Sn, Ag and Mo filters the deviation is below 1 %; the sign agrees with the weaker high-energy tail of the DM continuum (Section 3.1). Where residual Cu K lines carry a substantial part of the kerma, the DM gives higher values, by 0.5 % for 0.25 mm Al and by about 2 % for 0.1 and 0.2 mm Cu, because the underestimated lines shift the dose towards the hard continuum. Differences of 1–2 % are small compared with the differences between filters, so the DEF is robust to the choice of spectrum model.

The choice of the reference medium has a small but systematic effect. With water instead of ICRU-44 soft tissue, the DEF for 1 % Ag is 1.2018 without a filter, 1.4965 with 1 mm Al, 1.644 with 2 mm Al and 1.8274 with 0.2 mm Cu, i.e. up to 1.3 % higher for filtered beams. Although ICRU-44 tissue contains less oxygen than water, its 1.3 % by mass of Na, P, S, Cl and K increases its photoabsorption, so that its $\mu_{en}/\rho$ exceeds that of water at 15–40 keV and the DEF is lower. This difference should be taken into account when DEF values calculated for water are compared with values for tissue.

## 3.6. Optimisation of the filtration

Every filter raises the DEF at the expense of the dose rate, so the optimal filter maximises the DEF at a given transmission. {{REF:fig16_tradeoff}} shows the DEF for 1 % Ag versus the relative air kerma for seven filter materials of increasing thickness (xraylib attenuation coefficients applied to the unfiltered SpekPy spectrum). The Al and Ti curves practically coincide and lie highest from 1 down to about $10^{-3}$, whereas the Ni and Cu curves stay low while the Cu Kα line is transmitted and rise steeply only below about $10^{-2}$. The K-edge filters fall behind: the Sn curve lies below the Al and Ti curves at all transmissions, the Mo curve levels off near 1.4, and the Ag curve passes through a maximum of about 1.35 and then decreases.

{{FIG:fig16_tradeoff|Dose enhancement factor for 1 % Ag versus the relative air kerma of the beam for Al, Ti, Ni, Cu, Mo, Ag and Sn filters of increasing thickness (45 kV Cu-anode tube, θ = 12°, 0.5 mm Be, 10 cm air; SpekPy v2).}}

For a systematic comparison, the thickness $t$ of each filter was found from the condition of equal air-kerma transmission

$$
\mathcal{T}_K(t) = \frac{\int_{0}^{T_0} \Phi_0(E)\,E\,\left(\mu_{en}/\rho\right)_{\mathrm{air}}\exp\left[-\mu_f(E)\,t\right]\mathrm{d}E}{\int_{0}^{T_0} \Phi_0(E)\,E\,\left(\mu_{en}/\rho\right)_{\mathrm{air}}\,\mathrm{d}E}
$$ {#eq:transm}

where $\mathcal{T}_K(t)$ is the air-kerma transmission, $\Phi_0(E)$ is the fluence spectrum of the unfiltered beam, $(\mu_{en}/\rho)_{\mathrm{air}}$ is the mass energy-absorption coefficient of air, $\mu_f(E)$ is the linear attenuation coefficient of the filter element and $T_0$ = 45 keV is the maximum photon energy. Equation {{REF:eq:transm}} was solved numerically for $\mathcal{T}_K$ = 10 %, 1 % and 0.1 % for the elements from Al (Z = 13) to Bi (Z = 83), excluding gases, liquids, P, S, Se, the alkali metals, radioactive elements, Tl and the lanthanides other than Gd. The results are shown in {{REF:fig17_filter_scan}} and, for selected elements, in {{REF:t3_6}}.

The dependence on Z reflects the positions of the absorption edges of the filter relative to the Cu lines and to the Ag K-edge; at 10 % transmission all elements still give 1.220–1.241. Elements from Al to Co (Z = 13–27), whose K-edges lie below the Cu Kα energy, absorb the Cu lines strongly, attenuate the rest of the spectrum smoothly and give 1.88–1.91 at 0.1 %; at 1 % the DEF decreases from Ti to Co, because a K-edge close to the Kα energy lets more of the soft continuum below the edge leak through. For Ni to As (Z = 28–33) the K-edge lies above the Kα energy, so the line is transmitted and dominates the kerma, making these the worst filters at 10 % and 1 %. Elements from Sr to Ag (Z = 38–47) absorb the photons above the Ag K-edge and at 0.1 % form a deep minimum, lowest for Ag (1.335), Pd (1.340) and Rh (1.346). For Cd to Ba (Z = 48–56) the K-edges lie above the Ag K-edge, and the DEF at 0.1 % grows with Z. Heavier elements act through L-shell absorption: Gd, with its L3 edge (7.243 keV) below the Cu Kα energy, behaves like the light absorbers, whereas for Hf to Bi the L3 edges lie above Kα (11.919 keV for Au, {{REF:t2_2}}), the line leaks through, and W, Au and Pb give only 1.350–1.351 at 1 %.

{{FIG:fig17_filter_scan|Dose enhancement factor for 1 % Ag obtained with filters of different elements (Z = 13–83) whose thickness gives an equal air-kerma transmission of 10 %, 1 % and 0.1 % (45 kV Cu-anode tube, θ = 12°, 0.5 mm Be, 10 cm air; SpekPy v2).}}

At 10 % and 1 % transmission the light and medium-Z absorbers Al, Si, Ca, Sc and Ti give practically the same DEF (1.236–1.241 and 1.452–1.455, respectively); at 0.1 % Ca, Sc, Ti and V give 1.907, whereas Al gives 1.884, i.e. 1.2 % less. As calcium and scandium are chemically or technically unsuitable as filter foils, Al and Ti are the practical materials. Titanium needs a much thinner foil (111.2 instead of 824.4 µm at 1 %) and is slightly more effective at strong attenuation, because in the several-millimetre-thick Al filter the weakly energy-dependent Compton scattering contributes more to the attenuation of the hard photons.

For an irradiation experiment, {{REF:t3_7}} gives the tissue kerma at the sample surface and the time needed to deliver a dose $D$ at tube current $I$,

$$
t_D = \frac{D}{c\,I\int_{0}^{T_0} \Phi(E)\,E\,\left(\mu_{en}/\rho\right)_t\,\mathrm{d}E}
$$ {#eq:texp}

where $D$ = 2 Gy, $I$ = 40 mA, $\Phi(E)$ is the fluence spectrum per unit tube charge at 10 cm (cm$^{-2}$ keV$^{-1}$ mAs$^{-1}$), $(\mu_{en}/\rho)_t$ refers to ICRU-44 tissue and $c = 1.602 \times 10^{-13}$ Gy g keV$^{-1}$ converts keV/g into Gy; under CPE this tissue kerma equals the absorbed dose at the surface, without backscatter (Section 2.5). Without a filter 2 Gy are delivered in 0.12 s, which is difficult to time reproducibly, whereas with 1 mm and 2 mm Al the irradiation takes 14.6 and 32.5 s. The 0.2 mm Cu filter gives the highest DEF of the studied set (1.80) only because of its very strong attenuation: at an equal transmission of 0.1 %, Cu gives 1.74, Al and Ti 1.88–1.91. It needs 158.4 s, and near this thickness the DEF changes steeply ({{REF:fig16_tradeoff}}), which makes it sensitive to thickness tolerances.

{{TABLE:t3_7}}

Filtration is thus the decisive design lever for dose enhancement by Ag with a copper-anode tube. Aluminium of 1–2 mm, which raises the DEF for 1 % Ag from 1.20 to 1.48–1.62 at convenient exposure times, or titanium of 0.1–0.3 mm is the optimal choice. K-edge filters with edges at 20–30 keV (Mo, Ag, Sn) and filters that transmit the Cu Kα line (Ni, thin Cu) should be avoided.
