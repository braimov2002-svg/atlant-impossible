# CHAPTER 3. X-RAY TUBE, CALCULATION OF ITS SPECTRUM AND OF THE DOSE ENHANCEMENT FACTOR FOR BIOLOGICAL TISSUE WITH Ag

This chapter describes the 45 kV copper-anode x-ray tube (Section 3.1), the semi-analytical developed model (DM) of its spectrum, its validation and the calculated spectra (Section 3.2), and the dose enhancement factor (DEF) of silver-loaded biological tissue with its Monte Carlo (MC) verification (Section 3.3). The models are implemented in the code of Appendix A; energies are in keV and lengths in cm unless stated otherwise. The reference spectrum code is SpekPy v2.5.4 [@poludniowski2021; @spekpy_soft], and the baseline conditions of {{REF:t2_1}} apply unless stated otherwise: constant potential 45 kV, anode angle θ = 12°, 0.5 mm Be window, 10 cm of air between focal spot and sample, and 0.05 keV energy bins. The DEF is calculated in the kerma approximation, which assumes charged-particle equilibrium (CPE), for ICRU-44 soft tissue of the International Commission on Radiation Units and Measurements (ICRU) [@icru44] with mass energy-absorption coefficients of the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) [@nist_xraymass]; $R(E)$ is the ratio of these coefficients for Ag and tissue (Section 3.3.1).

## 3.1. X-ray tube

### 3.1.1. Design and technical parameters of an x-ray tube with a copper anode

Electrons from the heated cathode are focused onto the anode focal spot, which they reach with the kinetic energy $T_0 = eU$, where $U$ is the tube voltage and $e$ the elementary charge (45 keV in the baseline); only a small fraction of this energy is emitted as x-rays [@bushberg2012; @podgorsak2016]. The photon number is proportional to the tube current–time product (mAs), while the spectral shape is independent of the current, so all spectra are normalised to 1 mAs. In the modelled reflection-target tube ({{REF:fig01_tube}}), the beam leaves through the electron-irradiated face of the thick anode. The anode angle $\theta$ between the anode surface and the central ray, which is perpendicular to the electron beam, is thus both the angle of electron incidence to the surface normal and the photon take-off angle. A small $\theta$ reduces the effective focal spot but makes the beam on the anode side weaker and harder (heel effect) [@bushberg2012]; the DM treats the central ray only. The beryllium window and anode self-absorption form the inherent filtration; added metal filters remove the soft part of the spectrum and harden the beam [@khan2014] before the photons cross the air gap to the sample at the focus–sample distance $r$.

{{FIG:fig01_tube|Modelled reflection-target copper-anode tube and irradiation geometry: central-ray photons emitted at the anode angle θ pass the beryllium window, the added filter and the air gap (focus–sample distance d, denoted r in the text) to the Ag-containing tissue sample.}}

Copper-anode tubes are standard x-ray diffraction sources, which use the intense Cu Kα line and suppress Kβ with a nickel filter whose K-edge lies between the two lines [@cullity2001]. For irradiations with silver, however, the useful photons lie above the Ag K-edge at 25.514 keV (Section 1.5), in the continuum that diffraction treats as background, so the whole spectrum must be modelled and shaped by filtration; as most spectrum models were developed for tungsten anodes (Section 3.2.1), a dedicated copper-anode model is required. {{REF:t2_1}} lists the tube parameters, their baseline values and the ranges studied in Sections 3.2 and 3.3; the voltage, anode, anode angle, window and filters change the spectral shape, whereas the distance acts through the inverse-square law and the air attenuation. A constant potential is assumed, so all electrons arrive with the energy $T_0$.

{{TABLE:t2_1}}

Anodes are built either as massive (reflection) anodes or as transmission anodes [@bushberg2012]. In a massive anode the target, of a material with a high melting point and low vapour pressure (tungsten for bremsstrahlung tubes; chromium, iron, copper, molybdenum or silver for characteristic radiation), is embedded in a body of oxygen-free copper that removes the heat, and the working beam leaves through the irradiated face; when the target and the body are of the same metal, as in the copper anode studied here, the anode is homogeneous. In a transmission anode a thin target is deposited on a substrate, usually beryllium, that also serves as the exit window, and the beam axis coincides with the electron beam. The photons generated at the mean electron penetration depth $x_1$ are attenuated in the anode over the layer

$$
x_2 = \begin{cases} X - x_1, & \text{transmission anode},\\ x_1\cos\alpha/\cos\beta, & \text{massive anode}, \end{cases}
$$ {#eq:anode_layer}

where $X$ is the thickness of a transmission anode, $\alpha$ the angle between the electron beam and the normal to the target surface and $\beta$ the angle between the normal and the direction of the extracted beam. In the reflection tube of {{REF:fig01_tube}} the electron beam is perpendicular to the central ray, so $\alpha = \theta$ and $\beta = 90° - \theta$, and {{REF:eq:anode_layer}} gives $x_2 = x_1\cos\theta/\sin\theta$, the exit path $d/\sin\theta$ of a photon produced at the depth $d = x_1\cos\theta$ below the surface used in Section 3.2.3.

### 3.1.2. Radiation safety when working with a kilovoltage x-ray tube

A 45 kV copper-anode tube emits very intense low-energy x-rays: the unfiltered air kerma at 10 cm is 383 mGy per mAs, i.e. 15.3 Gy/s at 40 mA, and the tissue surface receives 2 Gy in 0.12 s, so accidental hand exposure causes severe local injuries within seconds. The primary beam, scatter from the sample and holder, and housing leakage must be controlled. Protection follows the principles of justification, optimisation (as low as reasonably achievable, ALARA) and dose limitation of the International Commission on Radiological Protection [@icrp103], implemented in the Basic Safety Standards of the International Atomic Energy Agency [@iaea_gsr3] and, in Uzbekistan, in the Law "On Radiation Safety" [@uz_law_radsafety]. Occupational effective and equivalent doses (skin, extremities, eye lens) must stay below the limits.

The main measures are technical: a shielded housing, an enclosure whose door interlocks switch off the high voltage or close the shutter, warning lights, and fixed holders and collimators that keep hands out of the beam. Staff training, local rules, personal dosimetry and dose-rate surveys, particularly after maintenance, complement them. The high-voltage generator is a further hazard, and the beryllium window must not be touched (beryllium compounds are toxic).

Filtration also serves safety: the filters that markedly raise the DEF (1 mm Al to 0.2 mm Cu) lower the air kerma rate 120–1300-fold; with 1 mm Al the surface tissue kerma rate at 40 mA falls from 16.5 to 0.137 Gy/s and the time for 2 Gy rises from 0.12 to 14.6 s, controllable by a timer or shutter. The removed soft component would be absorbed superficially: the relative primary dose at 10 mm is 0.009 of the surface value without a filter and 0.44 with 1 mm Al. The DEF-maximising filter, whose beam can be specified by its HVL [@ma2001], thus also reduces unnecessary skin dose, in line with ALARA.

## 3.2. Calculation of the x-ray tube spectrum

### 3.2.1. Methods of modelling x-ray tube spectra

Measuring the spectrum needed for the DEF with semiconductor detectors [@birch1979] requires detector-response corrections and is impractical for every configuration, so parametric studies use calculated spectra; four groups of methods are compared in {{REF:tab:methods}}. The empirical model TASMIP (tungsten anode spectral model using interpolating polynomials) interpolates measured tungsten-anode spectra from 30 to 140 kV [@boone1997], and the spektr 3.0 tool implements a similar interpolation model [@punnoose2016]. Such models are tied to the anode material, angle and inherent filtration of the measured tubes: added filtration can be modelled as attenuation, but the anode material and angle, studied in this work, cannot.

Semi-empirical models go back to Kramers' theory, in which the energy emitted from a thick target per unit photon-energy interval is proportional to $Z(T_0 - E)$, where $Z$ is the target atomic number, $T_0$ the initial electron kinetic energy and $E$ the photon energy [@kramers1923; @podgorsak2016]. Birch and Marshall compared their model with Ge(Li) detector measurements [@birch1979], and Tucker, Barnes and Chakraborty proposed the TBC model for tungsten [@tucker1991]. Such models combine a simplified bremsstrahlung cross-section with approximate electron penetration and target self-absorption; they show the dependence on voltage, anode angle and filtration explicitly, but their fitted parameters are target-specific, usually for tungsten.

Physics-based deterministic models replace most empirical parameters with theoretical or MC-derived data. MC-derived electron-penetration characteristics [@poludniowski2007a] were used to calculate x-ray production and filtration in the target [@poludniowski2007b] and implemented for tungsten in SpekCalc [@poludniowski2009]. SpekPy v2, its successor, offers several physics models, including the casim model used here, and several anodes, including copper [@poludniowski2021], available for 10–50 kV in version 2.5.4 [@spekpy_soft]. It builds on models of K and L emission [@omar2018] and of bremsstrahlung energy and angular distributions [@omar2020a], validated from 20 to 300 kV [@omar2020b] and against measured spectra and HVLs [@bujila2020].

MC simulation of coupled electron–photon transport in the tube gives the most complete description, for example MCNP4C for diagnostic and mammography spectra [@ay2004] or EGS4/BEAM for kilovoltage radiotherapy units [@verhaegen1999]; other general-purpose codes are EGSnrc [@kawrakow2000], Geant4 [@agostinelli2003] and PENELOPE [@salvat2019]. It includes electron backscattering, the bremsstrahlung angular distribution and the real tube geometry, but is slow and needs detailed geometric data, so it suits benchmarks rather than extensive parametric studies.

| Semi-empirical | Kramers [@kramers1923]; Birch–Marshall [@birch1979]; TBC [@tucker1991] | Simple; explicit parameter dependence | Simplified physics; fitted parameters, mostly tungsten |

In kilovoltage dosimetry, beam quality is specified by the HVL together with the tube voltage [@hill2014], as in the protocol of the American Association of Physicists in Medicine for 40–300 kV beams (AAPM TG-61) [@ma2001], the TRS-398 code of practice of the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) [@iaea_trs398] and the standard radiation qualities of the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC 61267) [@iec61267]. The HVL is thus the natural quantity for comparing models and measurements. It does not, however, fix the energy-fluence fraction above the Ag K-edge, so beams with similar HVLs can give different DEFs for silver, as Section 3.3.5 shows for filters with absorption edges ({{REF:t3_4}}).

Most of these models were developed for tungsten, the usual target of medical tubes [@bushberg2012]. Copper-anode tubes serve mainly x-ray diffraction, where Cu Kα is the working line, Kβ is suppressed by a nickel filter [@cullity2001] and the bremsstrahlung continuum is unwanted background. For silver dose enhancement, by contrast, the continuum is the useful part, since only bremsstrahlung photons can exceed the Ag K-edge; yet the full spectrum of copper-anode tubes and its dependence on tube parameters have received much less attention. This work therefore constructed a semi-analytical physics-based model of the copper-anode tube, the developed model (DM), combining the Bethe collision stopping power in the continuous slowing-down approximation (CSDA) [@bethe1930; @icru37], the non-relativistic Bethe–Heitler bremsstrahlung cross-section with the Elwert factor [@bethe1934; @elwert1939; @koch1959] and the Gryziński K-shell ionisation cross-section [@gryzinski1965], with one adjustable parameter, the electron depth factor (Section 3.2.2). All dependences on tube parameters remain explicit.

### 3.2.2. Electron slowing-down in the anode

Neglecting radiative losses, the DM uses the continuous-slowing-down approximation (CSDA) with the Bethe mass collision stopping power [@bethe1930] in the form of Report 37 of the International Commission on Radiation Units and Measurements (ICRU) [@icru37]:

$$
\frac{S(T)}{\rho} = \frac{\kappa}{2}\,\frac{Z}{A}\,\frac{1}{\beta^{2}}\left[\ln\frac{\tau^{2}(\tau + 2)}{2\,(I/m_ec^{2})^{2}} + F^{-}(\tau)\right]
$$ {#eq:bethe}

where $S(T)$ is the linear collision stopping power at the electron kinetic energy $T$, $\rho$ the density of the anode, $\kappa = 4\pi N_A r_e^{2} m_ec^{2} = 0.307075$ MeV cm²/mol, $N_A$ the Avogadro constant, $r_e$ the classical electron radius, $m_ec^{2}$ = 510.999 keV the electron rest energy, $Z$ and $A$ the atomic number and molar mass (g/mol) of the anode, $\tau = T/m_ec^{2}$, $\beta$ the electron velocity in units of the speed of light, $\beta^{2} = 1 - (1 + \tau)^{-2}$, $I$ the mean excitation energy, and $F^{-}(\tau)$ the electron term derived from the Møller cross-section:

$$
F^{-}(\tau) = 1 - \beta^{2} + \frac{\tau^{2}/8 - (2\tau + 1)\ln 2}{(\tau + 1)^{2}}
$$ {#eq:fminus}

For copper $I$ = 322 eV; the density-effect correction (negligible below 100 keV) and the shell corrections (relevant only at the lowest energies) are omitted; for water the code reproduces the ESTAR stopping power of the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) [@nist_estar] (Section 3.2.6). The path length travelled from $T_0$ to $T$ is

$$
s(T) = \int_{T}^{T_0} \frac{\mathrm{d}T'}{S(T')}
$$ {#eq:csda}

where $T'$ is the integration variable. Evaluated by the trapezoidal rule (step 0.02 keV) down to $T_{\min}$ = 1 keV, it gives practically the CSDA range, 6.48 µm for 45 keV electrons in copper. A constant logarithm in {{REF:eq:bethe}} and $\beta^{2} \approx 2T/m_ec^{2}$ give $S \propto 1/T$, and {{REF:eq:csda}} then yields $T_0^{2} - T^{2} \propto s$, the empirical Thomson–Whiddington law [@whiddington1912]. Since multiple elastic scattering makes the electrons diffuse, the CSDA path is not the photon production depth; instead of the MC-derived penetration distributions of Poludniowski and Evans [@poludniowski2007a], the DM uses one mean depth for each electron energy, the projected depth

$$
d(T) = \eta\, s(T)\cos\theta
$$ {#eq:depth}

where $d(T)$ is the depth (along the normal) at which the electron has slowed down to $T$, $\eta$ is the electron depth factor ($\eta = 1$ for straight-line motion, $\eta < 1$ for diffusion), and $\theta$ is the angle of incidence. This single adjustable parameter of the DM was fitted to the bremsstrahlung shape of SpekPy [@poludniowski2021] (Section 3.2.6), which gave $\eta$ = 0.40 and a maximum projected depth of 2.54 µm for 45 keV electrons at $\theta$ = 12°. Backscattering (about 30 % of the electrons in copper) is not modelled, contributing to the overestimated absolute output (Section 3.2.7). As panel (a) of {{REF:fig04_electron}} shows, the stopping power rises steeply as the electron slows down, so the depth grows rapidly as the energy falls from 45 to about 20 keV and then saturates. Photons close to $T_0$ are therefore emitted only near the surface, whereas soft photons are produced along the whole path and suffer most from self-absorption.

{{FIG:fig04_electron|Electron slowing-down and K-shell ionisation in the copper anode: (a) Bethe collision stopping power (solid, left axis) and projected depth at which a 45 keV electron has slowed down to T, for η = 0.40 and θ = 12° (dashed, right axis); (b) Gryziński K-shell ionisation cross-section.}}

A widely used empirical alternative to the CSDA range is the Kanaya–Okayama range [@kanaya1972],

$$
R_{\mathrm{KO}} = \frac{0.0276\,A\,E_0^{1.67}}{\rho\,Z^{0.889}}
$$ {#eq:ko}

where $R_{\mathrm{KO}}$ is in micrometres, $E_0$ the electron energy in keV, $A$ the molar mass in g/mol, $\rho$ the density in g/cm³ and $Z$ the atomic number. For 45 keV electrons in copper it gives 5.66 µm, 13 % less than the CSDA path length of 6.48 µm. Both values exceed the maximum projected depth of the DM, 2.54 µm (η = 0.40, θ = 12°), because multiple elastic scattering bends the electron paths, so that the electrons lose their energy closer to the surface than a straight-line estimate suggests.

### 3.2.3. Bremsstrahlung production in a thick target

Kramers' classical theory of the continuous spectrum [@kramers1923] gives the cross-section

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\mathrm{Kr}}}{\mathrm{d}E} = \frac{16\pi}{3\sqrt{3}}\,\frac{\alpha Z^{2} r_e^{2}}{\beta_0^{2}\,E}
$$ {#eq:kramers}

where $\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\mathrm{Kr}}/\mathrm{d}E$ is the cross-section per atom per unit photon energy (cm²/keV) for $E < T$ (zero otherwise), $\alpha$ the fine-structure constant, and $\beta_0$ the velocity of an electron of energy $T$ in units of the speed of light. With $\beta_0^{2} \propto T$ and $S \propto 1/T$, integration over the slowing-down yields Kramers' law (Section 3.2.1), an energy spectrum proportional to $Z(T_0 - E)$. The DM retains it only as an option, using by default the non-relativistic, unscreened Born-approximation cross-section of Bethe and Heitler [@bethe1934] as compiled by Koch and Motz [@koch1959]:

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\mathrm{B}}}{\mathrm{d}E} = \frac{16}{3}\,\frac{\alpha Z^{2} r_e^{2}}{\beta_0^{2}\,E}\,\ln\frac{p_0 + p}{p_0 - p}
$$ {#eq:bhnr}

where $p_0$ and $p$ are the electron momenta before and after the emission, $p_0c = \sqrt{T(T + 2m_ec^{2})}$ and $pc = \sqrt{(T - E)(T - E + 2m_ec^{2})}$. The Born approximation requires $2\pi Z\alpha/\beta_0 \ll 1$ and $2\pi Z\alpha/\beta \ll 1$, $\beta$ being the electron velocity after the emission [@koch1959], whereas for copper $2\pi Z\alpha/\beta_0$ exceeds 3 even at 45 keV. The error is partly corrected by the Elwert factor for the Coulomb distortion of the electron wave functions [@elwert1939]:

$$
f_E = \frac{\beta_0}{\beta}\,\frac{1 - \exp(-2\pi Z\alpha/\beta_0)}{1 - \exp(-2\pi Z\alpha/\beta)}
$$ {#eq:elwert}

The DM uses the product $\mathrm{d}\sigma/\mathrm{d}E = f_E\,\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\mathrm{B}}/\mathrm{d}E$ (BH-E). As $E \to T$, {{REF:eq:bhnr}} vanishes while $f_E$ grows as $1/\beta$, so BH-E remains finite at the high-energy end. Unlike the tabulated cross-sections of Seltzer and Berger [@seltzer1986], BH-E neglects atomic screening and electron–electron bremsstrahlung, contributing to the overestimated absolute yield of hard bremsstrahlung relative to SpekPy. The DEF, however, depends only on the spectral shape, which is reproduced at 45 kV with a root-mean-square deviation of 2.46 % after the fit of $\eta$ (Section 3.2.7).

In a thick target the electron passes $n_a\,\mathrm{d}T/S(T)$ atoms per unit area while losing the energy $\mathrm{d}T$, and a photon produced at the depth $d(T)$ is attenuated along the exit path $d(T)/\sin\theta$. Assuming isotropic emission, the number of bremsstrahlung photons per unit energy interval, per steradian and per incident electron along the central ray is

$$
N_b(E) = \frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{E}^{T_0} \frac{n_a}{S(T)}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma}{\mathrm{d}E}(T, E)\,\exp\left[-\frac{\mu(E)\,d(T)}{\sin\theta}\right]\mathrm{d}T
$$ {#eq:thick}

where $n_a = \rho N_A/A$ is the number of atoms per unit volume of the anode, $\mathrm{d}\sigma/\mathrm{d}E$ the BH-E cross-section, and $\mu(E)$ the linear attenuation coefficient of the anode from the xraylib library [@schoonjans2011]. Without the exponential and the factor $1/4\pi$, it gives the generated spectrum $N_g(E)$, emitted in all directions. The integrals are evaluated by the trapezoidal rule in photon bins of 0.05 keV; a similar formulation with MC-derived penetration data underlies SpekCalc [@poludniowski2007a; @poludniowski2007b]. The self-absorption grows as $\theta$ decreases and is strongest for soft photons and just above the copper K-edge. Multiple scattering smooths the anisotropy of single-interaction bremsstrahlung, but the isotropic approximation, avoided by more detailed models [@omar2020a], remains a limitation of the DM (Section 3.4).

### 3.2.4. Characteristic radiation

An electron with an energy above the K-shell binding energy $E_K$ (8.979 keV for copper) can ionise the K shell; the vacancy is filled by emitting either a characteristic photon, with the probability $\omega_K$ (the fluorescence yield, 0.454 for copper), or an Auger electron [@bambynek1972; @krause1979]. The DM includes the four strongest lines, K$\alpha_1$ (K–L$_3$, 8.048 keV), K$\alpha_2$ (K–L$_2$, 8.028 keV), K$\beta_1$ (K–M$_3$) and K$\beta_3$ (K–M$_2$), the last two at 8.905 keV, with relative intensities $p_j$ (radiative rates normalised over the four lines) of 0.583, 0.299, 0.078 and 0.040; the copper L lines (below 1 keV) are absorbed in the window. K vacancies arise directly from electron impact and indirectly from the absorption of bremsstrahlung photons above $E_K$. The K-shell ionisation cross-section is Gryziński's binary-encounter formula [@gryzinski1965]:

$$
\sigma_K(T) = \frac{\pi e^{4} n_K}{E_K^{2}}\,\frac{1}{U_K}\left(\frac{U_K - 1}{U_K + 1}\right)^{3/2}\left[1 + \frac{2}{3}\left(1 - \frac{1}{2U_K}\right)\ln\left(2.7 + \sqrt{U_K - 1}\right)\right]
$$ {#eq:gryz}

where $U_K = T/E_K$ is the overvoltage ratio ($\sigma_K = 0$ for $U_K \le 1$), $n_K = 2$ the number of K-shell electrons, and $\pi e^{4} = 6.5141\times10^{-20}$ cm² keV² in Gaussian units. For copper the cross-section has a broad maximum of about 351 b near 35 keV (panel (b) of {{REF:fig04_electron}}), and integration of $n_a\sigma_K/S$ over the slowing-down gives 0.0159 K ionisations per incident 45 keV electron. Because the Gryziński formula rises too slowly near the threshold, the DM underestimates the Cu K-line intensity by about 26–43 % relative to SpekPy, whose model of K and L emission [@omar2018] relies on distorted-wave Born calculations [@bote2008], reviewed together with experimental data by Llovet et al. [@llovet2014]; the deficit is largest at low tube voltages (Section 3.2.7). Edge and line energies, fluorescence yields and radiative rates are taken from xraylib [@schoonjans2011] (transition energies of Deslattes et al. [@deslattes2003]) and listed in {{REF:t2_2}} for the anode, window and filter materials and several high-Z radiosensitizers. Filters of Mo, Ag and Sn, with K-edges close to that of Ag, strongly absorb the photons most effective for dose enhancement (Section 3.2.10).

{{TABLE:t2_2}}

The direct yield of line $j$ along the central ray, per steradian and per incident electron, is

$$
N_j^{\mathrm{dir}} = \frac{\omega_K\,p_j}{4\pi}\int_{E_K}^{T_0} \frac{n_a\,\sigma_K(T)}{S(T)}\,\exp\left[-\frac{\mu_j\,d(T)}{\sin\theta}\right]\mathrm{d}T
$$ {#eq:charK}

where $\mu_j = \mu(E_j)$ at the line energy $E_j$ and the other symbols are as in {{REF:eq:thick}}; lying below the copper K-edge, the lines are weakly absorbed in the anode and cannot themselves ionise the K shell. For the indirect fluorescence, since the electrons penetrate only a few micrometres, less than the attenuation lengths above the K-edge, the bremsstrahlung is represented by an isotropic point source on the anode surface (surface-source approximation). Then $u = \cos\vartheta$, $\vartheta$ being the angle between the photon direction and the inward normal, has the probability density 1/2 on $-1 \le u \le 1$, and a photon with $u > 0$ interacts at depths $z$ to $z + \mathrm{d}z$ with the probability $(\mu/u)\exp(-\mu z/u)\,\mathrm{d}z$, where $\mu = \mu(E)$, so the interaction-depth density per emitted photon is

$$
P(z) = \frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1} \frac{\mu}{u}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mu z/u}\,\mathrm{d}u = \frac{\mu}{2}\,\mathrm{E}_1(\mu z)
$$ {#eq:absdepth}

where $t = 1/u$ was substituted and $\mathrm{E}_1(x) = \int_{1}^{\infty} t^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{-xt}\,\mathrm{d}t$ is the exponential integral. A fraction $\tau_K(E)/\mu(E)$ of the interactions are K-shell photoabsorptions, $\tau_K(E)$ being the linear K-shell photoelectric coefficient of copper (from xraylib). A line-$j$ photon, emitted per vacancy with the probability $\omega_K p_j$, is attenuated along the central ray by $\exp(-b_j z)$, where $b_j = \mu_j/\sin\theta$. Per steradian and per generated bremsstrahlung photon of energy $E$, the yield of line $j$ is therefore

$$
Y_j(E) = \frac{\omega_K\,p_j}{4\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty} \frac{\tau_K(E)}{\mu(E)}\,P(z)\,\mathrm{e}^{-b_j z}\,\mathrm{d}z = \frac{\omega_K\,p_j}{4\pi}\,\frac{\tau_K(E)}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty} \mathrm{E}_1(\mu z)\,\mathrm{e}^{-b_j z}\,\mathrm{d}z
$$ {#eq:indyield}

Interchanging the order of integration gives the Laplace transform $\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{E}_1(x)\,\mathrm{e}^{-sx}\,\mathrm{d}x = \int_{1}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t/[t(t + s)] = \ln(1 + s)/s$, with $x = \mu z$ and $s = b_j/\mu$:

$$
\int_{0}^{\infty} \mathrm{E}_1(\mu z)\,\mathrm{e}^{-b_j z}\,\mathrm{d}z = \frac{1}{b_j}\ln\left(1 + \frac{b_j}{\mu}\right)
$$ {#eq:laplace}

Weighting $Y_j(E)$ with $N_g(E)$ and integrating above the K-edge gives the indirect yield per steradian and per incident electron:

$$
N_j^{\mathrm{ind}} = \frac{\omega_K\,p_j}{4\pi}\int_{E_K}^{T_0} N_g(E)\,\frac{\tau_K(E)}{2\,b_j}\,\ln\left[1 + \frac{b_j}{\mu(E)}\right]\mathrm{d}E
$$ {#eq:indirect}

The total yield of line $j$ is $N_j = N_j^{\mathrm{dir}} + N_j^{\mathrm{ind}}$. For the baseline tube the indirect contribution is 4.5 % of the Kα and 4.8 % of the Kβ intensity; it is slightly larger for Kβ because the indirect vacancies lie deeper on average and Kβ photons are attenuated less. The surface-source approximation and the neglect of scattered photons therefore matter little for the line intensities.

### 3.2.5. Filtration, beam-quality parameters and air kerma

Attenuation in the window, added filters and air follows the narrow-beam Beer–Lambert law, which neglects scattered photons:

$$
\mathcal{T}(E) = \exp\left[-\mu_{\mathrm{Be}}(E)\,t_{\mathrm{Be}} - \sum_{i}\mu_i(E)\,t_i - \mu_{\mathrm{air}}(E)\,r\right]
$$ {#eq:filter}

where $\mathcal{T}(E)$ is the transmission, $\mu_{\mathrm{Be}}$, $\mu_i$ and $\mu_{\mathrm{air}}$ are the linear attenuation coefficients of beryllium, the $i$-th added filter and air, $t_{\mathrm{Be}}$ and $t_i$ the thicknesses of the window and the filters, and $r$ the focus–sample distance. For the elements, the xraylib total mass attenuation coefficients, including coherent scattering, are multiplied by the densities in {{REF:t2_2}}; for dry air the mixture rule is applied to the NIST data for C, N, O and Ar [@nist_xraymass] with the density $1.205\times10^{-3}$ g/cm³, and treating the whole distance as air slightly overestimates the attenuation. The spectral photon fluence at the sample is then

$$
\Phi(E) = \frac{n_e\,Q}{r^{2}}\,\mathcal{T}(E)\left[N_b(E) + \sum_{j} N_j\,\delta(E - E_j)\right]
$$ {#eq:fluence}

where $\Phi(E)$ is the number of photons per unit area and energy interval (cm$^{-2}$ keV$^{-1}$), $Q$ the exposure in mAs, $n_e = 6.242\times10^{15}$ the number of electrons per mAs, $N_j$ the total yield of line $j$, and $\delta$ the Dirac delta function. Numerically, each delta function is replaced by $N_j/\Delta E$ in the bin of width $\Delta E$ = 0.05 keV that contains $E_j$. With the spectral energy fluence $\Psi(E) = E\,\Phi(E)$, the tube output is characterised by the air kerma [@attix1986; @icru85]:

$$
K_{\mathrm{air}} = \int_{0}^{T_0} \Phi(E)\,E\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_{\mathrm{air}}\mathrm{d}E
$$ {#eq:kerma}

where $(\mu_{en}/\rho)_{\mathrm{air}}$ is the mass energy-absorption coefficient of dry air, obtained in the same way, and 1 keV/g = $1.602\times10^{-13}$ Gy. Strictly, {{REF:eq:kerma}} gives the collision kerma, which for kilovoltage photons in air practically equals the total kerma [@attix1986]. With ICRU-44 soft tissue [@icru44] instead of air, it gives the tissue kerma at the sample surface without backscatter, used in Section 3.3.5 to estimate exposure times. For the SpekPy spectra, filtered with SpekPy's own attenuation data, the SpekPy air kerma is used, whereas the half-value layers (HVL) and mean energies of all copper-anode spectra are computed identically for both models.

Kilovoltage beam quality is specified primarily by the first HVL, the thickness of a reference absorber (usually aluminium or copper) that halves the narrow-beam air kerma [@ma2001; @iaea_trs398; @iec61267]. In aluminium, HVL1 = $x_1$ follows from

$$
\int_{0}^{T_0} \Phi(E)\,E\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_{\mathrm{air}}\exp\left[-\mu_{\mathrm{Al}}(E)\,x_1\right]\mathrm{d}E = \frac{1}{2}\,K_{\mathrm{air}}
$$ {#eq:hvl}

where $\mu_{\mathrm{Al}}(E)$ is the linear attenuation coefficient of aluminium. The thickness $x_2$ that reduces the air kerma to one quarter gives HVL2 = $x_2 - x_1$ (both found by bisection) and the homogeneity coefficient $h$ = HVL1/HVL2. The effective energy $E_{\mathrm{eff}}$, that of the monoenergetic beam with the same HVL1, is found from $\mu_{\mathrm{Al}}(E_{\mathrm{eff}}) = \ln 2/\mathrm{HVL1}$ by Brent's method, and the mean energy of the photon fluence spectrum is

$$
\bar{E} = \frac{\int_{0}^{T_0} E\,\Phi(E)\,\mathrm{d}E}{\int_{0}^{T_0} \Phi(E)\,\mathrm{d}E}
$$ {#eq:emean}

where both integrals include the characteristic lines. The fraction of photons above the Ag K-edge, $f_{\mathrm{Ag}} = \int_{E_{K,\mathrm{Ag}}}^{T_0}\Phi(E)\,\mathrm{d}E \big/ \int_{0}^{T_0}\Phi(E)\,\mathrm{d}E$ with $E_{K,\mathrm{Ag}}$ = 25.514 keV, and the corresponding energy-fluence fraction describe the part of the spectrum in which silver absorbs most strongly relative to tissue (Sections 2.2 and 1.4). Like the DEF, the HVLs and the mean energy depend only on the spectral shape, not on the absolute output.

### 3.2.6. Data sources, software implementation and validation strategy

All $\mu_{en}/\rho$ values and the $\mu/\rho$ of tissue, water and air are obtained from the NIST elemental tables [@nist_xraymass; @seltzer1993], for compounds by the mixture rule. The module `nist.py` interpolates them log–log between absorption edges, where the coefficients follow approximate power laws. Elemental $\mu/\rho$, edge and line energies, fluorescence yields, radiative rates and the MC cross-sections come from xraylib 4.3.0 [@schoonjans2011], whose $\mu/\rho$ agree with NIST [@nist_xcom] to better than 0.1 %.

The xraylib function `CS_Energy`, however, returns too low $\mu_{en}/\rho$ for light elements at 15–45 keV: for oxygen at 30 keV 0.108 instead of 0.173 cm²/g (−37 %) and for carbon 0.0515 instead of 0.0661 cm²/g, whereas for silver it agrees with NIST (16.58 versus 16.60 cm²/g). For tissue, composed mainly of O, C and H, it would underestimate the denominator of {{REF:eq:def_spec}} and overestimate the DEF: 1.62 instead of 1.48 for 1 % Ag with 1 mm Al. The xraylib cross-sections are unaffected (Section 3.3.2), but `CS_Energy` must be checked against NIST before use for low-Z media.

The reference spectrum model is SpekPy v2.5.4 [@poludniowski2021; @spekpy_soft] ("casim" physics model, copper anodes at 10–50 kV), validated against measured spectra and HVLs [@bujila2020]; its spectra validate the DM and are the principal DEF input. The code uses Python 3.13 with NumPy [@harris2020], SciPy [@virtanen2020] (minimisation and root-finding) and Matplotlib [@hunter2007].

The single adjustable parameter, the electron depth factor $\eta$ in {{REF:eq:depth}}, minimises the root-mean-square deviation between the DM and SpekPy bremsstrahlung spectra at 45 kV, θ = 12° and 0.5 mm Be, normalised to unit area above 3 keV and taken relative to the reference maximum; $\eta$ is searched in 0.25–0.7 and rounded to two decimals, all other configurations being predictions. The validation comprises component checks, e.g. of the stopping power against NIST ESTAR [@nist_estar] (Section 3.2.2); comparison with SpekPy for 25–50 kV and anode angles of 6–30°, with and without 1 mm Al, plus a tungsten-anode check; DEF comparison for DM and SpekPy spectra; MC verification; and a DEF sensitivity analysis.
