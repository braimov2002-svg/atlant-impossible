# CHAPTER 2. PHYSICAL MODEL OF THE X-RAY SPECTRUM AND METHODS OF DOSE-ENHANCEMENT CALCULATION

This chapter describes the semi-analytical spectrum model of this work, the developed model (DM), and the methods for calculating the dose enhancement factor (DEF) and verifying it by Monte Carlo (MC) simulation. Formulas are given as implemented in the code (Appendix A), with energies in keV and lengths in cm unless stated otherwise.

## 2.1. Design and technical parameters of an x-ray tube with a copper anode

Electrons emitted by the heated cathode are focused onto the focal spot of the anode, which they reach with the kinetic energy $T_0 = eU$, where $U$ is the tube voltage and $e$ the elementary charge (45 keV in the baseline); only a small fraction of this energy is emitted as x-rays [@bushberg2012; @podgorsak2016]. The photon number is proportional to the product of tube current and exposure time (mAs), whereas the spectral shape does not depend on the current, so all spectra are normalised to 1 mAs. In the modelled reflection-target tube ({{REF:fig01_tube}}), the beam leaves through the face of the thick anode on which the electrons are incident. The anode angle $\theta$ lies between the anode surface and the central ray, which is perpendicular to the electron beam, so the electrons are incident at $\theta$ to the surface normal and the central-ray photons leave at the take-off angle $\theta$. A small $\theta$ reduces the effective focal spot but makes the beam on the anode side weaker and harder (heel effect) [@bushberg2012]; the DM treats the central ray only. The beryllium window and the self-absorption in the anode form the inherent filtration, and added metal filters remove the soft part of the spectrum and harden the beam [@khan2014] before the photons cross an air gap to the sample at the focus–sample distance $r$ (labelled d in {{REF:fig01_tube}}).

{{FIG:fig01_tube|Schematic diagram of the modelled reflection-target copper-anode tube and of the irradiation geometry: photons emitted along the central ray at the anode angle θ pass the beryllium window, the added filter and the air gap (focus–sample distance d, denoted r in the text) to the Ag-containing tissue sample.}}

Copper-anode tubes are standard sources for x-ray diffraction, which uses the intense Cu Kα line and suppresses the Kβ line with a nickel filter whose K-edge lies between the two lines [@cullity2001]. For irradiations with silver, however, the useful photons lie above the Ag K-edge at 25.514 keV (Section 1.4), in the continuum that is merely a background in diffraction, so the whole spectrum must be modelled and shaped by filtration; as most spectrum models were developed for tungsten anodes (Section 1.5), a dedicated copper-anode model is required. {{REF:t2_1}} lists the tube parameters, their baseline values and the ranges studied in Chapter 3; the voltage, anode, anode angle, window and filters change the spectral shape, whereas the distance acts through the inverse-square law and the air attenuation. A constant potential is assumed, so all electrons arrive with the energy $T_0$.

## 2.2. Electron slowing-down in the anode

Neglecting radiative losses, the DM uses the continuous-slowing-down approximation (CSDA) with the Bethe mass collision stopping power [@bethe1930] in the form of ICRU Report 37 [@icru37]:

$$
\frac{S(T)}{\rho} = \frac{\kappa}{2}\,\frac{Z}{A}\,\frac{1}{\beta^{2}}\left[\ln\frac{\tau^{2}(\tau + 2)}{2\,(I/m_ec^{2})^{2}} + F^{-}(\tau)\right]
$$ {#eq:bethe}

where $S(T)$ is the linear collision stopping power at the electron kinetic energy $T$, $\rho$ the density of the anode, $\kappa = 4\pi N_A r_e^{2} m_ec^{2} = 0.307075$ MeV cm²/mol, $N_A$ the Avogadro constant, $r_e$ the classical electron radius, $m_ec^{2}$ = 510.999 keV the electron rest energy, $Z$ and $A$ the atomic number and molar mass (g/mol) of the anode, $\tau = T/m_ec^{2}$, $\beta$ the electron velocity in units of the speed of light, $\beta^{2} = 1 - (1 + \tau)^{-2}$, $I$ the mean excitation energy, and $F^{-}(\tau)$ the electron term derived from the Møller cross-section:

$$
F^{-}(\tau) = 1 - \beta^{2} + \frac{\tau^{2}/8 - (2\tau + 1)\ln 2}{(\tau + 1)^{2}}
$$ {#eq:fminus}

For copper $I$ = 322 eV; the density-effect correction (negligible below 100 keV) and the shell corrections (relevant only at the lowest energies) are omitted; for water the code reproduces the NIST ESTAR stopping power [@nist_estar] (Section 2.8). The path length travelled while slowing down from $T_0$ to $T$ is

$$
s(T) = \int_{T}^{T_0} \frac{\mathrm{d}T'}{S(T')}
$$ {#eq:csda}

where $T'$ is the variable of integration. Evaluated by the trapezoidal rule (step 0.02 keV) down to $T_{\min}$ = 1 keV, it gives practically the CSDA range, 6.48 µm for 45 keV electrons in copper. A constant logarithm in {{REF:eq:bethe}} and $\beta^{2} \approx 2T/m_ec^{2}$ give $S \propto 1/T$, and {{REF:eq:csda}} then yields $T_0^{2} - T^{2} \propto s$, the empirical Thomson–Whiddington law [@whiddington1912]. Because multiple elastic scattering makes the electrons diffuse into the target, the CSDA path is not the depth at which the photons are produced. Instead of the MC-derived penetration distributions of Poludniowski and Evans [@poludniowski2007a], the DM uses one mean depth for each electron energy, the projected depth

$$
d(T) = \eta\, s(T)\cos\theta
$$ {#eq:depth}

where $d(T)$ is the depth below the anode surface, along the normal, at which the electron has slowed down to $T$, $\eta$ is the electron depth factor ($\eta = 1$ for straight-line motion, $\eta < 1$ for diffusion), and $\theta$ equals the angle of incidence measured from the normal. This single adjustable parameter of the DM was fitted to the bremsstrahlung shape of SpekPy [@poludniowski2021] (Section 2.8), which gave $\eta$ = 0.40 and a maximum projected depth of 2.54 µm for 45 keV electrons at $\theta$ = 12°. Backscattering (about 30 % of the electrons in copper) is not modelled, which contributes to the overestimate of the absolute output (Section 3.1). As panel (a) of {{REF:fig04_electron}} shows, the stopping power rises steeply as the electron slows down, so the depth grows rapidly as the energy falls from 45 to about 20 keV and then saturates. Photons close to $T_0$ are therefore emitted only near the surface, whereas soft photons are produced along the whole path and suffer most from self-absorption.

## 2.3. Bremsstrahlung production in a thick target

Kramers' classical theory of the continuous spectrum [@kramers1923] gives the cross-section

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\mathrm{Kr}}}{\mathrm{d}E} = \frac{16\pi}{3\sqrt{3}}\,\frac{\alpha Z^{2} r_e^{2}}{\beta_0^{2}\,E}
$$ {#eq:kramers}

where $\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\mathrm{Kr}}/\mathrm{d}E$ is the cross-section per atom per unit photon energy (cm²/keV) for $E < T$ (zero otherwise), $\alpha$ the fine-structure constant, and $\beta_0$ the velocity of an electron of energy $T$ in units of the speed of light. With $\beta_0^{2} \propto T$ and $S \propto 1/T$, integration over the slowing-down yields Kramers' law (Section 1.5), an energy spectrum proportional to $Z(T_0 - E)$. The DM keeps this cross-section only as an option and by default uses the non-relativistic, unscreened Born-approximation cross-section of Bethe and Heitler [@bethe1934] as compiled by Koch and Motz [@koch1959]:

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\mathrm{B}}}{\mathrm{d}E} = \frac{16}{3}\,\frac{\alpha Z^{2} r_e^{2}}{\beta_0^{2}\,E}\,\ln\frac{p_0 + p}{p_0 - p}
$$ {#eq:bhnr}

where $p_0$ and $p$ are the electron momenta before and after the emission, $p_0c = \sqrt{T(T + 2m_ec^{2})}$ and $pc = \sqrt{(T - E)(T - E + 2m_ec^{2})}$. The Born approximation requires $2\pi Z\alpha/\beta_0 \ll 1$ and $2\pi Z\alpha/\beta \ll 1$, $\beta$ being the electron velocity after the emission [@koch1959], whereas for copper $2\pi Z\alpha/\beta_0$ exceeds 3 even at 45 keV. The error is partly corrected by the Elwert factor for the Coulomb distortion of the electron wave functions [@elwert1939]:

$$
f_E = \frac{\beta_0}{\beta}\,\frac{1 - \exp(-2\pi Z\alpha/\beta_0)}{1 - \exp(-2\pi Z\alpha/\beta)}
$$ {#eq:elwert}

The DM uses the product $\mathrm{d}\sigma/\mathrm{d}E = f_E\,\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\mathrm{B}}/\mathrm{d}E$ (BH-E). As $E \to T$, {{REF:eq:bhnr}} vanishes while $f_E$ grows as $1/\beta$, so BH-E remains finite at the high-energy end. Unlike the tabulated cross-sections of Seltzer and Berger [@seltzer1986], BH-E neglects atomic screening and electron–electron bremsstrahlung, which contributes to the overestimate of the absolute yield of hard bremsstrahlung relative to SpekPy. The DEF, however, depends only on the spectral shape, which is reproduced at 45 kV with a root-mean-square deviation of 2.46 % after the fit of $\eta$ (Section 3.1).

In a thick target the electron passes $n_a\,\mathrm{d}T/S(T)$ atoms per unit area while losing the energy $\mathrm{d}T$, and a photon produced at the depth $d(T)$ is attenuated along the exit path $d(T)/\sin\theta$. Assuming isotropic emission, the number of bremsstrahlung photons per unit energy interval, per steradian and per incident electron along the central ray is

$$
N_b(E) = \frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{E}^{T_0} \frac{n_a}{S(T)}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma}{\mathrm{d}E}(T, E)\,\exp\left[-\frac{\mu(E)\,d(T)}{\sin\theta}\right]\mathrm{d}T
$$ {#eq:thick}

where $n_a = \rho N_A/A$ is the number of atoms per unit volume of the anode, $\mathrm{d}\sigma/\mathrm{d}E$ the BH-E cross-section, and $\mu(E)$ the linear attenuation coefficient of the anode from the xraylib library [@schoonjans2011]. Without the exponential and the factor $1/4\pi$, the integral gives the generated spectrum $N_g(E)$ (photons emitted in all directions). The integrals are evaluated by the trapezoidal rule in photon bins of 0.05 keV; a similar formulation with MC-derived penetration data underlies SpekCalc [@poludniowski2007a; @poludniowski2007b]. The exponential in {{REF:eq:thick}} describes the self-absorption, which grows as $\theta$ decreases and is strongest for soft photons and just above the copper K-edge. Multiple scattering smooths the anisotropy of single-interaction bremsstrahlung, but the isotropic approximation, avoided by more detailed models [@omar2020a], remains a limitation of the DM (Section 3.11).

## 2.4. Characteristic radiation

An electron with an energy above the K-shell binding energy $E_K$ (8.979 keV for copper) can ionise the K shell; the vacancy is filled with the emission either of a characteristic photon, with the probability $\omega_K$ (the fluorescence yield, 0.454 for copper), or of an Auger electron [@bambynek1972; @krause1979]. The DM includes the four strongest lines, K$\alpha_1$ (K–L$_3$, 8.048 keV), K$\alpha_2$ (K–L$_2$, 8.028 keV), K$\beta_1$ (K–M$_3$) and K$\beta_3$ (K–M$_2$), the last two at 8.905 keV, with relative intensities $p_j$ (radiative rates normalised over the four lines) of 0.583, 0.299, 0.078 and 0.040; the copper L lines (below 1 keV) are absorbed in the window. K vacancies arise directly from electron impact and indirectly from the absorption of bremsstrahlung photons above $E_K$. The K-shell ionisation cross-section is given by the binary-encounter formula of Gryziński [@gryzinski1965]:

$$
\sigma_K(T) = \frac{\pi e^{4} n_K}{E_K^{2}}\,\frac{1}{U_K}\left(\frac{U_K - 1}{U_K + 1}\right)^{3/2}\left[1 + \frac{2}{3}\left(1 - \frac{1}{2U_K}\right)\ln\left(2.7 + \sqrt{U_K - 1}\right)\right]
$$ {#eq:gryz}

where $U_K = T/E_K$ is the overvoltage ratio ($\sigma_K = 0$ for $U_K \le 1$), $n_K = 2$ the number of K-shell electrons, and $\pi e^{4} = 6.5141\times10^{-20}$ cm² keV² in Gaussian units. For copper the cross-section has a broad maximum of about 351 b near 35 keV (panel (b) of {{REF:fig04_electron}}), and integration of $n_a\sigma_K/S$ over the slowing-down gives 0.0159 K ionisations per incident 45 keV electron. Because the Gryziński formula rises too slowly near the threshold, the DM underestimates the Cu K-line intensity by about 25–43 % relative to SpekPy, whose model of K and L emission [@omar2018] relies on distorted-wave Born calculations [@bote2008], reviewed together with experimental data by Llovet et al. [@llovet2014]; the deficit is largest at low tube voltages (Section 3.1). Edge and line energies, fluorescence yields and radiative rates are taken from xraylib [@schoonjans2011] (yields compiled by Krause [@krause1979], transition energies by Deslattes et al. [@deslattes2003]) and are listed in {{REF:t2_2}} for the anode, window, filter materials and several high-Z radiosensitizers. Filters of Mo, Ag and Sn, with K-edges close to the Ag K-edge, strongly absorb the photons most effective for dose enhancement by silver (Section 3.4).

The direct yield of line $j$ along the central ray, per steradian and per incident electron, is

$$
N_j^{\mathrm{dir}} = \frac{\omega_K\,p_j}{4\pi}\int_{E_K}^{T_0} \frac{n_a\,\sigma_K(T)}{S(T)}\,\exp\left[-\frac{\mu_j\,d(T)}{\sin\theta}\right]\mathrm{d}T
$$ {#eq:charK}

where $\mu_j = \mu(E_j)$ is the linear attenuation coefficient of the anode at the line energy $E_j$, and the other symbols are as in {{REF:eq:thick}}; lying below the copper K-edge, the lines are weakly absorbed in the anode and cannot themselves ionise the K shell. Indirect fluorescence follows the K-shell photoabsorption of bremsstrahlung photons with $E > E_K$. Since the electrons penetrate only a few micrometres, less than the attenuation lengths above the K-edge, the bremsstrahlung is represented by an isotropic point source on the anode surface (surface-source approximation). Then $u = \cos\vartheta$, where $\vartheta$ is the angle between the photon direction and the inward normal, has the probability density 1/2 on $-1 \le u \le 1$, and a photon with $u > 0$ interacts between the depths $z$ and $z + \mathrm{d}z$ with the probability $(\mu/u)\exp(-\mu z/u)\,\mathrm{d}z$, where $\mu = \mu(E)$; the probability density of the interaction depth per emitted photon is therefore

$$
P(z) = \frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1} \frac{\mu}{u}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mu z/u}\,\mathrm{d}u = \frac{\mu}{2}\,\mathrm{E}_1(\mu z)
$$ {#eq:absdepth}

where the substitution $t = 1/u$ has been made and $\mathrm{E}_1(x) = \int_{1}^{\infty} t^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{-xt}\,\mathrm{d}t$ is the exponential integral. A fraction $\tau_K(E)/\mu(E)$ of the interactions are K-shell photoabsorptions, where $\tau_K(E)$ is the linear K-shell photoelectric coefficient of copper (from xraylib) and $\mu(E)$ the total linear attenuation coefficient. Each vacancy yields a photon of line $j$ with the probability $\omega_K p_j$, attenuated on its way out along the central ray by $\exp(-b_j z)$, where $b_j = \mu_j/\sin\theta$. Per steradian and per generated bremsstrahlung photon of energy $E$, the yield of line $j$ is therefore

$$
Y_j(E) = \frac{\omega_K\,p_j}{4\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty} \frac{\tau_K(E)}{\mu(E)}\,P(z)\,\mathrm{e}^{-b_j z}\,\mathrm{d}z = \frac{\omega_K\,p_j}{4\pi}\,\frac{\tau_K(E)}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty} \mathrm{E}_1(\mu z)\,\mathrm{e}^{-b_j z}\,\mathrm{d}z
$$ {#eq:indyield}

The remaining Laplace transform of the exponential integral follows by interchanging the order of integration, $\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{E}_1(x)\,\mathrm{e}^{-sx}\,\mathrm{d}x = \int_{1}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t/[t(t + s)] = \ln(1 + s)/s$, with $x = \mu z$ and $s = b_j/\mu$:

$$
\int_{0}^{\infty} \mathrm{E}_1(\mu z)\,\mathrm{e}^{-b_j z}\,\mathrm{d}z = \frac{1}{b_j}\ln\left(1 + \frac{b_j}{\mu}\right)
$$ {#eq:laplace}

Weighting $Y_j(E)$ with $N_g(E)$ and integrating above the K-edge gives the indirect yield per steradian and per incident electron:

$$
N_j^{\mathrm{ind}} = \frac{\omega_K\,p_j}{4\pi}\int_{E_K}^{T_0} N_g(E)\,\frac{\tau_K(E)}{2\,b_j}\,\ln\left[1 + \frac{b_j}{\mu(E)}\right]\mathrm{d}E
$$ {#eq:indirect}

The total yield of line $j$ is $N_j = N_j^{\mathrm{dir}} + N_j^{\mathrm{ind}}$. For the baseline tube the indirect contribution is 4.5 % of the Kα and 4.8 % of the Kβ intensity; it is slightly larger for Kβ because the indirect vacancies lie deeper on average and Kβ photons are attenuated less. Because this contribution is small, the surface-source approximation and the neglect of scattered photons matter little for the line intensities.

## 2.5. Filtration, beam-quality parameters and air kerma

The attenuation in the window, the added filters and the air follows the Beer–Lambert law for narrow-beam geometry (scattered photons do not reach the point of interest):

$$
\mathcal{T}(E) = \exp\left[-\mu_{\mathrm{Be}}(E)\,t_{\mathrm{Be}} - \sum_{i}\mu_i(E)\,t_i - \mu_{\mathrm{air}}(E)\,r\right]
$$ {#eq:filter}

where $\mathcal{T}(E)$ is the transmission, $\mu_{\mathrm{Be}}$, $\mu_i$ and $\mu_{\mathrm{air}}$ are the linear attenuation coefficients of beryllium, of the $i$-th added filter and of air, $t_{\mathrm{Be}}$ and $t_i$ the thicknesses of the window and the filters, and $r$ the focus–sample distance. For the elements, the xraylib total mass attenuation coefficients, including coherent scattering, are multiplied by the densities in {{REF:t2_2}}; for dry air the mixture rule is applied to the NIST data for C, N, O and Ar [@nist_xraymass] with the density $1.205\times10^{-3}$ g/cm³, and treating the whole distance as air slightly overestimates the attenuation. The spectral photon fluence at the sample is then

$$
\Phi(E) = \frac{n_e\,Q}{r^{2}}\,\mathcal{T}(E)\left[N_b(E) + \sum_{j} N_j\,\delta(E - E_j)\right]
$$ {#eq:fluence}

where $\Phi(E)$ is the number of photons per unit area and per unit energy interval (cm$^{-2}$ keV$^{-1}$), $Q$ the exposure in mAs, $n_e = 6.242\times10^{15}$ the number of electrons per mAs, $N_j$ the total yield of line $j$, and $\delta$ the Dirac delta function. Numerically, each delta function is replaced by $N_j/\Delta E$ in the bin of width $\Delta E$ = 0.05 keV that contains $E_j$. With the spectral energy fluence $\Psi(E) = E\,\Phi(E)$, the output of the tube is characterised by the air kerma [@attix1986; @icru85]:

$$
K_{\mathrm{air}} = \int_{0}^{T_0} \Phi(E)\,E\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_{\mathrm{air}}\mathrm{d}E
$$ {#eq:kerma}

where $(\mu_{en}/\rho)_{\mathrm{air}}$ is the mass energy-absorption coefficient of dry air, obtained in the same way, and 1 keV/g = $1.602\times10^{-13}$ Gy. Strictly, {{REF:eq:kerma}} gives the collision kerma, which for kilovoltage photons in air practically equals the total kerma [@attix1986]. With ICRU-44 soft tissue [@icru44] instead of air, it gives the tissue kerma at the sample surface without backscatter, used in Section 3.6 to estimate exposure times. For the SpekPy spectra, which SpekPy filters with its own attenuation data, the SpekPy air kerma is used, whereas the half-value layers (HVL) and mean energies of all copper-anode spectra are evaluated with the same routines for both models.

Kilovoltage beam quality is specified primarily by the first HVL, the thickness of a reference absorber (usually aluminium or copper) that halves the narrow-beam air kerma [@ma2001; @iaea_trs398; @iec61267; @khan2014]. In aluminium, HVL1 = $x_1$ follows from

$$
\int_{0}^{T_0} \Phi(E)\,E\left(\frac{\mu_{en}}{\rho}\right)_{\mathrm{air}}\exp\left[-\mu_{\mathrm{Al}}(E)\,x_1\right]\mathrm{d}E = \frac{1}{2}\,K_{\mathrm{air}}
$$ {#eq:hvl}

where $\mu_{\mathrm{Al}}(E)$ is the linear attenuation coefficient of aluminium. The thickness $x_2$ that reduces the air kerma to one quarter gives HVL2 = $x_2 - x_1$ (both found by bisection) and the homogeneity coefficient $h$ = HVL1/HVL2. The effective energy $E_{\mathrm{eff}}$, that of the monoenergetic beam with the same HVL1, is found from $\mu_{\mathrm{Al}}(E_{\mathrm{eff}}) = \ln 2/\mathrm{HVL1}$ by Brent's method, and the mean energy of the photon fluence spectrum is

$$
\bar{E} = \frac{\int_{0}^{T_0} E\,\Phi(E)\,\mathrm{d}E}{\int_{0}^{T_0} \Phi(E)\,\mathrm{d}E}
$$ {#eq:emean}

where both integrals include the characteristic lines. The fraction of photons above the Ag K-edge, $f_{\mathrm{Ag}} = \int_{E_{K,\mathrm{Ag}}}^{T_0}\Phi(E)\,\mathrm{d}E \big/ \int_{0}^{T_0}\Phi(E)\,\mathrm{d}E$ with $E_{K,\mathrm{Ag}}$ = 25.514 keV, and the corresponding energy-fluence fraction describe the part of the spectrum in which silver absorbs most strongly relative to tissue (Sections 1.2 and 1.3). Like the DEF, the HVLs and the mean energy depend only on the shape of the spectrum and are insensitive to errors in the absolute output.
